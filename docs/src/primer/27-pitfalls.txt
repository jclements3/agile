== Pitfalls and anti-patterns {#pitfalls}

::: bridge
You know: lessons-learned reports that nobody reads.

Here it is called: pitfalls and anti-patterns.

What is new: each pitfall has a symptom you can spot early in your own project.
:::

::: survival Knowing the ditches
What you have: a working process and, by now, some habits, good and bad.

What you need: a way to recognize the bad habits early, from the first symptom you would actually notice, before
they cost a sprint. Every team moving from documents and Cameo to a text model in GitLab falls into some of these.
Tidewater fell into more than half of them.
:::

=== How to read this chapter {#pitfalls-reading}

Each pitfall has the same four parts:

- **Symptom**: what you will see first. Usually it is small: a number that stops moving, a conversation that keeps
  recurring, a pipeline colour nobody comments on any more.
- **Why it happens**: the reason a sensible team ends up there. None of these pitfalls is stupidity. Most of them are
  an old habit, correct in the document era, applied in a place where it no longer fits.
- **Fix**: what to do, as concretely as possible.
- **At Tidewater**: where the team met it, and what happened.

The pitfalls are grouped by where they bite: the model, the change process, the pipeline, the measures, and the
people. A checklist version, for a monthly look in the mirror, closes the chapter.

=== Pitfalls in the model {#pitfalls-model}

==== Porting the spider web {#pitfall-spider-web}

**Symptom.** The v2 model has the same duplicated blocks, stale packages and dependency cycles as the v1 model.
Someone finds `Battery_old` in the part tree and asks whether it is real.

**Why it happens.** The converter is faithful: it carries what is in the XMI, clutter included, because it cannot know
which blocks are clutter. A team under schedule pressure measures progress by TODO lines closed and treats
everything the converter did not flag as done.

**Fix.** Treat untangling as its own work, with its own stories, measured by scorecard measure 7 (cycles, duplicates,
the most-connected block). Do not delete clutter silently: raise a discrepancy report so the prime removes it upstream,
and remove it in the government model in an MR that cites the report. Until the prime's drop removes it, the next
vendor import will bring it back as a conflict, which is the reminder you want.

**At Tidewater.** The drop-1 conversion carried the `Legacy Import` package with its copied blocks, and the two
package cycles from [[cameo]]. Bob's first instinct was to delete the package in one commit. Jim stopped it: the
deletion would conflict with every future drop until the prime removed it too. The team raised discrepancy reports,
removed the clutter in the government model with the report numbers in the MR, and watched the prime's later drops
remove it upstream (the `Copy of SensorSuite` block went in drop 3, the whole `Legacy Import` package in drop 6).

==== Reformatting the converter's output {#pitfall-reformat}

**Symptom.** Every prime drop produces dozens of merge conflicts, in files where nobody changed anything that matters.

**Why it happens.** Converter output is not how a person would lay the text out, and a tidy engineer reformats it:
reorders members alphabetically, re-indents, splits long lines, moves the `doc` comment above the tag. Each change is
harmless alone. Together they mean that the next vendor import, which writes the converter's layout again, differs from
the government file on nearly every line, and git's three-way merge can no longer tell the prime's changes from the
government's.

**Fix.** Edit converter output *in place* and *minimally*: replace a `// TODO` line with the line that closes it, add
a line where one is needed, and leave every other line exactly as the converter wrote it. Put government-only content
(new packages, the conventions library, views) in separate files the converter never writes (on Tidewater, `model/library/conventions/` and its neighbours), which the vendor branch never
touches. Write the rule into the Definition of Done.

**At Tidewater.** Tom reformatted `Interfaces.sysml` in sprint 2 to make it "readable" and the change sailed through
review because the diff looked like pure layout. Drop 2's import then conflicted on forty lines of that file. Resolving
them took Tom most of a day, after which the team wrote the in-place rule into the Definition of Done and reverted the
reformatting.

==== Hand-editing the vendor branch {#pitfall-vendor-edit}

**Symptom.** A delta report shows changes the prime says it never made, or a vendor import does not reproduce when run
again from the same drop.

**Why it happens.** The vendor branch looks like any other branch, and a quick fix there (a converter glitch, a typo in
the prime's requirement text) seems faster than a discrepancy report.

**Fix.** The vendor branch holds exactly what the converters produce from the drop, nothing else. Protect it so only
maintainers can push and nobody can merge into it. Fixes go on `develop` (government edits) or to the prime (a
discrepancy report). If a converter is wrong, fix the converter, re-import, and record the converter version in the
import commit.

**At Tidewater.** Never happened, because `vendor/prime` was protected from week zero with merge set to no one. Tom
tested the protection in sprint 2 by trying to push a one-line change to it and kept the rejection message as evidence.

==== Changing requirement text in the model {#pitfall-req-text}

**Symptom.** The trace job's drift count goes up after a government MR, not after a drop.

**Why it happens.** An engineer sees a badly worded requirement in the model, knows what it should say, and fixes the
`doc` text. It feels like the obvious improvement.

**Fix.** DOORS is the authority for requirement text, and the prime owns DOORS. The model's requirement text is a copy.
A wording problem is a discrepancy report to the prime; the fix arrives in a later drop, through both the ReqIF and the
XMI. The government model may add notes or analysis around a requirement, never different words in it.

**At Tidewater.** TW-SYS-006 drifted from the start ([[sysmlv2]]). Bob wanted to align the model text by hand in sprint
3. Jim raised a discrepancy report instead, and drop 2 fixed both sides. The drift count fell to zero through the
process rather than around it, which is what the scorecard needed to show.

==== Trusting the TODO count alone {#pitfall-todo-only}

**Symptom.** The TODO count is zero, the pipeline is green, and a reviewer finds that the government's hand-written
state machine is missing a state the prime added months ago.

**Why it happens.** The converter writes a TODO line for each construct it does not carry. When the prime changes
something *inside* such a construct (a new state inside a state machine, a new message in an interaction), the TODO line
in the converted output is unchanged, so the vendor import shows no difference at all. The TODO count cannot see it.

**Fix.** Run the v1 delta on every drop: `xmi2sysml.pl --delta OLD.xmi NEW.xmi`. It compares the two drops element by
element by `xmi:id` and marks every change the converter does not carry as *manual*. Each manual line becomes a task.

**At Tidewater.** Drop 3 added a `Storm` state to `BuoyModes`. The converted v2 output did not change at all. The delta
listed five manual changes inside the state machine, and Bob's drop-3 story carried a task to add the state by hand
([[maintenance]]).

=== Pitfalls in the change process {#pitfalls-change}

==== The big-bang merge request {#pitfall-big-mr}

**Symptom.** An MR has thirty files and two thousand changed lines, it stays open for a week, and the review is a
single comment saying "looks fine".

**Why it happens.** The document era trained people to batch: a document revision collected months of changes because
each revision cost a CCB meeting. The instinct carries over: finish everything, then ask for review.

**Fix.** One task, one MR; one task, less than a week. If an MR touches more than one package or takes more than
fifteen minutes to review, split it. Small MRs are reviewed properly, merge without conflicts, and keep lead time
(scorecard measure 2) low.

**At Tidewater.** Bob's first structure MR in sprint 3 closed every TODO in two packages at once. Jim's review took two
sessions and missed a wrong level tag, which a later MR had to fix. From sprint 4 on the team cut stories into
package-sized tasks, and median review turnaround fell below a working day.

==== Long-lived branches {#pitfall-long-branch}

**Symptom.** A feature branch is two weeks old, and merging `develop` into it produces conflicts in files the branch's
author never touched.

**Why it happens.** The engineer is "nearly done" for days, and keeping the branch private feels safer than showing an
unfinished change.

**Fix.** Branches live days, not weeks. Merge `develop` into your branch daily if it must live longer, and push it so
the MR pipeline checks the integration. If a task will take longer than a week, it is two tasks.

**At Tidewater.** The only branch older than a week was the behavior work in sprint 6, which straddled Bob's leave.
Its merge needed a manual conflict resolution in `Behavior.sysml`. The retrospective added a rule: before leave, merge
or hand over.

==== Rewriting shared history {#pitfall-rewrite}

**Symptom.** A colleague's `git pull` fails with "diverged" messages, or a merge request suddenly shows commits that
were already merged.

**Why it happens.** Tutorials teach `git rebase` and `git push --force` as tidy ways to clean up a branch, and they are,
on a branch only you use. On a shared branch they rewrite history other people have.

**Fix.** Protected branches forbid force push; keep it that way. On your own feature branch, tidy before you push for
the first time, not after. The project uses merge commits and no squash, because the merge history is part of the
record (who merged what, when, after which review) and feeds the status metrics.

**At Tidewater.** Tom followed a tutorial's advice to squash commits in sprint 2, on a test project. When the team saw
that squashing would make the merge history unable to show which engineer made which change, they set squash to
*never* in `project.conf` and wrote the reason beside it.

==== Silent conflict resolution {#pitfall-take-ours}

**Symptom.** After a vendor merge, a change the prime announced in its manifest is not in the government model.

**Why it happens.** Conflicts are uncomfortable, and git offers one-word ways out: take our side, or take theirs, for a
whole file. Taking ours drops the prime's change; taking theirs drops the government's edits.

**Fix.** Resolve conflicts hunk by hunk in Vim ([[vim]]), or take the vendor side and re-apply the government's edits
deliberately, which is the method the Drop Intake Runbook prescribes. Either way, the MR description lists each
conflicted file and how it was resolved, and the reviewer checks the manifest's changes against the merged result.

**At Tidewater.** Drop 5 changed the `EnergyBalance` constraint expression in a TODO line the government had already
replaced by hand. The conflict was real: both sides changed the same line. Tom took the vendor side and re-applied the
hand-written constraint with the new expression, and listed the file in the MR. `gitlab.pl flow take` exists for the
wholesale case, but the runbook uses it only on files the government never edits.

==== Old reviews in new clothes {#pitfall-old-review}

**Symptom.** Every merge request is approved, and then a monthly meeting reviews the same changes again from slides.

**Why it happens.** The organization does not yet trust the MR approval as the review of record, so it keeps the old
board as a safety net. Nobody says out loud that the MR review is now redundant or that the board is.

**Fix.** Decide which review is the review of record, write it into the configuration management plan, and make the
other one a summary. For Tidewater, the MR approval by a code owner is the change approval; the CCB meets monthly to
look at the *outcomes* (baselines, discrepancy reports, metrics), not at individual changes.

**At Tidewater.** The deputy program manager, who chairs the CCB, asked in sprint 4 for "the list of model changes
for the board". Jim answered with the merged MR list from `gitlab.pl export` and offered to replace the change-by-change
review with a fifteen-minute review of the export. The board agreed after seeing two months of it.

=== Pitfalls in the pipeline {#pitfalls-pipeline}

==== Red is normal {#pitfall-red-normal}

**Symptom.** The pipeline on `develop` has been red for three days, and the stand-up no longer mentions it.

**Why it happens.** A failing check that blocks nothing becomes background. Usually it started with a job that failed
for a reason outside the team's control (a runner problem, a server outage), and when the reason went away nobody
re-ran it.

**Fix.** A red pipeline on `develop` or `main` is the team's first priority, above any story, until it is green. The
stand-up asks one question first: "is develop green?" Scorecard measure 8 measures the time from a failure to the
next pass so that the habit is visible.

**At Tidewater.** The runner's disk filled in sprint 6 and every job failed. The fix took Tom an hour once noticed, but
it was noticed only the next morning. The team added "is develop green?" as the first line of the stand-up and a
disk-space check to the weekly runner look ([[admin]]).

==== Allowed to fail, forever {#pitfall-allow-failure}

**Symptom.** Half the jobs are yellow (allowed to fail) and nobody can remember why.

**Why it happens.** `allow_failure: true` is the easy way to add a check that does not pass yet. It is meant to be
temporary and almost never is.

**Fix.** Every `allow_failure` has a comment in `.gitlab-ci.yml` saying why and until when, and an issue to remove it.
Tidewater's `trace` job is allowed to fail by design (the planted requirement gaps are the prime's to fix, and the
discrepancy reports track them); that reason is written beside the job.

**At Tidewater.** The `outline` check started as allowed to fail during the port because the converter's layout broke
the outline rule. It had an issue, a waiver in the wiki, and a removal date tied to the end of the structure epic. When
the date came the waiver was removed in an MR, and the job went back to blocking.

==== Bypassing the gate {#pitfall-bypass}

**Symptom.** A protected branch was unprotected "for five minutes" to push a fix directly, and the audit shows it.

**Why it happens.** Urgency, and a maintainer's role that permits it. In the document era, the equivalent was walking
a change past the CCB because the review was next month.

**Fix.** The gate has no bypass that leaves no trace. Urgent fixes go through a hotfix branch and a merge request like
everything else; a small MR with a green pipeline merges in minutes. The monthly protection audit ([[admin]]) checks
that nothing was unprotected, and the audit events (Premium) show who did it.

**At Tidewater.** Nobody unprotected a branch, which Jim attributes to the sprint 1 exercise: the team deliberately
opened an MR with a lint error and watched the merge button refuse ([[sprint-1]]). Seeing the gate work made it
feel like protection rather than obstruction.

==== Unpinned tools {#pitfall-unpinned}

**Symptom.** The pipeline fails on `develop` with no model change, and the failure is in a check's behavior.

**Why it happens.** The runner uses whatever version of the kit or the converters is newest, and someone updated it.

**Fix.** The runner and the laptops use the same pinned version of every tool. Upgrading a tool is a story with its
own MR and its own pipeline run ([[admin-upgrades]]).

**At Tidewater.** The runner and laptops ran the same tagged kit from week zero, after Tom saw what a moving dependency
did in the vendor import rehearsal: re-running the drop-1 import with a newer converter produced a different
`Stations.sysml`, which would have shown up as a phantom prime change.

==== Tools before habits {#pitfall-tools-first}

**Symptom.** In week two the project has a board with nine columns, forty labels, epics, iterations, value stream
analytics and a security dashboard, and the team still cannot say what the next task is.

**Why it happens.** GitLab has a feature for everything, and the enthusiastic member of the team turns them all on.

**Fix.** Adopt one tool when it pays for itself, and say what it pays for. Tidewater's adoption timeline ([[tools]])
added one or two features per sprint, each when a problem made it worth the cost of learning it.

**At Tidewater.** Tom proposed a full security scanning suite in week zero. Jim asked what it would scan: the model
had no code, so SAST and dependency scanning had nothing to look at. Secret detection, which did have something to
look at, went in at sprint 3.

=== Pitfalls in the measures {#pitfalls-measures}

==== Measuring by hand {#pitfall-hand-measure}

**Symptom.** The status chart is assembled on Thursday night from three spreadsheets, and the numbers on it cannot be
reproduced the following week.

**Why it happens.** It is how status was always done, and the automated numbers are not yet trusted.

**Fix.** Every number in a status report comes from the repository, GitLab's records or the journal, produced by a
script or the pipeline. If a number cannot be produced that way, it is not on the scorecard. The rule was set in
[[directive]] and it is the one that made the sprint 6 briefing believable.

**At Tidewater.** In sprint 2 Jim was tempted to report "percent of model ported" from a judgment. The TODO count was
not yet automated, so Jim reported nothing for that measure and said why. The `todo` job came in that sprint.

==== Gaming the TODO count {#pitfall-gaming}

**Symptom.** The TODO count falls faster than the work could possibly have been done, and some MRs delete TODO lines
without replacing them.

**Why it happens.** Any measure that becomes a target invites shortcuts. Deleting a TODO line is a one-keystroke
"closure".

**Fix.** The Definition of Done says a TODO line is closed only by the construct that replaces it, and the reviewer
checks the replacement. The v1 delta and the trace job catch missing content later, but review catches it first.
Report the TODO count together with measures that would move if it were gamed (trace completeness, lint warnings).

**At Tidewater.** It did not happen, partly because Bob, the strongest skeptic, was also the one closing most TODO
lines and saw the count as proof of work rather than a target set by others.

==== Reporting only the good numbers {#pitfall-good-numbers}

**Symptom.** The sprint report shows four measures; last sprint it showed six.

**Why it happens.** Two measures looked bad, and leaving them out felt kinder than explaining them.

**Fix.** Report every agreed measure, every sprint. A bad number with a reason and a plan is credible; a missing number
is noticed and remembered.

**At Tidewater.** Sprint 3 was bad: the pipeline caught Bob's grammar error, the ISSM blocked the security story for
three days, and morale dipped ([[sprint-3]]). Jim reported all of it. At the sprint 6 briefing the program manager
said that the sprint 3 report was the reason the sprint 6 numbers were believed.

=== Pitfalls with people {#pitfalls-people}

==== The hero {#pitfall-hero}

**Symptom.** Only one person can fix the pipeline, run a vendor import or cut a baseline, and that person's leave is
planned around.

**Why it happens.** The person who set it up knows it best, and it is faster for them to do it than to teach it.

**Fix.** Rotate the chores. Every procedure has a wiki runbook, and every runbook has been followed at least once by
someone other than its author. Pair on the first instance of anything.

**At Tidewater.** Tom built the pipeline and could have stayed its only mechanic. From sprint 4 on, Bob did one
pipeline change per sprint with Tom reviewing, and Jim ran the drop-2 vendor import with Tom watching. When Tom was on
leave in sprint 9 nothing stopped.

==== Forcing it on the skeptic {#pitfall-force}

**Symptom.** The skeptic complies, silently, does the minimum, and the old way continues in a spreadsheet on the side.

**Why it happens.** Management directed the change, so arguing feels pointless; the skeptic's objections are treated
as resistance instead of requirements.

**Fix.** Write the objections down and answer each one with evidence, as [[directive]] did and [[minds]] revisits.
Give the skeptic the measure they would believe and let them run it. Never ask anyone to pretend to be enthusiastic.

**At Tidewater.** Bob's objections were the most specific, and so the most useful. Bob's question "how will I know the
model is right?" became the lint and trace jobs. Bob's quote from sprint 1 ("I found the drift in two minutes, it used
to take a week") came from running one of them.

==== Stand-up as a status meeting {#pitfall-standup}

**Symptom.** The stand-up takes forty minutes, each person reports to Jim, and problems are solved in the meeting.

**Why it happens.** The team's last daily meeting format was a status meeting with a manager, and that is what a daily
meeting feels like.

**Fix.** Terse statuses: yesterday, today, blockers, with story ids, posted in the meeting chat and checked by the
kit's lint ([[ceremonies]]). Problems get named in the stand-up and solved after it, by the people involved.

**At Tidewater.** The first stand-up ran twenty-five minutes. By sprint 3, statuses were one line each, the lint found
nothing to complain about, and the meeting took ten minutes.

==== Tasks too big to finish {#pitfall-big-tasks}

**Symptom.** Stories carry over sprint after sprint, and tasks sit in the "in progress" column for more than a week.

**Why it happens.** Estimates in the document era were in weeks or months, and a five-point ceiling feels artificial.

**Fix.** Enforce the sizes: a task is at most five points (five ideal days) and is cut further if it might not finish
in a week; a story fits in a sprint. A carried-over story is discussed at the retrospective, not just moved.

**At Tidewater.** The Stations story in sprint 4 carried two points into sprint 5 ([[sprint-4]]). The retrospective
found that its level-tag task was really two tasks. Carryovers were rare after that.

=== A monthly look in the mirror {#pitfalls-checklist}

::: checklist Pitfall check, once a month at the retrospective
[ ] The v2 model has no duplicate blocks, no package cycles, and no clutter without a discrepancy report
[ ] Converter output is edited in place only; government-only content is in files the converter never writes (`model/library/conventions/` and the like)
[ ] Nobody pushed to `vendor/prime` except imports
[ ] Requirement text changes came only from drops
[ ] Every drop had a v1 delta, and its manual items became tasks
[ ] No MR this month was larger than a task; no branch lived longer than a week
[ ] No force push, no squash, no unprotected branch
[ ] Every conflicted file in a vendor merge is listed in its MR with how it was resolved
[ ] `develop` was never red for more than a working day
[ ] Every `allow_failure` has a reason and an issue
[ ] Tools are pinned; upgrades were stories
[ ] Every reported number came from a script; every agreed measure was reported
[ ] Each runbook was followed by someone other than its author at least once
[ ] Stand-ups took fifteen minutes or less
[ ] No story carried over twice
:::

=== Applying this to your project {#pitfalls-applying}

- **Copy** the monthly checklist into your retrospective template. It costs five minutes.
- **Adapt** the pitfalls to your sources. If your v1 model has no prime contractor and no vendor branch, the vendor
  pitfalls become pitfalls about your own Cameo exports, but reformatting and in-place editing matter just as much.
- **Decide locally** which pitfalls your organization is most prone to. A team with a strong CCB culture will fall
  into old reviews in new clothes; a team with an enthusiastic tool-builder will fall into tools before habits.
  Watch for your own two.

=== Exercises {#pitfalls-exercises}

::: exercise {#ex-pitfall-diagnose} Diagnose the symptom
For each symptom, name the pitfall and the first thing you would do: (a) a vendor merge conflicts on 60 lines of a
file the government only added level tags to; (b) the drift count rose after a government MR; (c) the TODO count is
zero but the prime's manifest mentions a new state; (d) `develop` has been red since Tuesday.
:::

::: exercise {#ex-pitfall-yours} Your two pitfalls
Pick the two pitfalls your own organization is most likely to fall into. For each, write the symptom you would expect
to see first in your environment, and one rule that would prevent it.
:::

::: exercise {#ex-pitfall-delta} Prove the TODO count blind spot
Generate drops 2 and 3 with `perl sim/tidewater-v1.pl --drop 2 /tmp/d2` and `--drop 3 /tmp/d3`. Convert both with
`xmi2sysml.pl --out` and compare the two `stations/Stations.sysml` files. Then run
`xmi2sysml.pl --delta /tmp/d2/tidewater.xmi /tmp/d3/tidewater.xmi`. Which change appears in the delta and not in the
converted files, and why?
:::
