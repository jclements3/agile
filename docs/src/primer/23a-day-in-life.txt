== A day in the life, week twelve {#day-in-life}

::: bridge
You know: an ordinary day in the Cameo years: e-mail, meetings, screenshots, a briefing to update.

Here it is called: Bob's day in week twelve.

What is new: the day goes to engineering, and the machinery does the bookkeeping.
:::

::: survival Where this chapter fits
What you have: Bob's Thursday, 17 December 2026 -- the second-to-last week of the twelve -- exactly as GitLab and
the stand-up chat recorded it.

What you need: a concrete picture of what the work feels like once the tools are learned, beside the same kind of
day three years earlier in the Cameo era. Managers ask "what does a normal day look like now?" This is the answer.
:::

Nothing on this day was staged for the book. It is an ordinary working day in sprint 6, chosen because it has most
of what a small model team meets in practice: a failed overnight check, a change that the pipeline sends back, a
review that requests changes, a merge that is refused until a discussion is resolved, and the next morning a secret
that nearly got into the repository. Every time below is Eastern time, as GitLab recorded it.

{{include: docs/tidewater/sprints/06/day-in-life/timeline.txt}}

=== 09:00 -- the stand-up, and the night's failure {#dil-standup}

The first thing Bob saw was not an email. It was the GitLab To-Do list and the board: the scheduled "Nightly full
check" pipeline from 02:00 had failed, in every job, before any check ran. Bob opened an issue for it at 09:15,
labelled it, and linked the failing job:

{{include-md: docs/tidewater/sprints/06/day-in-life/incident.md}}

The failure was not in the model. The runner -- the Windows machine that runs the team's pipeline -- had filled its
disk with old build directories. At the stand-up it took one sentence to hand over ("Tom, the nightly died on disk
space, #132"), and Tom had it cleared and re-run by 10:30. In the Cameo era the equivalent -- a tool failing
overnight -- would have been discovered when someone opened the model and it would not load.

The stand-up itself was three lines in the meeting chat, drafted with `talk.pl` and edited before posting
([[talking]]):

{{include: docs/tidewater/sprints/06/standups/2026-12-17-chat.txt}}

You can open the issue Bob filed in the simulation's GitLab:

```sh run sandbox=tidewater timeout=180 expect="#132\s+closed"
perl "$KIT/bin/gitlab.pl" --local gitlab --project tidewater/model issue show 132
```

=== 10:00 -- a draft that the pipeline sends back {#dil-draft}

Bob's own work that week was the hand-written flows in the `ObserveCoast` activity: the fork, the pin, the object
and control flows that the converter could not carry from v1 ([[workbook]]). Bob opened merge request !23 as a
*draft* at 10:00 -- a draft is a merge request that says "look, but do not merge yet" -- and the pipeline ran on it
straight away. It failed. The grammar, lint and outline checks were green; the trace check was red:

{{include: docs/tidewater/sprints/06/pipelines/mr23-first-86.log | lines 97-101}}

Bob had tidied a doc comment on requirement TW-SYS-007 while in the file, and the new wording no longer
matched DOORS. The trace job compares the model's requirement text with the DOORS export on every change; a
*new* difference fails the merge request ([[pipeline]]). The old difference, TW-SYS-006, is the prime's and is
tracked as a discrepancy report, so it does not fail anything. Bob put the wording back, pushed, and the next
pipeline (#87) was green. Bob marked the draft ready at noon. In the Cameo era a reworded requirement in the model
would have been found -- if at all -- at the next DOORS synchronisation, weeks later, by someone else.

=== 14:05 -- reviewing Tom's change {#dil-review}

At 14:05 Bob's To-Do list showed one item: Tom had asked Bob to review !24, "Document what crosses each interface".
Bob did not read the raw text diff alone. Bob read the generated documentation the pipeline attached, then went to
the line that looked wrong and left a line comment with a *suggestion* -- a proposed replacement the author can
apply with one click -- and requested changes:

{{include-md: docs/tidewater/samples/mr-review-thread.md}}

The comment is the review culture the team built in [[reviewing]]: it names the problem ("late" assumes a schedule
nobody wrote down), cites the requirement, and offers a concrete fix that keeps the uncertainty visible as an
assumption to confirm with the prime.

At 14:20 Tom tried to merge. GitLab refused: the project requires every discussion on a merge request to be
resolved first ([[gitlab-setup]]), and Bob's thread was open. Tom applied the suggestion; Bob resolved the thread
at 14:50 and approved; Jim, as the code owner for interfaces, had already approved; Tom merged at 15:10. The same
click closed task #123, because the merge request description said "Closes #123".

The merge request's page shows its checks as GitLab's widgets would:

```sh run expect="!24\s+merged"
perl "$KIT/bin/gitlab.pl" --local gitlab --project tidewater/model mr show 24 | head -8
```

One rough edge is visible there, and the book does not hide it: the test widget lists the requirement gaps the
government has already reported to the prime as *failed tests*, even though the pipeline is green, because the
trace report marks every gap, old or new, as a failure. The pipeline itself only fails on new gaps. Until the
report is changed to mark inherited gaps as skipped, read the widget's "failed" count together with the trace
job's verdict line.

=== 15:30 -- the day closes {#dil-close}

Bob merged !23 at 15:30, which closed Bob's task #121. By the end of the day both changes were on `develop`, both
had passed their checks on the merge request, both had been reviewed and approved by someone other than the
author, and the board, the burn-down and the model site all showed it without anyone writing a
status report. The nightly schedule would run the full check again at 02:00.

> Six things happened before lunch and I knew about every one of them from GitLab, not from email.
> -- Bob, 17 December 2026

The next morning, Friday, Tom's merge request !25 failed a different check: the secrets scan found a test access
token pasted into a helper script. By 11:30 it was fixed: revoke the token, issue a new one as a masked CI/CD
variable, and rewrite the branch so the token never reached `develop` ([[rescue]]). The sprint 6 retrospective's
"stop" list has one line that came from that morning: "Pasting tokens into scripts, even test ones."

=== The same Thursday, 2023, the Cameo way (notional) {#dil-2023}

::: note This half is notional
Nothing below comes from the simulation. It is a composite of how the team described their Cameo-era days in
[[cameo]], written as one day so it can be compared. Treat the times as typical, not measured.
:::

|| Time | December 2023, Cameo and documents | December 2026, GitLab and SysML v2 ||
| 08:30 | Email from the prime: the model export for the review will be late. | GitLab To-Do: the nightly check failed; an issue is open by 09:15. |
| 09:00 | Status meeting, forty minutes, everyone reads their slide. | Stand-up, fifteen minutes, three lines in chat, drafted from GitLab. |
| 10:00 | Bob checks out a Cameo project lock; Tom has it, Bob waits. | Bob opens a draft merge request; the pipeline runs on it in minutes. |
| 11:00 | Bob edits the activity diagram; layout takes most of the hour. | The pipeline finds a reworded requirement before anyone else sees it. |
| 13:00 | Screenshots pasted into a review briefing, slide by slide. | Bob reads Tom's change in the generated docs and leaves a line comment. |
| 14:30 | The review comments come back as a marked-up PDF. | The comment is a suggestion Tom applies with one click. |
| 15:30 | Someone asks which requirements are unverified; "I'll get back to you". | The trace answer is on the model site, current as of the last merge. |
| 16:30 | A requirement changed in DOORS last month; nobody noticed. | Every DOORS difference is checked on every change. |
Table: One Thursday, three years apart. The left column is notional; the right column is the evidence above.

The point of the comparison is not that the old day was lazy. The engineers worked as hard in 2023. The difference
is where the effort went: in 2023 most of it went into moving information between tools and people by hand, and
the checks happened at reviews, weeks later. In 2026 the moving and checking are done by the pipeline on every
change, and the people spend their day on the model and on each other's work.

::: exercise {#ex-dil-own-day} Write your own two-column day
Pick one ordinary day from last month. Write what you did, hour by hour, in the left column. In the right column
write what the same work would look like with a merge request, a pipeline and a model site. Which rows would
change most? Which would not change at all?
:::

=== You can now {#dil-you-can}

- Describe a normal working day in a GitLab model team, hour by hour, with the evidence a manager can check.
- Explain why a draft merge request that fails its pipeline is good news, not bad.
- Read a merge request's widgets and know which numbers are new problems and which are inherited ones.

=== Applying this to your project {#dil-applying}

Copy the shape: a To-Do list instead of email, a nightly full check, draft merge requests, required discussion
resolution, suggestions in reviews. Adapt the times to your team's hours and your stand-up. Decide locally whether
a nightly failure is picked up at the stand-up (Tidewater) or by whoever is on call that week.
