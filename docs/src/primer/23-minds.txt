== How the team changed its mind {#minds}

::: bridge
You know: the objections people raise to any new process: some fair, some fear.

Here it is called: the objections, revisited.

What is new: each one is answered with evidence from the twelve weeks, not with a promise.
:::

::: survival Where this chapter fits
What you have: twelve weeks of evidence -- stand-up chat, retrospectives, a pulse survey every second Thursday,
quotes people did not expect to have written down.

What you need: an honest account of how three skeptical engineers moved from "why can't we just send the Word
doc?" to "keep doing this", without pretending the middle was smooth. You also need a way to measure that change
on your own team, because management will ask, and because the team deserves to see it.
:::

In [[directive]] the team raised nine objections in one meeting. They were not excuses. Each one was a real fear
held by a person who had been burned before: by a document port that took two years, by a tool nobody else could
read, by an approval that never came, by management goals that moved. This chapter takes the objections one at a
time, in the order they were raised, and sets beside each one the evidence the twelve weeks produced. Where the
evidence does not answer the objection, the chapter says so.

=== The pulse, sprint by sprint {#minds-pulse}

At every retrospective each person answered the same five questions, anonymously, on a scale of one (strongly
disagree) to five (strongly agree). The questions were chosen in [[directive]] before any work started, so nobody
could tune them to the result.

The shape matters more than the end point. The line rose in sprint 2, *fell* in sprint 3, and only then climbed.
The dip is the most useful number in this chapter. It came in the sprint where the team stopped learning in a
sandbox and started being judged by the pipeline on real work -- and in the same sprint, a security approval sat
unsigned for three days. If you run this survey on your own team and see a dip in the third or fourth sprint, it
does not mean the change is failing. It means the change has become real.

=== The objections, revisited {#minds-objections}

==== "We already did this once. Modeling took two years and gave us a spider web."

Bob's objection was the deepest, because it was about the last time. The Cameo port had been sold as the end of
document drift; it delivered a model that only three people could open and that nobody trusted.

The evidence is partial, and that is worth saying. The v1 drop arrived with
{{value: docs/tidewater/sprints/06/scorecard.json 7_spider_web.cycles}} package dependency cycle remaining in the v2
model by sprint 6, against two in drop 1 -- the Network and Operations cycle was broken in sprint 4; the DataCenter
and Stations cycle is still there at the end of sprint 6. The worst part definition dropped from fourteen relationship ends in
v1 to {{value: docs/tidewater/sprints/06/scorecard.json 7_spider_web.max_relationships_per_part}} in v2. Tom's
reaction to the first cycle break, in sprint 4:

> The cycle was two lines. In Cameo it was a week of looking at a diagram.
> -- Tom, 24 November 2026

The measurement is in [[no-spider-web]]. What changed Bob's mind was not the numbers, though. It was that the
complexity was *visible on every change* instead of discovered at a review. A spider web grows one innocent link at
a time; a check that runs on every merge request stops the innocent link.

==== "Why can't we just do systems engineering the old way?"

Tom asked it; Bob said it out loud on the first morning:

> Why can't we just send the Word doc? It worked for twenty years.
> -- Bob, 5 October 2026

The answer in [[directive]] was that nothing changes what a requirement or a baseline *is*; what changes is how
the team keeps them. The evidence for that answer came three days later, from Bob:

> I found the drift in two minutes. It used to take a week, and we only looked before a review.
> -- Bob, 8 October 2026

That sentence was the first time anyone on the team compared the new way to the old way in a unit they cared
about. It was also the first number in the scorecard ([[scorecard]]): the trace answers in
{{value: docs/tidewater/sprints/06/scorecard.json 4_trace_answer_seconds}} seconds what the document era answered in
{{value: docs/tidewater/sprints/06/scorecard.json document_era_baseline_notional.unverified_question_days}} days
(notional, from the program's last document-era review cycle).

==== "We're systems engineers, not programmers."

Nobody wrote a program in twelve weeks. They edited text files in Vim, which all three already used, and typed git
commands they learned in an afternoon ([[git]]). Bob's verdict after that afternoon:

> Spent the afternoon in the practice repo. Branch, commit, merge. It is configuration management with fewer forms.
> -- Bob, 6 October 2026

The harder moment came in the third sprint, when the pipeline said no to Bob for the first time:

> A red X on my own MR. One missing word, and the machine found it before Jim did.
> -- Bob, 4 November 2026

That quote is from the dip sprint, and it is not a happy one. It became one of the reasons the pulse rose
afterwards: the red X was private, immediate and fixable, which a red-pen review two weeks later never was.

==== "The contractor owns the model. Anything we do is a copy that will go stale."

This objection is only partly answered by the first twelve weeks, because only one prime drop arrived in them. What
the twelve weeks proved is the *mechanism*: drop 1 came in on the vendor branch ([[sprint-2]]), the government's own
edits sat beside it without being overwritten, and every gap the government found went back to the prime as a
discrepancy report -- {{value: docs/tidewater/sprints/06/scorecard.json 6_drs.opened}} of them by the end of sprint 6,
none closed yet, because the prime closes them in later drops. Whether the copy stays current is answered by the
drops in [[prime-changes]], not by this chapter.

==== "Nobody outside our team will be able to read SysML v2 text."

Jim raised it as devil's advocate. The answer turned out to be that nobody outside the team *needed* to read the
text: they read the model site the pipeline publishes. The program manager's reaction in the sprint 4 review:

> I can open this on my phone? I have never seen the model without a briefing.
> -- Program manager, 20 November 2026

Fewer people could read the Cameo model, which needed a licence. More people read the v2 model than ever read the
v1 one, because it was a web page.

==== "The ISSM will never approve this in time."

Tom was half right, and the evidence shows it. The toolchain review did slow the team down:

> Three days waiting on a signature. The pipeline was the fast part.
> -- Tom, 10 November 2026

That wait is part of the sprint 3 dip. The lesson the team drew, recorded in the sprint 3 retrospective, was to
stop "waiting for the ISSM without a date" -- to ask for a date, and to keep working on what did not need the
approval. The approval came. The objection was right about the delay and wrong about the outcome.

==== "We'll spend twelve weeks learning tools and have nothing to show."

The BLUF ([[bluf]]) answers this with the time split: learning and platform work fell from most of sprint 1 to a
small share of sprint 6. The scorecard answers it with results: TODO lines from the conversion fell to
{{value: docs/tidewater/sprints/06/scorecard.json 1_todo_lines}}, every change in sprint 6 was gated
({{value: docs/tidewater/sprints/06/scorecard.json 3_gated_changes}}), and an interim baseline was tagged and
reproduced ({{value: docs/tidewater/sprints/06/scorecard.json 9_baselines.reproducible}}). There was something to
show from sprint 2 onward; the problem in sprint 2 was that the team showed it badly ([[talking]]).

==== "Management will just move the goalposts."

Tom said this quietly, at the end of the meeting. The defense was the scorecard, agreed in writing in week one. At
the sprint 6 review the program manager did not move the goalposts; they asked for the same ones again:

> Keep doing this. Show me the same numbers in March.
> -- Program manager, 24 December 2026

==== "In three years there'll be another directive."

Probably true, and the twelve weeks cannot disprove it. What the team kept from them is not a tool but a habit:
the system's description as reviewed text under configuration control, with checks that run on every change. The
text is in an open standard, the checks are scripts the team owns, and the history is in git. Whatever the next
directive brings, it starts from a model that can be read, diffed and converted -- which the v1 model could not.

=== What actually changed minds {#minds-what-changed}

Reading the quotes in order, three things recur:

- **A number in a unit the engineer cared about.** "Two minutes, it used to take a week." Not a dashboard, not a
  velocity chart -- a comparison to their own experience.
- **A mistake caught privately and early.** The red X on Bob's merge request, the trace gate that stopped Bob
  deleting a satisfy Bob thought was a duplicate (sprint 5). Being stopped by a machine before a colleague saw the
  mistake built trust in the checks faster than any explanation.
- **Being seen by management without a briefing.** The program manager on the phone; Jim answering the deputy PM's
  trace question with a link instead of a week of slides:

> The deputy PM asked for the trace numbers. I sent a link.
> -- Jim, 9 December 2026

What did *not* change minds: being told the new way was better, the GitLab feature list, and the sprint 2 demo,
which showed the terminal instead of the answer. Management's feedback on that demo was blunt -- "I did not follow
the demo. What did we get for the two weeks?" -- and it is in the evidence too.

::: exercise {#ex-minds-survey} Run the pulse on your own team
Take the five questions above. Ask them at your next three retrospectives, anonymously, on a one-to-five scale.
Plot the mean. Before you look at the result, write down which sprint you expect the dip in, and why.
:::

::: exercise {#ex-minds-objections} Write your team's objections down
In your kickoff meeting, write every objection on the whiteboard in the speaker's words, with the speaker's
name if they agree. Put the list in the wiki. At the end of each sprint, add the evidence for or against each one.
Which objections are still unanswered after six sprints?
:::

=== You can now {#minds-you-can}

- Measure a team's change of mind with a short, fixed pulse survey and read its shape, not just its end point.
- Answer an old-way objection with evidence from your own project instead of argument.
- Recognize the third-sprint dip as a sign the change has become real.

=== Applying this to your project {#minds-applying}

Copy the five questions and the habit of writing objections down in the speaker's words. Adapt the scale and the
timing to your retrospectives. Decide locally whether the survey is anonymous (Tidewater's was) and who sees the
raw answers. Do not promise management a rising line; promise them the measurement, and explain the dip in advance.
