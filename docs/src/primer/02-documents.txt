== Thirty years of documents {#documents}

::: note What you have, what you need
**What you have:** a career's worth of habits for controlling documents, reviewing changes and briefing decisions.

**What you need from this chapter:** a clear list of which of those habits are worth keeping (most of them) and
which costs they hid. Nothing to type yet.
:::

=== A Tuesday in 2019 {#documents-tuesday}

The request arrived at 4:10 on a Tuesday afternoon, the way such requests always arrived: as a one-line email from
the deputy program manager, copied to the program manager, with the subject line "Quick question."

> Jim -- before Thursday's program review, can you tell me which Tidewater requirements don't have a verification
> method yet? Just a list is fine. Thanks.

It was not a quick question, and everybody on the distribution knew it. The answer lived in four places. The
System Requirements Specification (SRS), revision C, held the requirement text and a section 4 "Qualification
provisions" table. The Verification Cross-Reference Matrix (VCRM) was an Excel workbook with one row per
requirement and a column for the method (test, analysis, inspection, demonstration). The prime contractor kept the
authoritative requirement text in DOORS and sent a CSV export every month. And the action item tracker, a
different spreadsheet, held the open items from the last design review, several of which said things like
"Contractor to propose verification approach for SRS 3.2.7."

Jim forwarded the email to Bob with a single line ("Can you pull this together?") and Bob spent Wednesday doing
what Bob had done many times before. Bob opened the latest DOORS CSV in Excel, sorted it by object identifier, and
pasted it next to the VCRM. A lookup formula matched the identifiers. Forty rows did not match, because the VCRM
still used the paragraph numbers from revision B and DOORS used the new object identifiers. Bob fixed those by hand
from the cross-reference table in the SRS appendix, found three requirements in DOORS that were not in the VCRM at
all, found two in the VCRM that DOORS had deleted, checked the action item tracker for the ones with open actions,
and by 3 p.m. on Wednesday had a list of eleven requirements with no verification method. Jim reformatted it as a
slide, the deputy program manager presented it on Thursday, and the program review moved on to the next topic.

Two weeks later the prime delivered revision D of the VCRM. Four of the eleven had been fixed. Nobody updated the
slide, and nobody ran the comparison again until the next "quick question."

::: sidebar Why this scene matters
Nothing in the scene is a failure. Every person did their job well. Bob's answer was correct on Wednesday
afternoon. The trouble is that it was correct *only* on Wednesday afternoon, it cost a skilled engineer a day and
a half, and the work that produced it left no trace that anyone could re-run. Keep that last point in mind; it is
the whole argument of [[devsecops]] in one sentence.
:::

=== The document set {#documents-set}

For most of Jim's career, systems engineering on a government program meant producing, reviewing and controlling
a set of documents. The names vary by organization; Tidewater's set was typical:

|| Document | What it held | Who changed it ||
| SEMP (Systems Engineering Management Plan) | how the program does systems engineering | the government, rarely |
| ConOps (Concept of Operations) | who uses the system and how, in prose | the government, once |
| SRS (System Requirements Specification) | the "shall" statements, numbered paragraphs | the prime, through the CCB |
| SSDD (System/Subsystem Design Description) | the architecture: segments, subsystems, block diagrams as pictures | the prime |
| ICDs (Interface Control Documents) | each interface: messages, fields, timing, connectors | the prime, both sides sign |
| VCRM (Verification Cross-Reference Matrix) | each requirement's verification method and event | the prime, a spreadsheet |
| RTM (Requirements Traceability Matrix) | parent-child links between requirement levels | the prime, a spreadsheet or DOORS |
| Risk register, action item tracker | open risks and actions | the government, spreadsheets |
| Design review packages | slides summarizing all of the above | everybody, for each review |
Table: The document-era artifact set (typical; Tidewater's names)

Each document had an owner, a revision letter, a change history page and a signature block. Changes moved through
a formal process: somebody wrote an engineering change proposal (ECP) or a document change request, the request
went into the queue for the monthly configuration control board (CCB), the board approved, rejected or deferred
it, and the owner incorporated approved changes into the next revision. Between revisions, changes were tracked as
"redlines": a marked-up copy of the document, usually a PDF with comments, sometimes a Word file with tracked
changes, occasionally a printout with handwriting.

The spreadsheets lived alongside the documents and did the work the documents could not. The VCRM was a
spreadsheet because a matrix is a spreadsheet. The RTM was a spreadsheet because parent-child links between a few
hundred requirements are rows. The action item tracker was a spreadsheet because everything that did not fit
anywhere else was a spreadsheet.

The briefings sat on top. Every review, every status meeting and every decision produced slides. The slides
summarized the documents and spreadsheets, and for the people in the room the slides *were* the system: few
decision makers read the SRS, but everybody saw the chart.

=== What worked {#documents-worked}

It is fashionable to sneer at document-based systems engineering. This book will not. The document era got a
great deal right, and almost everything it got right survives into the way of working this book describes.

**Everybody could read the documents.** A Word document or a PDF needs no special software, no license and no
training. An operator, a test engineer, a contracting officer and a general can all open the SRS. This is a real
virtue, and the team's fear of losing it is one of the objections in [[directive]].

**The documents were the contract.** The CDRL told the prime to deliver an SRS, and the government accepted or
rejected it. Signatures meant something. When a dispute arose, the signed revision settled it.

**Changes were reviewed.** Nothing went into a controlled document without a second pair of eyes and a board
decision. The review culture of the CCB, the habit of asking "who approved this, and when?", is exactly what
DevSecOps calls *change control*, and it is the habit Tom will build the pipeline around.

**Baselines existed.** "SRS revision C" named a fixed, known set of requirements. Everyone could point at it.

**Traceability was taken seriously.** The RTM and VCRM were tedious to maintain, but the program knew that every
requirement needed a parent and a verification, and it checked, at least at each review.

::: tip Keep this list
Hold on to these five virtues: readable by anyone, contractually meaningful, reviewed, baselined, traced. When
someone tells you DevSecOps throws away good systems engineering practice, ask which of the five it throws away.
In the chapters that follow, each one is kept, and each one gets cheaper.
:::

=== What it cost {#documents-cost}

The costs of the document era were real but hidden, because they were paid in engineers' time rather than in a
budget line. Nobody ever wrote "1.5 days to answer the deputy's question" on a timecard; it was just Wednesday.

To make those costs visible, Jim later reconstructed four of them from the program's last document-era review
cycle. They are reconstructions, not measurements, and this book labels them as notional wherever they appear.
They are the "before" column of the scorecard in [[scorecard]].

|| Activity in the document era | Typical effort or elapsed time (notional) ||
| A requirement change, from change request to incorporated revision | 23 working days |
| "Which requirements are unverified?", answered from the VCRM, DOORS export and SRS | 2 working days |
| A model or design review: screenshots in a 60-slide briefing, prepared, reviewed and presented | 3 weeks |
| Finding text that differs between DOORS and the documents (or the model) | never done systematically |
Table: The document-era baseline (notional, reconstructed from the last review cycle)

Where did the 23 working days of a requirement change go? Almost none of it was engineering. Jim's reconstruction
of one change, a clarification to a data-latency requirement, looked like this:

|| Step | Working days (notional) ||
| Engineer drafts the change request in the CR template | 1 |
| Change request waits for the next monthly CCB agenda | 11 |
| CCB meeting; approved with a comment | 1 |
| Comment resolved by email; revised wording approved by the chair | 3 |
| Prime incorporates the change into the next SRS revision draft | 4 |
| Government reviews the draft revision, signs | 3 |
Table: Where the time went in one requirement change (notional)

Most of the elapsed time is waiting: for a meeting, for a revision, for a signature. The meeting itself took a day,
and the engineering, deciding what the requirement should say, took less than that. The process was designed for a
world where making a copy of a document was expensive and distributing it was slow, so changes were batched into
monthly meetings and quarterly revisions. Batching is efficient when each batch costs a lot. It is wasteful when
each change could be reviewed, approved and incorporated on the day it is proposed.

The other hidden costs were less visible still.

**Every answer was a one-off.** Bob's Wednesday produced a correct list and a slide. It did not produce anything
that could be run again. The next time someone asked, Bob started from scratch, because the spreadsheet formulas
were in a copy of the VCRM that was now two revisions out of date.

**Consistency was nobody's job.** The SRS, the VCRM, the RTM, the DOORS database and the ICDs each held part of
the truth, and each was maintained by a different person on a different schedule. When they disagreed, which they
always did a little, the disagreement was found by accident, usually by someone preparing for a review. Requirement
text in DOORS that differed from the text in the SRS was a known problem with no known size.

**The briefing became the truth.** Because decision makers saw slides, the slides were where decisions were made,
and the slides were copies of copies. A block diagram drawn for a 2017 review would reappear, slightly edited, in
2019, long after the architecture had changed. Nobody could say which picture was current.

**Configuration management meant file names.** The program's shared drive held files named
`SRS_RevC_final.docx`, `SRS_RevC_final_v2.docx`, `SRS_RevC_final_v2_JIM_comments.docx` and, memorably,
`SRS_RevC_FINAL_use_this_one.docx`. Tom, who ran the CM database, could tell you which file was the signed
revision. Nobody else could, and the CM database did not know about the working copies at all.

**Knowledge lived in people.** Bob knew why the VCRM's row 214 had a strange formula. Jim knew which ICD
paragraph had been "temporarily" waived in 2016. When people moved on, the knowledge went with them.

::: objection "The documents worked. We delivered systems with them for thirty years."
That is true, and nothing in this book disputes it.

Answer: The question is not whether documents can work but what they cost and what they make impossible. The
document process worked by spending skilled engineers' time on copying, reconciling and waiting. It made it
impossible to ask the same question twice and get a fresh answer cheaply. The chapters in Part III measure what
the same questions cost in the new way; [[scorecard]] puts the two side by side.
:::

=== The habits worth keeping {#documents-habits}

Before the team moves on, it is worth naming the document-era habits that will survive the transition, because the
team's discouragement in [[directive]] comes partly from believing that everything they know is being thrown out.
It is not. Here is the translation the book will use, one habit at a time. [[devsecops]] gives the full table.

- **Change requests** become *merge requests*: a proposed change, with a description, reviewed and approved before
  it is incorporated. The difference is that a merge request contains the actual change, not a description of it,
  and can be approved the day it is written.
- **The CCB's approval** becomes *a code owner's approval*: a named person, responsible for an area, approves
  changes to that area. The board still exists for decisions that need a board; it no longer has to touch every
  editorial fix.
- **Baselines** become *tags*: a name fixed to an exact, retrievable state of the model. Revision C becomes
  `baseline-1`.
- **The VCRM and RTM** become *reports computed from the model*: instead of maintaining a matrix by hand, the team
  writes the trace into the model and lets a program compute the matrix whenever anyone asks.
- **The design review package** becomes *a published site*: the current model, its diagrams and its reports, always
  generated from the baseline, never pasted.
- **Redlines** become *diffs*: a precise, line-by-line record of what changed, produced automatically.
- **The CM database** becomes *the repository's history*: every change, who made it, when, why, and who approved it,
  kept automatically.

None of these translations is perfect, and the book points out where they break. But the shape of the work is
recognizably the same. Systems engineering still means knowing what the system must do, how it is put together,
how each requirement will be shown to be met, and how changes are controlled. What changes is how much of that
work is done by hand.

=== What the spreadsheets were really doing {#documents-spreadsheets}

Every program has a spreadsheet that nobody is allowed to touch. On Tidewater it was the VCRM, and Tom, who had
seen every revision of it, once described it as "the most important database on the program, stored in the least
reliable format we have."

Spreadsheets did three jobs on the program, and it is worth separating them, because each job has a better home in
the new way of working.

**The spreadsheet as a database.** The VCRM, the RTM, the interface list and the action item tracker were all
tables of records with identifiers, attributes and relationships between them. That is a database. Spreadsheets
are a poor database: nothing stops two rows from having the same identifier, a formula from referring to the wrong
column after someone inserts one, or a copy of the file from drifting away from the original. In the new way, the
records live in the model (requirements, satisfy and verify relationships) or in GitLab (issues), both of which
enforce identity and keep history.

**The spreadsheet as a calculator.** Bob's lookup formulas, the trace coverage percentages and the "requirements
without a verification method" count were calculations over the records. Each calculation was written by hand,
in one copy of one file, and lost when the file was replaced. In the new way, calculations are small programs that
run in the pipeline against the current model, every time. The trace report in [[pipeline]] is Bob's Wednesday,
written once and run thousands of times.

**The spreadsheet as a report.** The colored cells, the filtered views and the pivot tables pasted into slides were
how the records reached decision makers. In the new way, reports are generated from the model and published
([[tools]]); nobody formats a cell.

::: sidebar The VCRM's row 214
Row 214 of the VCRM held a formula that combined three columns and a lookup into a hidden sheet. It computed whether
a requirement's verification event had been scheduled. Bob wrote it in 2017. In 2021 someone sorted the sheet
without including the hidden columns, and for four months row 214's formula pointed at the wrong requirement. Nobody
noticed until a test readiness review. The formula was right; the format was the problem. A calculation that lives
in a cell is only as safe as every edit anyone ever makes to that file.
:::

=== A year in the document era {#documents-year}

To see the pattern of the old way, it helps to look at a whole year rather than a single Tuesday. Here is
Tidewater's systems engineering calendar for a typical year before the Cameo port, reconstructed from the team's
old meeting invitations. The effort figures are notional.

|| When | Event | What the team did | Effort (notional) ||
| monthly | CCB | assemble change requests, write the agenda, record decisions, update the change log | 2 days per month |
| monthly | DOORS export from the prime | reconcile the export against the VCRM and SRS | 1 to 2 days |
| quarterly | SRS / ICD revision | review the prime's revision against approved changes, comment, sign | 1 to 2 weeks |
| quarterly | program management review | build the SE section of the briefing (status, risks, TBD/TBR counts) | 3 to 5 days |
| twice a year | technical review (SRR, PDR, CDR, TRR ...) | review packages, comment matrices, action items | 3 to 6 weeks |
| ad hoc | "quick questions" | one-off answers from the spreadsheets | about a day each, several a month |
Table: A document-era year, reconstructed (effort notional)

Add it up and roughly a third of the team's year went into reconciling, reformatting and re-answering: work that
produced no new engineering knowledge, only fresh copies of old knowledge in a new format. Nobody on the team
thought of it that way at the time, because each piece of it was necessary given how the information was stored.
The waste was in the storage, not in the people.

That third of a year is the budget the new way of working draws on. When [[directive]] asks what the team can stop
doing, and when [[scorecard]] compares effort before and after, this table is where the comparison starts.

=== Why the document era lasted {#documents-lasted}

If the costs were this large, why did the document era last thirty years? It is worth answering honestly, because
the same forces will push back against the new way.

- **The costs were invisible.** Time spent reconciling spreadsheets was never charged to "reconciling
  spreadsheets." It was charged to systems engineering. Nobody could see it, so nobody could argue to reduce it.
- **The contract required documents.** CDRLs named document deliverables with data item descriptions that assumed
  a document. Changing what the prime delivered meant changing the contract.
- **Everyone knew how.** A new engineer could be productive with Word and Excel on the first day. Any new way had
  to compete with zero training cost.
- **Failures were rare and quiet.** Most of the time, the reconciliations caught the inconsistencies before they
  mattered. When they did not, the failure appeared months later as a test problem or a rework, far from its cause.
- **Nothing better was available to this team.** Until SysML v2's textual notation, the alternatives were diagram
  tools that, as [[cameo]] shows, kept most of the costs and added new ones.

The last point matters most. The team's resistance to the directive is not stubbornness; it is the reasonable
conclusion of people who were offered an alternative once before and found it worse. The new way has to be better
on the measures they care about, and it has to show that early.

=== Exercises {#documents-exercises}

::: exercise Count the copies
Pick one requirement on a program you know. List every place its text appears (documents, spreadsheets, databases,
slides, emails). For each, write who maintains it and when it was last updated. How many of those places agree,
word for word, today? How would you find out without reading them all?
:::

::: exercise Reconstruct a change
Take one recent change to a controlled document on your program. Reconstruct its timeline as in the table "Where
the time went in one requirement change". Separate the time spent deciding from the time spent waiting. What
fraction was waiting?
:::

For each of the five virtues of the document era (readable by anyone, contractually meaningful, reviewed,
baselined, traced), write one sentence on how your current process achieves it and one on what it costs. Keep the
page; you will revisit it after [[scorecard]].
:::
