== Preface {#preface}

This book is a parachute drop.

Picture three systems engineers standing at the door of an aircraft. They have spent their careers writing
specifications, reconciling spreadsheets and briefing chiefs. A few years ago someone handed them a modeling tool
and told them to put everything they knew into it. They did, by hand, one box and one line at a time. Now the
jump light is on again. Below them is a place called "MBSE DevSecOps," and they have been told to land there,
set up camp, and show progress within twelve weeks. They have no map of that place. They have no software
background. They have never written a pipeline, opened a merge request or resolved a merge conflict. They have
the clothes on their backs, and one tool they trust: the Vim editor, which all three of them have used for years
to write notes, edit requirement lists and clean up exported text files.

This book is written for people in that position. It follows Jim, Bob and Tom, the three government engineers
responsible for the Tidewater system model, through their first twelve weeks. It shows what they did each day,
what they typed, what broke, what they argued about and what they measured. It also explains, as plainly as we
can, why each step is worth taking. By the end of the twelve weeks the team has a SysML v2 model under
configuration control in GitLab, an automated pipeline that checks every change, a trace report that used to take
two days and now takes seconds, and a management briefing built from measurements instead of opinions. They also,
somewhat to their own surprise, like working this way. The book tries to show how that change of mind happened,
without pretending it was easy.

::: note What you have, what you need
**What you have:** a Windows laptop, the Vim editor, years of systems engineering experience, and a model in a tool
you were never comfortable with.

**What you need from this preface:** a picture of the people, the system and the calendar, so the rest of the
book has somewhere to hang. Nothing to install yet; that starts in [[survival]].
:::

=== Who this book is for {#preface-audience}

You will get the most from this book if you are a systems engineer who:

- knows what a requirement, an interface, a verification method and a baseline are;
- has worked with documents (a SEMP, an SRS, ICDs), spreadsheets (requirement matrices, trace tables, action item
  lists) and briefings (design reviews, CCB packages);
- has seen or used a SysML v1 tool such as Cameo Systems Modeler, even if you did not enjoy it;
- has been told that the program is "moving to DevSecOps" and is not yet sure what that means for you.

You do not need to know how to program. You will type commands into a terminal, edit text files and read the
output of automated checks, and the book explains every one of those things the first time it appears. If you
already know git, GitLab or SysML v2, you can skim the chapters that teach them and spend your time on the case
study chapters in Part III and the chapters about making the case to management in Part IV.

The book is also for the people around such a team: the program manager who issued the directive and wants to
know what to expect, the information system security manager (ISSM) who has to approve the new tools, and the
contractor who delivers the models. Each of them appears in the story, and each chapter notes what they need to
do and when.

=== How to read this book {#preface-how}

The book has five parts.

- **Part I, Where they start**, tells the team's history: decades of documents, then the Cameo years, then the
  directive that started this story. It explains DevSecOps in systems engineering words and introduces SysML v2 to
  people who know SysML v1. Read it first even if you are impatient. The objections raised in Part I are the
  objections you will hear (or raise) yourself, and the rest of the book answers them one at a time.
- **Part II, Week zero**, sets everything up: the survival kit on the laptop, git from zero, Vim for models, the
  operating model, GitLab, the DevSecOps tools worth turning on, the pipeline and the daily rhythm. Part II is the
  part you type along with.
- **Part III, Twelve weeks**, is the case study proper: six two-week sprints, one chapter each, with the real
  issues, merge requests, pipeline logs, stand-up notes and metrics from the run.
- **Part IV, Making the case**, assembles the twelve-week scorecard, walks through the management briefing slide
  by slide, and revisits each objection from Part I against the evidence.
- **Part V, Living with it**, covers what comes after the first twelve weeks: administration, maintenance through
  the life cycle (every prime contractor delivery, every baseline, the remaining months of the project), and the
  mistakes to avoid.

The appendices hold a command reference, every template, checklists, a glossary that translates between systems
engineering, DevSecOps and SysML v2, and answers to the exercises.

=== Type along {#preface-type}

This book is meant to be read with a terminal open. Every command shown in a code block is real and has been run
against the material that ships with the agile kit this book comes from. When you see

```sh run expect="drop 1 \(v1\.0, 2026-10-02\)" timeout=120
perl "$KIT/sim/tidewater-v1.pl" --drop 1 drop1
```

type it, press Enter, and compare what you see with what the book shows. (The commands in this book are not only
shown; they are run: a test in the kit runs every command a reader is told to type, chapter by chapter, against the
same material, before the book is printed.) If they differ, that is useful
information: either your setup differs (and [[survival]] will help you find out how), or the material has changed
since this book was generated. Either way you will learn more from the difference than from reading.

Commands are written for the Git Bash terminal that comes with Git for Windows, because that is what Jim, Bob and
Tom have on their government laptops. They also work in a Linux terminal. Paths use forward slashes. When a
command is long it is broken across lines with a trailing backslash, which the shell joins back together.

Vim commands are written the way Vim's own help writes them: `:w` means type a colon, then `w`, then press Enter;
`\mf` means press the backslash key, then `m`, then `f` in normal mode. The kit's Vim help is always one command
away: `:help agile`.

::: tip The exercises
Most chapters end with exercises. Some ask you to type and observe; some ask you to decide something and defend the
decision. Answers are in [[answers]]. The decision exercises have more than one defensible answer; the answers
appendix gives the one the team chose and why.
:::

=== Conventions used in this book {#preface-conventions}

- `Fixed-width text` is something you type or something a program prints: a command, a file name, a line of SysML.
- A code block that starts with a command is meant to be typed. In a block whose lines start with `$ `, type what
  follows each `$ ` (not the `$` itself); the other lines are what the command prints.
- `$KIT` stands for the folder the agile kit is in (`~/projects/agile` on Tidewater's laptops); [[survival]] shows
  how to set it once, the way the pipeline sets it. File paths without `$KIT` are relative to the folder you are in
  unless they start with `/` or `~`. `~/w2s` is the practice folder the book uses; use any folder you like.
- **Bold** marks a term the first time it is defined. The glossary in [[glossary]] has all of them.
- Boxes are labeled by kind. *Note* and *tip* boxes add detail; *warning* boxes mark a mistake the team made or nearly
  made; *sidebar* boxes step back from the story; *objection* boxes record a skeptic's argument in the speaker's words,
  followed by the answer and where the evidence is; *exercise* boxes are for you.
- Quotations from Jim, Bob and Tom come from the team's notes, retrospectives and stand-up answers, lightly edited.
- Numbers from the document era are reconstructions and are labeled *notional*. Numbers from the twelve weeks come
  from the records the tools kept, and each table says where it came from.

=== The system: Tidewater {#preface-tidewater}

Tidewater is a fictional coastal weather-observation network, invented for this book. Nothing in it describes a
real system, a real program or a real organization, and every number in it is notional.

Tidewater has three segments:

- **Stations.** Moored buoys offshore and shore stations on masts. Each station measures wind, air pressure and
  air temperature; buoys also measure waves. A buoy runs on its own solar panel and battery and sends its
  observations over a radio link; when the link is down it keeps them and sends them later.
- **The data center.** It ingests every observation, checks its quality, archives it with a quality flag, and
  publishes a marine bulletin on a fixed schedule for forecasters and mariners.
- **Operations.** A maintenance depot, a service vessel with a deck crane, and a pool of spare parts that keep the
  stations running.

The system is small on purpose. A real program's model has thousands of elements; Tidewater's v1 model has a few
hundred. Small enough to read in an afternoon, large enough to have every problem a real model has: requirements
that nothing satisfies, requirements that nothing verifies, text in the requirements database that no longer
matches the text in the model, diagrams nobody updates, and packages so tangled that a change in one place
surprises someone in another.

=== The cast {#preface-cast}

**Jim** is the MBSE lead and the person responsible for the Tidewater system model on the government side. Jim
owns the big picture: what the model must contain, which work comes first, what "done" means, and what gets
reported upward. In this story Jim is also the one who received the directive to adopt DevSecOps, which makes Jim
the person who has to explain it to the other two without fully understanding it yet. Jim has twenty-six years in
systems engineering, most of them writing and reviewing specifications.

**Bob** is the MBSE engineer. Bob built most of the Cameo model during the document port and knows where every
block came from, including the ones that should not be there. Bob is the most skeptical member of the team about
the new direction, and the one whose skepticism is most useful, because Bob asks precise questions: "Who approves a
change?", "Where is the baseline?", "What happens when the contractor's model changes?"

**Tom** is the configuration management engineer, and in this story becomes the team's DevSecOps engineer. Tom
volunteered to build the pipeline, mostly because somebody had to and Tom has run the program's CM database for a
decade. Tom has never written a pipeline, never administered a GitLab project, and never written a line of code
beyond spreadsheet formulas and Vim macros. Tom likes procedures, checklists and things that can be audited.

Around the team:

- **The program manager** issued the directive and will judge the results at the end of the twelve weeks. The
  program manager is not hostile, but needs to show the program's leadership that the money spent on tools and
  training produces something measurable.
- **The deputy program manager** chairs the configuration control board (CCB) and cares about one question above
  all: can a change be traced from request to approval to implementation?
- **The ISSM** (information system security manager) must approve any new tool or data flow on the program's
  network. The ISSM is careful, busy and, for three days in week six, unreachable.
- **The prime contractor**, Northwind Marine Systems (fictional), builds Tidewater and maintains the authoritative
  SysML v1 model in Cameo under a contract data requirement (CDRL A003, "System Model"). The prime delivers the
  model to the government as "drops": an XMI export of the model, a ReqIF export of the DOORS requirements, a CSV
  copy of the requirements and a manifest with checksums.
- **The prime's modeler** is the contractor engineer the team talks to about the content of each drop.
- **The enterprise GitLab administrators** run the organization's GitLab server. They create groups and projects
  on request, register runners and enforce the organization's settings. They are helpful and slow.

::: note A note on pronouns
The book refers to every person by name or by role, and uses "they" when a pronoun is needed.
:::

=== The calendar {#preface-calendar}

The project in this book lasts eighteen months: thirty-nine two-week sprints from Monday 5 October 2026 to
Friday 31 March 2028. The first twelve weeks, sprints 1 to 6, are the subject of Part III. The rest of the project
is summarized in [[maintenance]].

|| Sprint | Dates | What happens ||
| 1 | 5 to 16 Oct 2026 | GitLab project created; the first pipeline; drop 1 received and assessed |
| 2 | 19 to 30 Oct 2026 | drop 1 imported through the vendor branch; every change gated |
| 3 | 2 to 13 Nov 2026 | Network and Types cleaned up; DOORS requirements imported; the hardest week for morale |
| 4 | 16 to 27 Nov 2026 | Stations cleaned up; gaps reported to the prime; the model site shown to management |
| 5 | 30 Nov to 11 Dec 2026 | Data center and operations cleaned up; release procedure rehearsed |
| 6 | 14 to 25 Dec 2026 | behavior work starts; interim baseline; the management briefing |
| 7 to 20 | Dec 2026 to Jul 2027 | the port completed; drops 2 and 3 absorbed; baseline 1 accepted |
| 17 to 39 | May 2027 to Mar 2028 | maintenance against drops 4 to 7; baselines 2 and 3 |
Table: The eighteen months at a glance (the twelve weeks are sprints 1 to 6)

The prime delivers seven drops over the eighteen months:

|| Drop | Version | Delivered | What changed upstream ||
| 1 | v1.0 | Fri 2 Oct 2026 | the model at contract start |
| 2 | v1.1 | Fri 8 Jan 2027 | a humidity sensor and its requirement; one requirement text corrected |

Federal holidays fall inside the twelve weeks on 12 October, 11 November, 26 November and 25 December, and each
team member takes some leave. The plans in Part III account for both, because a plan that ignores the calendar is
the first thing a skeptic will pick apart.

=== What "done" looks like after twelve weeks {#preface-done}

So that you know where the story is going, here is the state of things at the end of sprint 6, in the terms the
program manager asked for:

- every change to the model goes through a merge request, is checked automatically by a pipeline, and is approved
  by a code owner other than its author; nothing reaches the integration branch any other way;
- the model's conversion debt, the number of places where the converter could not carry a SysML v1 construct into
  SysML v2, is measured on every change and has fallen sprint by sprint;
- the trace between the DOORS requirements and the model is computed on every change, including the text drift
  between them that nobody had ever checked systematically;
- every problem in the contractor's model is tracked as a discrepancy report with an owner, a due drop and a
  closing condition;
- an interim baseline is tagged, reproducible and published with release notes;
- the team can show all of the above in a browser, live, in a management review, instead of in screenshots pasted
  into slides.

None of these things require the team to become software developers. They require the team to do what systems
engineers already know how to do (control configuration, trace requirements, review changes, verify) with tools
that make those things cheap instead of expensive. That is the argument of this book, and the twelve weeks are the
evidence.
