== Writing stories and tasks for model work {#stories}

::: survival
What you have: years of work breakdown structures, action item lists and IMS lines. You know how to break a
program into deliverables, and you know how a schedule line that says "Model update -- 40% complete" can stay at
40% for three months.

What you need: to write the small pieces of work this process runs on -- a story that fits one sprint and ends in
something you can show, tasks under a week that each end in a merged change -- and to write their "done" so that a
machine can check most of it. You also need to budget the time it takes to learn all of this, before it gets
taken from your evenings.
:::

::: note Core path
This chapter is CORE: you write stories and tasks from the first sprint planning on. The splitting rules and the
size limits themselves are in [[om-splitting]] and [[om-limits]]; this chapter is about writing the issues.
:::

=== The Monday the backlog was empty {#stories-monday}

Jim's first sprint planning, on 5 October, was supposed to take ninety minutes. It took three hours, and most of it
went on a single question Bob asked about the first card on the board: "What does 'Port the Stations package' mean
when it's done?"

Nobody could say. In the document years, a line like that meant "Bob is working on Stations," and it would be done
when Bob said it was done, which would be at the review. In the Cameo years it meant the same thing, with a model
instead of a document. The line had no edge. It could not be finished, only abandoned.

By the end of the three hours the team had rewritten that one line into four stories, and two of them still did not
fit the sprint. The rest of this chapter is what they learned that afternoon and in the five plannings after it,
written down so you can get there in less than three hours.

=== Stories, tasks and where each lives {#stories-where}

Recall the levels from [[om-levels]]: a project holds tomes, a tome holds epics, an epic holds stories, a story
holds tasks. Two of those levels are written by the team every sprint, and they live in different places:

|| Level | Size | Lives in | Written by | Ends in ||
| Story | less than one sprint | a GitLab issue labelled `type::story`, and one line in the kit's journal (TW-&lt;issue number&gt;) | the person who owns it, at refinement or kickoff | something shown at the sprint review |
| Task | at most 5 points (a working week) | a GitLab issue labelled `type::task`, linked to its story | the person doing it, at kickoff | one merged change, or one recorded result |
Table: The two levels the team writes every sprint

The journal tracks stories because the architect's view of the program is stories, epics and tomes; the hours and
the one-day steps belong to the team ([[om-where]]). GitLab holds both, because the tasks are where the merge
requests are.

=== A story in systems-engineering words {#stories-invest}

Software teams test a story against the acronym INVEST. Translated into the words of a systems engineer, the six
letters are six questions you already ask of a work package:

|| Letter | Software word | The question in SE words | A Tidewater "no" ||
| I | Independent | Can this be done without waiting for another open item? | "Close the Stations TODOs" while the layout decision is still open |
| N | Negotiable | Is the *outcome* fixed and the method left to the person doing it? | "Use sed to replace the level TODO lines" |
| V | Valuable | Would the program office care if it were done? | "Reformat Interfaces.sysml" |
| E | Estimable | Do we know enough to size it, even roughly? | "Fix whatever drop 2 breaks" before drop 2 arrives |
| S | Small | Does it fit in one sprint with room to spare? | "Port the Stations package" (25 members, 18 TODO lines) |
| T | Testable | Could someone who did not do it check that it is done? | "Improve the trace" |
Table: INVEST, translated

The last one is the one that matters most and the one engineers find hardest. "Improve the trace" is a direction,
not a result. "No requirement in the Stations segment without a verify, checked by the trace job" is a result.

=== Writing the story issue {#stories-issue}

Every story is a GitLab issue made from the Story template (`.gitlab/issue_templates/Story.md`,
[[templates-story]]). The template has four parts, and each one answers a question the program office will ask.

**The title: a verb and an object.** "Close the Stations level tags." "Grade our requirements with the recipe."
"Data exchange table for the program review." Not "Stations," not "Recipe work," not "Support the review." A title
with a verb can be done; a title that is a noun is a topic.

**Why: one or two sentences, for the program.** Who gains what. "The part tree shows every Stations element at its
level, so the management site's tree view is readable and the outline check can count them." If you cannot write
this line, the story is probably platform work disguised as engineering, or it is not worth doing this sprint.

**What done looks like: a checklist someone else can tick.** This is the acceptance criteria. Each line must be
checkable by a person who did not do the work. The next section is about writing these lines so that a machine
ticks most of them.

**Tasks: one line per task issue, each under five points.** The tasks are written at kickoff by the people who will
do them, not by Jim the week before.

Here are three of Tidewater's real stories from the first twelve weeks, as they were filed, with their acceptance
criteria (these come straight from `sim/tidewater-plan.pl`, the plan the simulation runs):

|| Story (sprint) | Done looks like ||
| Laptops ready: Git for Windows, Vim, the kit (S1) | `kickoff.pl doctor` passes on all three laptops |
| Close the Network TODO lines (S3) | No TODO lines in `Network.sysml`; the pipeline is green |
| The trace job becomes a gate (S5) | The trace job fails an MR that adds an orphan, an unverified requirement or drift |
Table: Three stories and their acceptance criteria

Every one of those lines can be checked by running something. None of them says "reviewed by Jim" or "looks good,"
because the review is part of every merge request already ([[reviewing]]).

=== Acceptance criteria as pipeline checks {#stories-criteria}

The habit that changed the team's plannings most was a simple one: write each acceptance criterion as something a
job in the pipeline reports. Then "done" is not an opinion, and nobody has to remember to check.

|| Instead of | Write | Who checks it ||
| Stations is cleaned up | No `// TODO` lines in `model/physical/stations/` | the `todo` job's count for that folder |
| Requirements are traced | The trace job shows no new orphan, unverified or drifted requirement | the `trace` job (a ratchet from sprint 5) |
| The model is correct | Lint 0 errors 0 warnings; the grammar check passes | the `lint` and `grammar` jobs |
| The documents are updated | `docs/` regenerated; the docs job is green | the `docs` job |
| The spider web is smaller | One package dependency cycle fewer in the package view | `model.pl draw pkg` ([[diagrams]]) |
| Requirements are better written | The recipe report shows no finding for the Stations requirements | the `recipe` job ([[recipe]]) |
| Every Stations requirement is verified | The VCRM shows 100% verify coverage for Stations | the `vcrm` job |
Table: Acceptance criteria a machine can check

Not every criterion can be a job. "The program office has seen the site" is a meeting. "DR-1 to DR-4 are sent to
the prime through the contract channel" is a transmittal record. Those are fine: write them as a fact that anyone can
confirm from a record ("the review notes list the program office attendees"; "the DR issues carry the transmittal
date"). The rule is not "only machine checks"; the rule is "no opinions."

::: tip Write the criteria before the estimate
Planning poker ([[ceremonies-poker]]) goes faster when the criteria are already on the card. Half of Tidewater's
estimate disagreements in sprint 1 were really disagreements about what done meant. In the poker for "Convert drop 1
and assess it" Bob voted 8 and Jim voted 3; once the criteria were written ("both converter reports attached, the
TODO inventory by package on the wiki, the backlog estimated"), the second round was 3, 3, 3.
:::

=== Writing the task issues {#stories-tasks}

A task is the smallest piece anyone tracks: at most five points, done by one person, ending in one merged change
or one recorded result. Tasks are GitLab issues of type `task`, made from the Task template, with three things the
story does not have:

- **"Part of #N"** on the first line of the description, naming the story, and a `/relate #N` quick action, so the
  story's page lists its tasks.
- **An estimate** with `/estimate 2d`. The team uses days because a point is an ideal day ([[om-points]]).
- **The merge request closes the task**, not the story. The MR description says `Closes #42` for the task; the story
  closes when its last task does (Jim closes it at the review if nobody did).

Tasks for model work fall into a few recognizable kinds. Knowing them makes kickoff faster:

|| Kind | Example from Tidewater | Typical size | Ends in ||
| Close TODO lines | Level tags in Network | 1 to 3 | an MR that removes TODO lines |
| Hand-write a construct | Write the BuoyModes state def; exhibit it on MooredBuoy | 2 to 4 | an MR with new SysML v2 text |
| Pipeline job | Add the trace job (report only) and the secrets job | 1 to 3 | an MR to `.gitlab-ci.yml` and `ci/job.pl` |
| Prime drop step | Import drop 1 onto vendor/prime | 1 to 2 | the vendor commit and the merge MR |
| Decision | Decide the layered layout; the layout map | 1 | an ADR in `docs/adr/` |
| Record or report | Open DR-1 to DR-4 | 1 to 2 | issues sent to the prime |
| Meeting with an outcome | Agree the delivery interface with the prime | 0.5 to 1 | notes and a decision on the wiki |
| Learning | Practice lessons 10-11; the survival kit, git and Vim chapters | 0.5 to 2 | a lesson checked as done |
Table: The kinds of task in model work

If a task does not end in one of those things, it is not a task yet. "Look at the Stations package" ends in nothing.
"Write the TODO inventory by package" ends in a wiki page.

=== Try it: a story and its first task {#stories-try}

Practice lesson 11's sandbox holds an offline GitLab with the Tidewater project and its templates. Make a story from
the Story template and a task under it, then look at both. First, step into the clone and load the lesson's
environment (it defines `gl` as a short name for `gitlab.pl`):

```sh run sandbox=practice-11 expect="#2 Close the Stations level tags"
. ./env.sh > /dev/null
cd work
gl issue new "Close the Stations level tags" --template Story --label type::story --milestone "Sprint 1"
```

The story is issue #2 (the lesson already filed #1). Now the task, with its link and estimate given as quick
actions in a note -- the same text you would type in the comment box on the web page (the block loads the
environment again with `. ../env.sh` only because the book's test runs each block in a fresh shell; in your terminal
once is enough):

```sh run cwd=work expect="changed time estimate to 2d"
. ../env.sh > /dev/null
gl issue new "Level tags in Stations" --type task --label type::task --milestone "Sprint 1"
gl issue note 3 "Part of #2
/estimate 2d
/relate #2"
gl issue show 3
```

The last command shows the task with the two system notes the quick actions left behind ("changed time estimate to
2d", "marked this issue as related to #2"). Open the story with `gl issue show 2` and fill in its "Why" and "What
done looks like" from the template's comments; in the real GitLab you would do that in the web editor or, as the
team did, in Vim ([[vim-markdown]]).

=== Splitting patterns for model work {#stories-splitting}

[[om-splitting]] gives the three rules (split by result, pull out the waiting, smallest useful thing first). Model
work has its own natural seams, and these are the ones the team used most:

1. **By package.** The converter writes one file per package, and packages are owned by people
   ([[repo-layout]]). "Close the structure TODOs" became one story per segment folder: Network, Stations,
   DataCenter, Operations.
2. **By TODO kind.** The converter's TODO lines name their kind (stereotype, state machine, interaction, include,
   fork, constraint). Level tags are quick and mechanical; a state machine is a design task. Never put both in one
   story: the quick ones finish and the hard one carries over, and the whole story shows as late.
3. **By requirement group.** For trace and verification work, split by the DOORS module or the system requirement
   heading: "Stations verification cases," "Data center verification cases."
4. **By interface.** An interface touches two owners. One story per interface keeps the reviews short and the
   right people on them.
5. **By drop change.** When a prime drop arrives, its change requests (NWM-CR-... numbers in the drop's change
   list) are natural stories: one per significant change, each with its disposition ([[prime-changes]]).
6. **Investigation apart from result.** "Find out why drop 3's Storm state does not appear in v2" is a one-day
   spike; "Add the Storm state by hand" is the task that follows.

::: sidebar The Stations story that would not fit
Bob's first try at the Stations work was one story of 9 points: level tags, procured parts, the power system's
parametric and the state machine. Planning poker came back 5, 8, 13. Splitting it by TODO kind gave four
stories: "Close the Stations level tags" (5, sprint 4, with its last two points carried into sprint 5), "Procured parts as metadata" (sprint 5, together with the DataCenter tags), the parametric
(later, in the behavior and analysis epic), and "BuoyModes state machine by hand" (3, sprint 6). The level tags
were the first visible dent in the TODO burn-down that management saw.
:::

=== Sizing: points, poker and the first sprints {#stories-sizing}

Sizes come from planning poker ([[ceremonies-poker]]): each person votes a number of points, the outliers explain
themselves, and the team votes again. A few things the team learned about sizing model work:

- **The first time is always bigger.** The first package with level tags took Bob three points; the third took one.
  Size the first of a kind generously and expect the next ones to shrink. Velocity in the first two sprints is not a
  forecast of anything.
- **Waiting is not effort.** A task blocked for three days on the enterprise GitLab administrators is not a
  five-point task. Size the effort, mark the wait (`/label ~workflow::blocked`), and say it at stand-up as an ask.
- **Half points are fine, quarter points are not.** A half-day task exists (send the DR, hold the meeting). Anything
  smaller is part of another task.
- **Engineering, platform and learning are labelled, not hidden.** Every story carries `work::engineering`,
  `work::platform` or `work::learning`. The scorecard shows the mix sprint by sprint, so management can see the
  platform work shrinking and the engineering growing.

=== The learning budget {#stories-learning}

No line in the first plan Jim drafted said "learn git." The first plan held 26 points against a capacity of about
19, and every point was work. The team would have learned git anyway -- at night, at home, or by failing in front
of each other in merge requests.

The plan that ran instead budgets the learning as stories, labelled `work::learning`, with real points:

|| Sprint | Learning share | What it covers ||
| 1 | {{value: docs/tidewater/work-split.json sprints.1.learning}}% | practice lessons 10 and 11; the survival kit, git, Vim and operating-model chapters |
| 2 | {{value: docs/tidewater/work-split.json sprints.2.learning}}% | practice lessons 12 and 13; Vim for models; the diagrams chapter; the SysML v2 workbook, first half |
| 3 and 4 | {{value: docs/tidewater/work-split.json sprints.3.learning}}% and {{value: docs/tidewater/work-split.json sprints.4.learning}}% | the workbook, second half; talking about progress; reviewing a model change |
| 5 and 6 | {{value: docs/tidewater/work-split.json sprints.5.learning}}% and {{value: docs/tidewater/work-split.json sprints.6.learning}}% | the requirement recipe; memos and briefings |
Table: Tidewater's learning budget (share of each sprint's task points done, [[om-done]])

Sprint 1's learning story is a good model to copy. Its title is a verb and an object ("Learn the tools: survival
kit, git, Vim, the operating model"); its done is checkable ("each of us finished practice lessons 10 and 11"; "each
of us read the primer's Part II chapters for our role"); and its tasks are per person, sized for that person (Jim
1, Bob 1.5, Tom 1.5), because the three do not start from the same place.

::: objection "Management will see 20% of the sprint spent on training and cut it."
Answer: Management sees it either way. Unbudgeted learning shows up as missed commitments, carried-over stories and
three tired engineers. Budgeted learning shows up as a line that shrinks every sprint while the engineering line
grows -- which is exactly the picture Jim showed at the sprint 6 briefing ([[briefing]]). A visible, shrinking cost
is a much easier conversation than an invisible one.
:::

The same goes for the other setup work a new team forgets: laptops (one point each, plus the days waiting on the
software request), the request for enablers memo, the conventions documents, the first demo rehearsal. If it takes
time, it is a task. If it is a task, it is on the board.

=== The Definition of Ready, applied {#stories-ready}

A story is ready to bring into a sprint when the team can start it on Monday without a meeting. Tidewater's
Definition of Ready is short ([[om-dor-dod]]); in practice the refinement session checks five things for every
candidate story:

::: checklist Ready for a sprint
[ ] The title is a verb and an object.
[ ] "Why" is written, in the program's words.
[ ] Every acceptance criterion is checkable by a job or a record.
[ ] Nothing it needs is waiting outside the team, or the waiting is its own task.
[ ] It has been through planning poker and fits the sprint with room to spare.
:::

Stories that pass get the `workflow::ready` label. Stories that do not stay in the backlog, and the conversation
about why is usually the most useful part of refinement.

=== A worked example: from an epic to its tasks {#stories-worked}

Here is one epic taken all the way down, as the team did it over sprints 2 to 6. The epic is E6, *Structure: close
the structure TODOs*. Its outcome, from the epic issue: every structural TODO line in the converted model closed, the
level tags in place, the clutter of the document port removed -- so the part tree and the outline check are true.

**The stories.** At the sprint 2 refinement Jim and Bob listed what "every structural TODO closed" meant, package by
package and TODO kind by TODO kind, and wrote one story per result:

|| Sprint | Story | Owner | Why it is its own story ||
| 2 | Level conventions for the part tree | Bob | everything else needs the level tags to exist first |
| 3 | Close the Network TODO lines | Bob | one package, two kinds of TODO (levels, an include) |
| 4 | Close the Stations level tags | Bob | the biggest package; levels only |
| 4 | Units and value types | Bob | a different kind of TODO (units), a different file |
| 4 | Break the Network to Operations cycle | Tom | a spider-web result, judged by the package view |
| 5 | Procured parts as metadata; DataCenter level tags | Bob | the second TODO kind in Stations, plus a small package |
| 5 | Untangle the Legacy Import package | Tom | clutter removal, with a DR to the prime |
| 6 | Level tags in Operations | Tom | the last package with level TODOs |
Table: Epic E6 as stories

No story mixes a quick kind of TODO with a slow one; no story spans two packages unless one of them is tiny; each has
an owner; and the dependency (conventions first) is a story of its own at the start.

**One story, written out.** Here is "Close the Network TODO lines" as Bob filed it from the Story template:

```md
## Why

The Network package is the top of the part tree. With its level tags and the use case include in place, the tree view
on the model site starts at the right level, and the outline check counts Network correctly.

## What "done" looks like

- [ ] No `// TODO` lines in `model/physical/network/Network.sysml` (the todo job's per-file count is 0)
- [ ] The pipeline is green on the MR (grammar, lint 0/0, outline, docs current)
- [ ] The trace job shows no new orphan, unverified or drifted requirement

## Tasks

| Task | Points | Who |
|---|---|---|
| #59 Level tags in Network | 1 | @bob |
| #60 The include use case in ReviewObservations | 2 | @bob |
```

**The tasks.** Each task issue says "Part of" the story, carries its estimate, and is closed by one merge request.
The include task was two points, not one, because nobody had written a SysML v2 `include use case` before; Bob's
merge request description says so, and links the workbook section Bob used ([[workbook]]). The next include, in a
later sprint, took half a day.

=== The story in the journal {#stories-journal}

The kit's journal keeps one line per story, not per task ([[journal]]). When a story is committed at kickoff, the
stand-up file for that day gets two lines; when its last task closes, one more:

```text
new TW-51 3 "Close the Network TODO lines" p:2 e:"E6 Structure" t:"T2 Port" o:Bob
commit TW-51 Bob
...
done TW-51
```

The id is TW- and the GitLab story issue number, so anyone can go from the journal to the issue and back. Points in
the journal are the story's total (the sum of its tasks); the journal is what produces the sprint's committed, done
and carried-over numbers, the velocity, and the quad ([[ceremonies-weekly]]).

=== Common mistakes, and their fixes {#stories-mistakes}

Every one of these happened on Tidewater in the first six sprints:

|| Mistake | What it looked like | Fix ||
| A topic, not a result | "Stations" as a story title | a verb and an object: "Close the Stations level tags" |
| An activity, not a result | "Analyze the trace gaps" | name what the analysis produces: "Write up the drift and the trace gaps" |
| Criteria as opinions | "Reviewed and approved by Jim" | the review happens on every MR anyway; write what the review is checking for |
| Hidden waiting | "Runner online" sized at 4 points, 3 of them waiting on the administrators | size the effort; make the wait its own task and label it blocked |
| Two kinds in one story | level tags and the state machine in one Stations story | split by TODO kind ([[stories-splitting]]) |
| A task over five points | "Port the requirements" at 8 | it is a story; split it into tasks |
| Learning as overtime | no learning stories in the first plan | budget it ([[stories-learning]]) |
| A story nobody owns | "Team: close the TODOs" | one owner per story; tasks can go to others |
Table: Mistakes in the first six sprints

=== When a story carries over {#stories-carry}

A story that is not done at the end of the sprint is **carried over**: its remaining points move to the next sprint
in the journal (`carry TW-67`: the Stations story in sprint 4, [[sprint-4]]), and the story issue moves to the next milestone. Carryover is not failure; it is
information. The question at the retrospective is always *why*:

- **It was too big.** Split the rest before the next kickoff; do not carry the same oversized story twice.
- **It waited on someone outside the team.** Was the waiting its own task? If not, next time it will be.
- **Something more urgent came in.** Was it a real interrupt? The journal records unplanned work separately, so the
  pattern is visible over sprints.

In the first six sprints the plan carried over one story on purpose: "BuoyModes state machine by hand," started in
sprint 6 and finished in sprint 7. Bob knew at kickoff that it would not finish, because the first hand-written state
machine was a learning task as much as a modeling one. Writing that down at kickoff turned a surprise into a plan.

For a new port, the kit has a generic starting backlog, `tools/gitlab/templates/backlog.csv`, derived from
Tidewater's plan with the Tidewater-specific words taken out: one row per task, with its tome, epic and story, its
size, the role that usually does it, a sprint hint and an acceptance criterion. The first rows:

Load it into a spreadsheet, delete what does not apply, rename the epics for your system, and file the first two

=== Applying this to your project {#stories-apply}

- **Copy:** the Story and Task templates, the habit of acceptance criteria as pipeline checks, the three work labels,
  and a learning budget that starts near a fifth of capacity and falls.
- **Adapt:** the task kinds to your own model's TODO kinds and drop rhythm; the size limits to your sprint length.
- **Decide locally:** who writes stories (Tidewater: the owner, at refinement), whether tasks live only in GitLab
  (Tidewater: yes), and how much learning time your management will accept on paper -- then ask for it in the
  request for enablers ([[memos]]).

:::
