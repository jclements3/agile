== Talking about progress: helps for engineers who would rather not {#talking}

::: bridge
You know: briefing a chief from a slide deck you rehearsed the night before.

Here it is called: talking about progress: the stand-up line, the demo and the weekly note.

What is new: one short sentence a day, outcome first, with one number, and tools that draft it for you.
:::

::: survival
What you have: a stand-up every morning ([[ceremonies]]), a demo every second Friday, a weekly note to management,
and a GitLab project that already knows almost everything you did.

What you need: words. Short ones, in an order management can follow, said out loud by people who have spent a
career letting the documents speak for them. This chapter gives you a pattern, a ladder, a stakeholder map, a few
scripts for bad news, and a tool that drafts the first version of every sentence from GitLab so you only have to
correct it.
:::

=== Why engineers go quiet {#talking-quiet}

The first two weeks of Tidewater stand-ups produced statuses like these, copied from the meeting chat:

```text
Y fixed lint warnings on the connector ends in PowerSystem T more of the same B none
Y rebased my branch after the vendor merge T trace job B waiting on the runner
Y stuff on TW-4 T TW-4 B none
```

Each one is true. None of them tells the program manager, who started coming to the sprint reviews in sprint 2, whether
the port is on schedule. And in the sprint 2 demo, Tom spent eleven minutes showing the pipeline file line by line, while the
program manager checked e-mail and the deputy program manager said, at the end, "I did not follow the demo. What did
we get for the two weeks?" Nobody answered for a long moment.

It is worth understanding why this happens, because the fix is different for each cause.

- **The precision instinct.** Systems engineers are trained not to claim more than they can show. "On track" feels
  like a claim; "fixed lint warnings on the connector ends" feels like a fact. So the fact is said and the claim is
  left for the listener to infer, which the listener cannot do.
- **Fear of being wrong in front of managers.** For thirty years the team's statements to management were reviewed,
  edited and signed before anyone said them. A live sentence with a number in it, said without a document behind it,
  feels exposed.
- **Not knowing what the audience needs.** The program manager needs a different sentence from the ISSM, and both
  need a different one from Bob. Engineers default to the sentence *they* would want to hear: the mechanism.
- **Talking in mechanism.** Mechanism is what the engineer spent the day on, so it is what is in their head. "Rebased
  my branch" is the most recent memory, not the most important news.
- **No practice.** Nobody on the team had ever rehearsed a status. Documents were drafted, reviewed and redlined;
  spoken updates were improvised. Improvisation under mild stress produces mechanism or silence.

None of these is a character flaw, and none is fixed by telling people to "communicate better". Each is fixed by a
structure: a pattern that puts the claim first, a number that backs it, a tool that drafts it, and a rehearsal.

> I wasn't hiding anything. I just didn't know which of the forty things I did was the one they wanted.
> -- Bob, sprint 2 retrospective

=== The pattern: outcome, evidence, next or ask {#talking-pattern}

Every status the team says to anyone outside the team follows one shape:

1. **Outcome**: what is now true that was not true before, in the listener's words.
2. **Evidence**: one number that proves it.
3. **Next step or ask**: what happens next, by when; or what you need, from whom, by when.

The mechanism, the *how*, is kept in reserve. If someone asks, you have it; if nobody asks, nobody needed it.

|| Instead of | Say ||
| "Y fixed lint warnings on the connector ends in PowerSystem" | "Y TW-12 Stations structure done: nine TODO lines closed, forty-three left. T TW-15 decide who owns the observation record, by Thursday. B none" |
| "B waiting on the runner" | "B TW-3 checks can't run on every change until IT approves the runner machine. Need: Jim to escalate today" |
| "T rebasing my branch after the vendor merge" | "T TW-31 the prime's January update: a list of what it changes for us by Wednesday" |
| "Y trace job is red" | "Y TW-22 the check found two requirements nothing satisfies. They're the prime's to fix; the report goes to them Friday" |
| "Refactoring the library package" | "Cleaning up shared definitions so the next change takes one edit instead of ten" |
Table: Stand-up statuses, before and after

Notice three things about the right-hand column. Every line still fits the kit's terse format (Y, T and B, each
naming a story), so the lint in [[ceremonies-standup]] still passes. Every line has exactly one number; two numbers
make the listener choose, and they choose wrong. And every blocker is an *ask with a name and a day*, because
management can act on "Jim to escalate today" and cannot act on "waiting".

Three sentence shapes cover most days. Copy them onto a card ([[qrg-talking]]) and keep it beside the keyboard:

- "**[X] of [Y] [things] are [done / covered / passing], up from [Z].**" Forty-three of fifty-two TODO lines left,
  down from fifty-two.
- "**We caught [problem] at [early stage] instead of [late stage].**" The check caught a broken interface at
  merge, the same day, instead of at integration.
- "**Blocked by [thing]. Need [specific help] from [person] by [day].**"

::: tip The "so what?" test
Read your sentence and ask "so what?" as the program manager would. If the honest answer is "so nothing, I just did
it", the sentence is mechanism: replace it with the outcome it served, or leave it out. If the answer is "so we're
one step closer", say how close, with the number.
:::

=== The ladder: one sentence, thirty seconds, five minutes {#talking-ladder}

Management will give you one of three slots, and you rarely know in advance which. Prepare all three for anything
you will talk about this week.

|| Rung | Length | What it holds | When you use it ||
| One sentence | about 10 seconds | the outcome and the number | the hallway, the elevator, the start of every longer version |
| Thirty seconds | three or four sentences | outcome, number, what it means for the next milestone, the ask | the stand-up, a question in a meeting, the opening of the demo |
| Five minutes | the thirty seconds plus one piece of evidence shown and one before-and-after | the demo, a briefing slot | the sprint review |
Table: The ladder

Write the one-sentence version first. If you cannot, you do not yet know what the outcome is, and no amount of
detail in the five-minute version will hide that. Then say each rung out loud, once, to a colleague or to the wall.
Saying it is different from reading it: you will hear the word you trip over, and that word is usually jargon.

Here is Tidewater's sprint 4 review, on all three rungs, as Tom (the narrator that sprint) rehearsed it on Day 9:

> One sentence: The model site management asked for is live, and the check now shows only
> {{value: docs/tidewater/sprints/04/scorecard.json 1_todo_lines}} of the {{value: docs/tidewater/sprints/02/scorecard.json 1_todo_lines}} conversion items left.
>
> Thirty seconds: The model site is live and rebuilt every night, so anyone on the program can see the current model
> without asking us. The conversion is more than half done, and the checks found four gaps in the prime's
> requirements that we have reported to them. For the January drop, that means we can tell you what it changes for
> us within a day. We need the second runner approved by the 15th.
> -- Tom, sprint 4 review, rehearsed version

The five-minute version was the thirty seconds, then the live site on the screen, then one before-and-after, then the
ask again. Nothing else.

=== Tools that draft the words {#talking-tools}

Writing the outcome-first sentence is the hard part, and it is hardest at 8:55 in the morning. Most of what it needs
is already in GitLab: which tasks you closed, which merge requests merged, which issue is blocked and why. The kit's
`talk.pl` reads that and drafts the sentence. It never posts anything; it prints a draft for you to correct.

`talk.pl` has four commands:

|| Command | Drafts | From ||
| `standup --as USER` | one Y/T/B line, outcome first, one number, a blocker as an ask | your closed tasks and merged merge requests since the last working day; the task in progress; issues labelled `workflow::blocked` |
| `review --sprint N` | a demo script: the question, the four headline numbers, what to show, a before-and-after, a safe live change, the ask, and the ladder | the sprint's milestone, issues, merge requests, pipelines and their metrics |
| `weekly` | a plain-language note for management: a headline that is a conclusion, three numbers, risks, asks | the same, for the week |
| `translate FILE` | a list of jargon in your text with plain alternatives, and the sentences that fail the "so what?" test | your draft |
Table: talk.pl

The kit also has `daily.pl propose`, which drafts each person's status from yesterday's answers in the journal (a
"+1" in the chat accepts it). `propose` is good for days when nothing changed; `talk.pl` is good for days when
something did, because it reads GitLab, where the change is recorded.

==== Try it on a practice project

The examples in this section run against the offline GitLab of practice lesson 11 (see [[git-practice]]). First,
give it a small sprint to talk about: a story with three tasks for Bob, a blocked task for Tom with the reason in a
comment, and two of Bob's tasks closed on Wednesday. Each `gl` command is an ordinary GitLab action; `--now` stamps it
with a date, so the example reads the same whatever day you run it. Run everything in this section from the
sandbox's `work` folder (`cd work` first).

```sh run sandbox=practice-11 cwd=work timeout=300 expect="#4 closed"
. ../env.sh > /dev/null
at() { d=$1; shift; gl --now "$d" "$@"; }
at 2026-10-05T09:00 --as jim issue new "Close the Stations level tags" --type issue --label type::story --milestone "Sprint 1" --assign bob
at 2026-10-05T09:10 --as jim issue new "Level tags in StationSegment and ObservingStation" --type task --label type::task --milestone "Sprint 1" --assign bob --description "Part of #2"
at 2026-10-05T09:11 --as jim issue new "Level tags in the station parts" --type task --label type::task --milestone "Sprint 1" --assign bob --description "Part of #2"
at 2026-10-05T09:12 --as jim issue new "Decide who owns ObservationRecord" --type task --label type::task --label workflow::in-progress --milestone "Sprint 1" --assign bob --description "Part of #2"
at 2026-10-05T09:20 --as jim issue new "Register the second runner" --type task --label type::task --label workflow::blocked --milestone "Sprint 1" --assign tom
at 2026-10-06T15:00 --as tom issue note 6 "Blocked by: IT approval of the runner machine -- need: Jim to escalate to the IT lead today"
at 2026-10-07T16:00 --as bob issue close 3
at 2026-10-07T16:30 --as bob issue close 4
```

You are now in the sandbox's `work` folder; the examples below stay there. (Each one sources `../env.sh` again so it also works in a new window.) Now draft Thursday's stand-up for Bob. `--prefix TW-` makes the story ids look like the journal's; `--why` adds the
line that says where each part of the draft came from.

```sh run expect="^Y TW-2 Close the Stations level tags: 2 of 3 tasks done\..* T TW-2 decide who owns ObservationRecord"
. ../env.sh > /dev/null
perl $KIT/tools/gitlab/talk.pl standup --as bob --prefix TW- --date 2026-10-08 --why
```

```text
Y TW-2 Close the Stations level tags: 2 of 3 tasks done. Reviewed !1. T TW-2 decide who owns ObservationRecord, by Oct 16. B none
# draft for bob on Thu Oct 8 (since Wed Oct 7): edit it, then post it yourself
# from: #3 closed, #4 closed, reviewed !1, today: #5 (story #2)
# lint: ok
```

The draft already has the shape: the outcome (the story's tasks), one number (two of three), the next step with a
day (the end of the sprint, which is a default worth correcting to "by Thursday" if you know better), and a lint
check, so the line will not bounce in the meeting. What Bob actually posted that morning was:

```text
Y TW-2 Stations level tags: 2 of 3 done, the part tree now shows every station's level. T TW-2 decide who owns ObservationRecord, by Thu. B none
```

Bob kept the number, added what it means ("the part tree now shows every station's level"), dropped the review of
!1 (true, but not what the team needed to hear that morning), and tightened the date.
That is the intended use: the tool does the remembering, the engineer does the meaning.

Tom's draft turns the blocked task into an ask, using the reason Tom wrote in the issue's comment:

```sh run expect="B TW-6 register the second runner blocked: IT approval of the runner machine -- need: Jim to escalate"
. ../env.sh > /dev/null
perl $KIT/tools/gitlab/talk.pl standup --as tom --prefix TW- --date 2026-10-08
```

```text
Y none T none B TW-6 register the second runner blocked: IT approval of the runner machine -- need: Jim to escalate to the IT lead today
```

"Y none T none" is honest, and it is also the moment to say something out loud: what *did* Tom do on Wednesday?
GitLab only knows what was recorded. If you spent the day on a meeting with the ISSM, the tool cannot know it, and
you should write it in.

::: warning Read every number before you say it
A draft is a draft. In this practice project every change was made by a script in the same second, so `talk.pl`'s
timing figures come out as zero minutes; on a real project they are real. On every project, check each number against
what you know before you say it in a meeting. The tool counts what GitLab recorded; you know what actually happened.
:::

==== The weekly note

On Friday, the weekly note drafts the headline and the asks. Add an open discrepancy report so there is something to
count, and a small file of document-era numbers (the "baseline") so the review can make its before-and-after:

```sh run expect="Weekly note, week of Oct 5"
. ../env.sh > /dev/null
gl --now 2026-10-08T10:00 --as jim issue new "DR-1: TW-SYS-006 text differs between DOORS and the model" --label type::discrepancy --label owner::prime --milestone "Sprint 1" > /dev/null
printf 'review_hours = 360 | a document review cycle: 3 weeks of comments on a Word file\nlead_hours = 552 | a change through the CCB: 23 working days\n' > ../baseline.txt
perl $KIT/tools/gitlab/talk.pl weekly --date 2026-10-09 --baseline ../baseline.txt
```

```text
Weekly note, week of Oct 5 -- On track

On track: 0 stories finished this week.

The numbers:
- 0 stories finished this week (2 changes accepted into the model)
- 1 open discrepancy reports and prime changes

Risks:
- #6 Register the second runner is blocked: IT approval of the runner machine.

Asks:
- Jim to escalate to the IT lead today (#6)

(The weekly quad -- daily.pl quad -- has the detail; this note is the headline.)
```

Two edits make this sendable. "On track: 0 stories finished" will alarm a reader in week one of a sprint, so Jim adds
the context the tool does not have ("two of three tasks in the first story are done; stories finish at the end of the
sprint"). And the risk deserves a date: "if the runner is not approved by the 15th, changes wait for one machine".
The note pairs with the kit's weekly quad ([[ceremonies-weekly]]), which carries the detail.

==== The review script

Before each sprint review, the person who will narrate drafts the demo script:

```sh run expect="THE SAME FOUR NUMBERS AS EVERY REVIEW"
. ../env.sh > /dev/null
perl $KIT/tools/gitlab/talk.pl review --sprint 1 --baseline ../baseline.txt
```

```text
Sprint 1 review -- demo script (draft: edit before the meeting)

1. THE QUESTION THIS SPRINT ANSWERED
"What did Sprint 1 change for the model, and how do we know?"
Answer in one line: 0 of 1 stories done.

2. THE SAME FOUR NUMBERS AS EVERY REVIEW
- TODO lines left: n/a (no todo metrics yet)
- Verify coverage: n/a (no trace or VCRM metrics yet)
- Open discrepancy reports and prime changes: 1 (up 1 from 0)
- Blocked items: 1
...
7. THE ASK
- Need: Jim to escalate to the IT lead today (#6 Register the second runner is blocked: IT approval of the runner machine)

30-SECOND VERSION
Sprint 1: 0 of 1 stories done. We need help with 1 blocked item.

1-SENTENCE VERSION
Sprint 1 moved the model forward by 0 stories.
```

The script has seven parts, which are the seven moves of a demo in [[talking-demo]]. On the practice project the
numbers are thin: "n/a" for TODO lines and verify coverage, because practice lesson 11's pipeline has no TODO or trace
job. On the Tidewater project those come from the pipeline's metrics, and Part III shows the real drafts for every
sprint review next to what the team actually said.

==== Translating your own draft

The fourth command is for text you wrote yourself: a status, a demo opening, a paragraph for the weekly note. It does
*not* rewrite your sentences. It lists the jargon it found with a plain alternative and a hint, and it flags the
sentences that fail the "so what?" test, with a fill-in suggestion in the outcome, evidence, ask shape.

```sh run sandbox=scratch expect="So what\? -- sentences to rework"
printf 'Rebased my feature branch and fixed the lint errors so the pipeline is green.\nWorking on the VCRM.\n' > draft.txt
perl $KIT/tools/gitlab/talk.pl translate draft.txt
```

```text
Jargon to replace:
  "the pipeline is green" -> all automated checks passed. Hint: say what that proves (e.g. "no grammar or lint errors").
  "lint errors" -> errors the automated checks found. Hint: say how many, and that they were caught before review.
  "Rebased" -> brought my change up to date. Hint: say what it achieved, or leave it out: nobody needs to hear it.
  "feature branch" -> private working copy of the model. Hint: usually leave it out: say what the change does.
  "VCRM" -> verification matrix. Hint: say how many requirements are verified.

So what? -- sentences to rework:
  1. "Rebased my feature branch and fixed the lint errors so the pipeline is green." -- no number
     SUGGESTION: Done: [what is now true] ([one number: how many, how much]). Next: [outcome, by a day]. Need: [an ask, or none].
  2. "Working on the VCRM." -- no number; no outcome (done, caught, ready, decided ... or an ask)
     SUGGESTION: Done: [what is now true -- topic: verification matrix] ([one number: how many, how much]). Next: [outcome, by a day]. Need: [an ask, or none].
```

The rework of those two sentences, by Tom, became: "Done: my change to the power system is checked and accepted, with
no errors found. Next: the verification matrix shows which requirements are verified, by Friday. Need: none." Longer
than the original, and something a manager can repeat to *their* manager, which is the real test.

You can give `translate` your own glossary with `--glossary FILE` (lines `term = plain alternative | hint`), so the
team's words, the prime's acronyms and your program's jargon get the same treatment.

=== Know your audience: the Tidewater stakeholder map {#talking-audience}

The same week sounds different to different people. Before a meeting, ask who is in the room and what one number each
of them wants. The Tidewater team kept this table on the wiki and updated it after every review:

|| Who | Cares about | The one number they want | Say it as ||
| Program manager | Is the money producing measurable results? Will the December review go well? | TODO lines left, and the trend | "{{value: docs/tidewater/sprints/04/scorecard.json 1_todo_lines}} of {{value: docs/tidewater/sprints/02/scorecard.json 1_todo_lines}} left after sprint 4; about two sprints to go at this pace." |
| Deputy program manager (chairs the CCB) | Can a change be traced from request to approval to implementation? | changes accepted with a green pipeline and a code-owner approval | "Every change this quarter is traceable from its issue to its merge." |
| ISSM | Is the toolchain approved, and is anything leaking? | open security findings, last secret-detection result | "Secret detection ran on every change; zero findings since the runner was hardened." |
| The prime's modeler | Which of our discrepancy reports are open, and what do we need from the next drop? | open DRs, oldest first | "{{value: docs/tidewater/sprints/06/scorecard.json 6_drs.opened}} open, none closed yet; the oldest is the TW-SYS-006 wording, open since sprint 4." |
| The test organization | Which requirements have a verification case and a result? | verify coverage | "{{value: docs/tidewater/sprints/06/scorecard.json 5_trace.G}} requirements still have no verification case, and all of them are the prime's to close." |
| The team itself | Are we getting better at this? | the pulse survey, the share of stand-up lines with an outcome and a number | "Morale up from 1.9 in sprint 1; most statuses now have a number." |
Table: Who wants which number

The numbers in this table are Tidewater's at the end of sprint 4 or 6, from the evidence; in Part III every sprint chapter
shows them sprint by sprint.

=== Demos that land {#talking-demo}

The demo is where the quiet engineer is most exposed: a live screen, managers in the room, and no document to hide
behind. Tidewater's sprint 2 demo went badly (Tom's eleven minutes on the pipeline file). The sprint 4 demo, after one
retro change and a rehearsal, went well. The change was not "Tom should present better". It was a structure.

==== Three roles

|| Role | Does | Says ||
| Driver | runs the keyboard: opens the merge request, the site, the board | nothing, or "here" |
| Narrator | tells the story in the listener's words | the question, the outcome, the before-and-after, the meaning |
| Note-taker | writes down every question, comment and request | nothing during; reads them back at the end |
Table: Demo roles

Splitting driving from narrating is the single most useful change. An engineer who is typing cannot also be choosing
words for a manager; an engineer who is only talking can look at the audience. The note-taker turns the room's
reactions into issues afterwards, so nothing said in the demo is lost.

The team **rotates** the roles every sprint. In sprint 4 Bob drove and Tom narrated and closed with the ask, because Jim was on
leave that Friday (the meeting record, `docs/tidewater/sprints/04/meetings.csv`, lists Bob, Tom, the deputy program
manager and the program manager); in sprint 5 Tom drove and Bob narrated. Rotation means everyone speaks to management at least once a month, and nobody becomes
"the presenter" by default while the others stay quiet.

==== The Day 9 dry run

On Day 9 (Thursday of week 2), after refinement, the team spends thirty minutes running the demo once, out loud, with
the roles they will have on Friday. Nothing is polished; the point is to hear it. The dry run catches:

- The demo step that needs a password nobody has typed this sprint.
- The jargon the narrator trips over ("so the MR's pipeline, I mean the merged-results pipeline...").
- The number nobody can explain ("why did the TODO count go *up* on Tuesday?" Because the drop-2 preview added three).
- The ask nobody has written down.

==== The six moves

A demo script, whoever drafts it, has six moves. `talk.pl review` drafts them; you edit them.

1. **Open with the question this sprint answered.** "Can we show every station requirement has a verification? Yes,
   twenty-three of twenty-six. Here are the other three."
2. **Show the output, not the tool.** The model site, the verification matrix, a merge request going from red to
   green, the data exchange table. Keep the `.sysml` text and the pipeline file ready in case someone asks.
3. **One before-and-after, with a number.** "Finding where DOORS and the model disagree used to be done never, or
   once before a review. Now it takes two minutes, on every change."
4. **One safe live change.** On a scratch branch, never on `develop`: remove one satisfy link and open a draft merge
   request; in about four minutes the trace check names the requirement that lost its design. Cause and effect, live,
   lands better than any slide. Close the merge request afterwards.
5. **What it means for the next milestone.** "For the January drop, we'll know what it changes for us within a day."
6. **The ask, with a date.** "We need the deputy program manager's decision on the delivery agreement by Friday."

And close the loop: the note-taker reads back the questions, and each becomes an issue before the end of the day.

==== The same four numbers, every time

Management learns to read a trend only if it sees the same numbers, in the same order, every review. Tidewater's four:

1. **TODO lines left**: how much of the conversion is still to do.
2. **Verify coverage**: how many requirements have a verification case.
3. **Open discrepancy reports and prime changes**: what is waiting on the prime, or on us.
4. **Blocked items**: what needs management's help.

They open every review, every weekly note and every memo. A headline states the conclusion of the numbers ("Trace: 29
of 30 linked, green"), not the topic ("Trace analysis").

=== Bad news, said so it builds trust {#talking-bad-news}

Engineers dread bad news more than managers do. Managers expect it; what they cannot forgive is hearing it late.
These are the phrases the Tidewater team used, each tied to a real moment.

|| Situation | Say ||
| A check found a problem | "Found early. That's the system working. Here is the impact and the fix." |
| A number got worse because you can now see it | "It's not new, it's newly visible. The checks show twelve requirements without a test; here is the date each closes." |
| You are behind | "This is behind by a week because the ISSM review moved. To recover we need the review slot on the 6th, or we can drop the second runner from this sprint." |
| You do not know | "I don't know yet. I'll know by Thursday, after the impact report." |
| Something broke on your watch | "The develop pipeline was red for a day after my merge. It's green now; the cause was X; the check that would have caught it is in this sprint." |
Table: Bad-news scripts

Every row has the same skeleton: the fact, the cause in one clause, and what happens next with a date. None of them
apologizes at length, and none of them blames.

==== Jim models "I don't know yet"

The hardest of these for the team was "I don't know yet", because for thirty years not knowing was something you
fixed before the meeting. In sprint 3 the deputy program manager asked, in the review, whether the prime's January drop would
be "a big one". Jim did not know, and said so:

> I don't know yet. The prime's modeler said "a few sensor changes". I'll know by the Tuesday after it lands, because
> the delta report lists every change and what it touches in our model. I'll send you the count that day.
> -- Jim, sprint 3 review

Two things happened. The program manager wrote "Tue after drop: count" on a pad and moved on, which surprised the team.
And in the next stand-up, Bob said "I don't know yet, I'll know by tomorrow" about the ObservationRecord ownership
question, for the first time. The lead going first is what makes it safe for everyone else.

=== Measuring whether it is working {#talking-measure}

Like everything else in this book, the team measured its own talking. Each sprint, Jim counted the share of stand-up
lines that had both an outcome and a number. In sprint 1 it was low: most lines were mechanism. By sprint 6 most lines
had both. Part III gives the real counts per sprint ([[sprint-6]]), and the scorecard in Part IV includes it.

The pulse survey's question "I can explain our progress to management in one sentence" moved too. That question was
the one Jim watched most closely, because it is the one that tells you whether the engineers will speak up when the
lead is on leave.

=== Exercises {#talking-exercises}

::: exercise {#ex-talk-rewrite} Rewrite five statuses
Take your last five spoken or written statuses (or the three at the start of this chapter). Rewrite each in the
outcome, evidence, next-or-ask shape, keeping the Y/T/B format and a story id. Run them through `talk.pl translate` and
fix what it flags. Which one was hardest, and why?
:::

::: exercise {#ex-talk-ladder} Climb the ladder
Pick one thing your team finished this week. Write the one-sentence, thirty-second and five-minute versions. Say each
out loud to a colleague and ask them to repeat the one-sentence version back to you. If they can't, rewrite it.
:::

::: exercise {#ex-talk-map} Your stakeholder map
Make the table in [[talking-audience]] for your own program: every person who hears your status, what they care about,
the one number they want, and the sentence. Which of those numbers can your pipeline produce today?
:::

::: exercise {#ex-talk-draft} Draft from GitLab
In the practice-lesson-11 sandbox, close one more of Bob's tasks with a `--now` date, then draft Friday's stand-up with
`talk.pl standup`. Edit the draft so it says what the change *means*, not only what was closed.
:::

::: exercise {#ex-talk-demo} Run a dry run
Plan next sprint's demo with three roles and the six moves. Hold a thirty-minute dry run on Day 9. Write down every
place the narrator stumbled, and change the script, not the narrator.
:::

=== Applying this to your project {#talking-apply}

- **Copy**: the outcome, evidence, ask pattern; the ladder; the demo roles and their rotation; the Day 9 dry run;
  the four headline numbers; the bad-news skeleton (fact, cause, next with a date).
- **Adapt**: the stakeholder map (your program's people and their numbers); the four headline numbers (pick the ones
  that say whether *your* project is on track: for a new model rather than a port, "requirements with a design" might
  replace "TODO lines left"); the glossary for `talk.pl translate`.
