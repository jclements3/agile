== The Vee, kept: the systems-engineering V in MBSE DevSecOps {#vee}

::: bridge
You know: the Vee. Decompose on the left arm, from stakeholder needs to design; integrate, verify and validate on the
right arm; tie each level on the left to its check on the right; hold a technical review at each gate.

Here it is called: still the Vee. The left arm is the model's layers, changed by merge requests. The right arm is
the pipeline's checks plus the test events. The horizontal lines are `verify` relationships. The reviews are
baselines: protected tags with a review package the pipeline generates.

What is new: the cadence. The program still walks one big Vee across its reviews, but every merge request now
walks a small one in minutes, and every baseline walks a medium one. Nothing on the right arm waits for a milestone.
:::

This chapter is CORE: it is the frame for everything that follows. Read it before [[devsecops]], which translates
the vocabulary; this chapter explains the shape.

=== Jim's whiteboard {#vee-whiteboard}

The second time the team met about the directive ([[directive]]), Bob walked to the whiteboard, drew a large V and
put the marker down.

> Every program I've been on runs on this. Requirements down the left, test up the right, a review at every corner.
> The memo says "continuous delivery". Where does the V go? Do we throw it away?
> -- Bob, the week before sprint 1

It was the right question, and the honest answer took Jim most of the afternoon to work out: the V stays. What
changes is how often you walk it, and who does the walking on the right-hand side. In the document era the team
walked the right arm by hand, a few times a year, in the weeks before a review. In the new way a machine walks the
lowest rungs of the right arm on every change, and people spend their time on the rungs only people can climb.

This chapter is that afternoon, written down.

=== The Vee in one paragraph {#vee-recap}

The systems-engineering Vee draws the life of a system as a letter V. Down the **left arm** the team decomposes:
stakeholder needs become system requirements, requirements become an architecture, the architecture becomes a
detailed design. At the **bottom** something gets built. Up the **right arm** the team integrates the pieces and
checks them, level by level, against what the left arm said: the design against its specification, the integrated
system against its requirements, the delivered system against the stakeholders' needs. The **horizontal lines**
across the V are the promise that every statement on the left has a check on the right. The **gates** on the arms
are the technical reviews -- SRR, SDR or PDR, CDR, TRR, PRR, and the configuration audits -- where a board looks at
the evidence and decides whether the program moves on.

Nothing in that paragraph mentions documents, tools or a calendar. That is why the Vee survives the move.

=== What DevSecOps changes: three Vees, not one {#vee-three}

{{figure: docs/img/vee-devsecops.svg | The Vee in MBSE DevSecOps: each element and the GitLab mechanism that carries it}}

In the document era there was one Vee per program, walked once, with reviews months apart. In MBSE DevSecOps the
same shape repeats at three time scales:

- **The program's Vee**, across the technical reviews. It is the one you already know, and it still takes years. The
  reviews still happen and a board still decides; what arrives at each review is different ([[vee-reviews]]).
- **A baseline's Vee**, release by release. Between reviews the team cuts baselines (Tidewater's are
  `baseline-0`, `baseline-1` ...). Each one goes down the left arm (what changed), through the build (the release
  branch), and up the right arm (the baseline audit, the verification matrix, the people who approve it).
- **A merge request's Vee**, in minutes. One change goes down the left arm (Bob edits a requirement and its
  satisfy relationship), is built (the merge request), and comes up the right arm (grammar, lint, the trace check,
  the verification matrix, a code owner's review) before it lands. The lowest rungs of the right arm are walked
  hundreds of times between two reviews instead of once.

The middle of the figure is where the shift happens: *a change to the model* sits at the bottom of the V, and the
model checks are its first rung up. Bob's worry was that continuous delivery would flatten the V into a line. It
does the opposite: it makes the V small enough to walk every day.

=== The left arm: the model's layers {#vee-left}

On the left arm the document era had a specification tree: the SRS, the SSDD, the ICDs, the design documents, each
with its own revision letter. In the new way the same content lives in one model, arranged in layers that mirror
the left arm ([[repo-layout]]):

|| Left arm level | Where it lives in the model | How it changes ||
| Stakeholder needs | `model/requirements/stakeholder/` (from the DOORS needs module) | DOORS export, imported by the vendor branch |
| System requirements | `model/requirements/system/`, each with its `doorsId` | merge request; the recipe check grades the wording ([[recipe]]) |
| Architecture | `model/logical/` and the parts, ports and interfaces of `model/physical/` | merge request; the layers and complexity checks guard the structure |
| Design | detail inside `model/physical/` | merge request, by the package's code owner |
Table: The left arm of the Vee as folders in the model.

The arm is still walked top to bottom: a stakeholder need is still decomposed into requirements before anyone
designs. What changed is the unit of work. A decomposition step is a story, its pieces are tasks of under a week, and
each lands as a merge request that a second engineer reviews ([[operating-model]], [[reviewing]]).

=== The right arm: checks at every level {#vee-right}

The right arm is where DevSecOps changes the most. In the document era every rung was a manual event: a desk check,
an integration test, a review of a verification matrix someone had assembled by hand. Now each rung has an owner,
and the lowest rungs are machines.

- **Model checks, the first rung (MIL, model in the loop).** On every merge request the pipeline checks the
  grammar, resolves every name, checks the outline, the layers and the complexity, compares the trace with DOORS,
  regenerates the verification matrix and grades the requirement wording. Minutes, not weeks.
- **Integration.** Interfaces are checked when they change: the interface tables and the connection view are
  generated from the model ([[diagrams]]), and an interface change needs the systems lead's approval through
  CODEOWNERS. Where a program has a simulation, a nightly software-in-the-loop job runs it (SIL).
- **System verification.** The trace ratchet keeps every requirement's verification case in place, the verification
  matrix (VCRM) shows each requirement's latest verdict, and a lab bench runs hardware-in-the-loop tests (HIL) when a
  person starts them ([[verification]]).
- **Validation and acceptance.** Test events and the customer's review: people and real conditions. The pipeline's
  job here is evidence, not judgement: the signed verdicts and the matrix go into the baseline's release.

You can see the rungs the pipeline walks for each kind of event. Start the practice project from lesson 11 (a copy of
the team's model repository on an offline GitLab), then ask the pipeline what it would run for a merge request:

```sh run sandbox=practice-11 cwd=work expect="pipeline: 10 jobs"
. ../env.sh > /dev/null
perl "$KIT/bin/gitlab.pl" ci --list --event merge_request_event --target develop | grep -v skipped
```

Ten jobs: the first rung of the right arm, on every change. Now the same question for a baseline tag:

```sh run expect="baseline +release"
perl "$KIT/bin/gitlab.pl" ci --list --event tag --tag baseline-1 | grep -v skipped
```

The same ten checks run again, and three more join them: `baseline` (the configuration audit), `publish-package`
and `publish-release` (the review package). That is the baseline's Vee coming up the right arm. Finally, the
nightly schedule with a simulation and a lab bench switched on:

```sh run expect="hil-test +trace +manual"
perl "$KIT/bin/gitlab.pl" ci --list --event schedule --branch develop --var SIL_ENABLED=true --var HIL_ENABLED=true | grep -v skipped
```

`sil-regression` runs by itself every night; `hil-test` waits, `manual`, for a person who has checked the bench.
Tidewater has neither switched on, because the government team owns no simulation and no lab; the template carries
them for the day a program does ([[verification]]).

=== The horizontal lines: verify, in the same merge request {#vee-links}

The horizontal lines are the Vee's most important promise and its most neglected one. In the document era the
promise was a verification cross-reference matrix, assembled in a spreadsheet before each review, out of date the day
after. Here a horizontal line is a `verify` relationship in the model: a verification case that names the requirement
it verifies. Two rules keep the line from breaking:

1. A requirement and its verification case change in **the same merge request**. The reviewer sees both.
2. The **trace check** compares the model with DOORS on every merge request and fails when a change *adds* a gap: a
   requirement that loses its satisfy (an orphan) or its verify (unverified). Gaps that were already there are
   reported, not blocked: they are someone else's to fix, often the prime's ([[pipeline]]).

Break a horizontal line on purpose and watch the check catch it. On a scratch branch, delete one `verify` and run the
trace job as a merge request into `develop` would:

```sh run expect="unverified 9 -> 10 \(new: TW-SYS-003\)"
git switch -q -c try-vee origin/develop
sed -i '/verify TW_SYS_003;/d' model/verification/Verification.sysml
git commit -qam "drop one verify"
CI_PIPELINE_SOURCE=merge_request_event CI_MERGE_REQUEST_TARGET_BRANCH_NAME=develop perl ci/job.pl trace 2>&1 | tail -1
git switch -q develop && git branch -q -D try-vee
```

The nine unverified requirements that were already there did not block anyone. The tenth, the one this change
created, does, and the message names it. In the document era that line would have broken silently, and the review
in three months' time would have found it, if anyone looked.

=== The gates: technical reviews as baselines {#vee-reviews}

The technical reviews keep their names, their boards and their decisions. Three things about them change.

- **What a review looks at.** A review used to look at a document set assembled for it. Now it looks at a
  **baseline**: a protected tag, `baseline-N`, on a commit of the model. Everything the board reads -- the generated
  documents, the verification matrix, the trace report, the toolchain list -- is produced from that tag by the
  pipeline, so it describes exactly what was tagged.
- **How the baseline is audited.** The configuration audit used to be a person comparing a document list with a
  library. Now the `baseline` job rebuilds the generated documents from the tagged model and compares a checksum
  with the one recorded in the tag. If the tag's documents were edited by hand, or the model changed without its
  documents, the audit fails.
- **Who decides.** Nobody new. The board still decides; in GitLab its decision is an approval on the release merge
  request, by the people CODEOWNERS names, with the change-board concurrence written as a comment ([[sprint-6]]).

Cut a baseline by hand and audit it. The tag records the checksum of the generated documents; the `baseline` job
rebuilds them and checks:

```sh run expect="baseline-try rebuilds identically"
h=$(git ls-tree -r HEAD -- docs | sha256sum | cut -c1-64)
git tag -a baseline-try -m "baseline-try" -m "sha256=$h"
CI_COMMIT_TAG=baseline-try perl ci/job.pl baseline | grep "rebuilds identically"
git tag -d baseline-try > /dev/null
```

Now tag a commit whose model changed without its generated documents, the way a hurried hand edit would:

```sh run expect="the tagged tree does not rebuild its docs"
git switch -q -c try-audit
sed -i 's/part def Thermometer\b/part def AirThermometer/' model/physical/stations/Stations.sysml
git commit -qam "rename a part without regenerating the documents"
h=$(git ls-tree -r HEAD -- docs | sha256sum | cut -c1-64)
git tag -a baseline-bad -m "baseline-bad" -m "sha256=$h"
CI_COMMIT_TAG=baseline-bad perl ci/job.pl baseline 2>&1 | tail -1 || echo "exit status $?"
git checkout -q -- . && git switch -q develop && git branch -q -D try-audit && git tag -d baseline-bad > /dev/null
```

The audit refuses the baseline. A board looking at that tag would have been reading documents that no longer
described the model; the machine noticed before the board met.

=== Change control between the reviews {#vee-change}

Between two reviews the document era ran change control through engineering change proposals, a change board
meeting every few weeks, and a revision letter on each document. In the new way:

- A **change proposal** is a merge request. Its description says what and why, cites the requirement or the prime's
  change request, and links its issue ([[tools]]).
- **Board approval** is a code owner's approval, enforced by GitLab: an interface change cannot merge without the
  systems lead, a requirement change without its owner ([[reviewing]]).
- The **revision letter** is the commit, and the release of record is the baseline tag.
- Changes the government does not own -- the prime's -- arrive as drops, are assessed by element id and dispositioned
  by the board before anyone edits the model ([[prime-changes]]).

The board meets less often for routine changes and more productively for the ones that matter, because the routine
ones have been checked and approved in the merge request.

=== The Vee, then and now {#vee-table}

|| Vee element | Document era | MBSE DevSecOps ||
| Stakeholder needs | a needs document, a DOORS module | the DOORS module, imported into `model/requirements/stakeholder/` |
| System requirements | the SRS, revised before each review | requirement definitions in the model, changed by merge request, wording graded on each change |
| Architecture and design | block diagrams in Cameo, SSDD, ICDs | parts, ports and interfaces as text; the diagrams and interface tables generated from them |
| Build | the design is drawn and documented | a change is made to the model on a branch |
| Unit and model checks | desk checks before a review | grammar, lint, outline, layers on every merge request |
| Integration | interface review meetings | generated interface tables; the systems lead approves interface changes; SIL where it exists |
| System verification | a VCRM assembled by hand before TRR | the trace ratchet and a VCRM regenerated on every change; HIL results ingested |
| Validation, acceptance | test events, customer acceptance | the same, with the evidence attached to the baseline's release |
| Horizontal lines | a spreadsheet cross-reference | `verify` relationships, changed in the same merge request as the requirement |
| Technical review | a document set assembled for the review | a baseline tag and the review package the pipeline generates from it |
| Configuration audit | a person compares lists | the baseline job rebuilds and checks the checksum |
| Change control | ECP, CCB meeting, revision letters | merge request, code-owner approval, commit and tag |
Table: Each Vee element in the document era and in MBSE DevSecOps.

=== What stays the same, and what changes {#vee-same}

What stays the same:

- The shape: decompose, build, integrate and check, and the promise that every statement has a check.
- The reviews, their names, their entry and exit criteria, and the board that decides.
- The roles: who owns the requirements, who owns each part of the design, who approves.
- Validation by people, in real conditions. No pipeline validates a system.

What changes:

- **Cadence.** The right arm's lowest rungs run on every change, not once before a review.
- **Evidence.** What a review reads is generated from a tag, so it cannot drift from the model.
- **Who walks which rung.** Machines walk the repetitive checks; people spend their time on design, judgement and
  the tests only people can run.
- **When a problem is found.** A broken horizontal line is found the day it breaks, by the person who broke it.

=== Where Tidewater sits on the Vee {#vee-tidewater}

Tidewater is not a new program. Its buoys and shore stations are fielded, and the program is in production and
sustainment, long past CDR: on the program's Vee it sits near the top of the right arm, with the prime maintaining
the design. That shapes the team's whole twelve weeks:

- **The port is a small Vee of its own, on the model.** On the left, the team planned the port: what the v2 model
  must hold, in what layers, with what conventions ([[sprint-1]], [[sprint-2]]). At the bottom, the conversion and
  the hand work that closed the TODO lines. On the right, the checks that prove the port is complete and correct
  ([[proving]]), the interim baseline, and the management briefing, which is the port's validation: did it give the
  program what it needed ([[sprint-6]])?
- **Each prime drop is a change Vee.** The prime's change requests come down the left arm (what changed, by element
  id), the impact report finds where the change lands in the government's model, the vendor merge builds it, and
  the checks and the board's disposition take it up the right arm ([[prime-changes]], [[maintenance]]).
- **The government's role is mostly the right arm.** The prime designs; the government verifies, accepts and keeps
  the authoritative view of verification. That is why the trace ratchet and the verification matrix matter more to
  Jim's team than any design tool.

A team at the start of a program sits at the top of the left arm instead. Everything in this chapter still applies;
the stories are about decomposition rather than conversion.

=== Variants you may meet {#vee-variants}

- **Incremental Vee.** The system is delivered in increments, each walking its own Vee, often sharing the upper
  left arm. In GitLab each increment is a release line: a release branch per delivered configuration, fixes landing
  on `develop` first ([[maintenance]]).
- **Iterative or spiral.** The upper left arm is walked several times as the requirements mature. Each pass is a
  set of sprints ending in a baseline; the baselines are the spiral's turns.
- **Dual Vee.** An architecture Vee for the system and an entity Vee for each element, walked by different teams. In
  GitLab the entity Vees are separate projects (one per subsystem) feeding the system project through tagged,
  published interface packages; this is the federation described in [[repo-layout]].

=== Digital thread and digital twin {#twin}

Two more terms travel with MBSE, and management will use both. This section is CORE for understanding and LATER for
the twelve weeks: the team builds the thread from the first sprint, and nobody builds a twin in the first year.

{{figure: docs/img/vee-devsecops-twin.svg | One picture: the Vee and its reviews, two-week sprints, the DevSecOps loop, the model in GitLab as the spine, the digital thread and the digital twin}}

::: definition
digital thread: the unbroken chain of identifiers that connects a need to the requirements derived from it, to the
  design elements that satisfy them, to the verification cases and their verdicts, to the configuration that was
  fielded, so that anyone can follow any element forward or backward through the life cycle.
digital twin: a live digital counterpart of a fielded system, built from the model and kept in step with the real
  thing by its operational data, used to predict, diagnose and decide.
:::

In plain words: the **thread** is a promise about the *records* -- every element has one id that every later record
uses, and every baseline keeps the ids. The **twin** is a *working copy of the system* that runs beside the real one.

The figure puts them together. The model in GitLab is the spine; both arms of the Vee read and write it. The thread
runs through it from requirement to design to verification to the fielded configuration, kept in every baseline. The
twin sits beyond the top of the right arm, built from the fielded configuration and fed by real data. Its findings
come back along the top to the left arm as new needs, discrepancy reports and change requests, which start the next
small Vee.

**What Tidewater's twin would be.** A model of the live observation network: each buoy and shore station from the
v2 model's part tree, with its power system and sensors, fed by the real observation and health streams the stations
already send. It could predict when a buoy's battery will not last the winter, show which stations a storm will take
offline, or test a new transmission schedule before the prime changes it.

**What the kit builds: the thread.**

- One stable id per element, carried from the prime's Cameo model through every drop (the prime agreed to stable
  ids in week zero) and into the v2 text, with a map from each v1 id to its v2 element ([[prime-changes]]).
- `doorsId` on every requirement, so DOORS and the model meet on one key.
- `satisfy` and `verify` relationships, checked on every merge request by the trace ratchet.
- A verification matrix that ties each requirement to its cases and their latest verdicts.
- Baselines that freeze all of it under one tag, audited by checksum.

**What the kit does not build: the twin.** A twin needs live data feeds from the fielded system, a simulation that
runs the model's behavior, and a synchronization layer that keeps the copy in step with the real configuration. None
of that is in this kit, and none of it is a twelve-week job.

**What a team needs before a twin is worth building.**

1. A thread that holds: stable ids, baselines that rebuild, a trace with no unexplained gaps. A twin built on a
   broken thread mirrors the wrong system.
2. Behavior and parameters in the model, not only structure: states, flows, the energy balance with real values
   ([[workbook]]).
3. Access to operational data, approved for the purpose, with its own data rights and markings.
4. A question worth the cost: one decision the twin would change, with a number attached.

Tidewater's team has the first by sprint 6 and part of the second; the third and fourth are a program decision. When
management asks "are we building a digital twin?", the honest answer is: "We are building the thread a twin would
need, and here is what it would take after that."

=== Pitfalls {#vee-pitfalls}

- **Calling sprints "phases".** A sprint is a timebox, not a stage of the Vee. A sprint can hold work from both arms:
  one story decomposes a requirement while another closes a verification gap. Teams that map sprints to Vee phases
  rebuild the waterfall at a two-week scale.
- **Skipping the right arm because the pipeline is green.** The pipeline walks the lowest rungs. A green pipeline
  says the model is well formed and its trace is intact; it does not say the system works. Integration, test events
  and validation still need people, plans and dates ([[verification]]).
- **Reviews with nothing to review.** If the review package is not generated from the tag, the board reads something
  the model has already moved past. Generate it, tag it, audit it.
- **Treating the twin as the goal.** A twin is the last step of a long chain. Start with the thread.

=== You can now {#vee-you-can}

- Show where each element of the systems-engineering Vee lives in an MBSE DevSecOps project, and say what changed and
  what did not.
- Explain the three Vees -- the merge request's, the baseline's and the program's -- and show each with the pipeline.
- Demonstrate that a broken horizontal line is caught on the day it breaks, and that a baseline's documents are
  audited before a board reads them.
- Tell a digital thread from a digital twin, and say what has to exist before a twin is worth building.

=== Applying this to your project {#vee-applying}

Copy the mapping in [[vee-table]] and fill in your own program's names for each element: your review names, your
requirement modules, your test events. Keep the shape and the reviews; change the cadence of the right arm first,
starting with the rung where checks are most repetitive. Decide locally which reviews read baselines from the first
day and which keep their current package until a pilot proves the generated one. Build the thread before anyone
mentions a twin: stable ids from your prime, `doorsId` on every requirement, the trace check on every merge request.
