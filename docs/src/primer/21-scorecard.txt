== The twelve-week scorecard {#scorecard}

::: bridge
You know: the stoplight chart.

Here it is called: the twelve-week scorecard.

What is new: every measure was agreed in week zero and is computed from the evidence, including the one that did
not move.
:::

::: survival Before you start
What you have: the twelve measures the program manager signed on 1 October ([[directive-scorecard]]), and a
scorecard file the pipeline and the journal filled in at the end of every sprint.

What you need: to read the scorecard the way a skeptical manager will: measure by measure, before against after,
including the measures that did not move.
:::

The scorecard was the team's insurance. Bob had said it in the first week: "If we don't write down now what counts
as progress, in December they'll judge us on whatever they happen to look at." So the measures were agreed before
sprint 1, each with a definition, a source and a target, and each sprint's numbers were written by the system into
`docs/tidewater/sprints/NN/scorecard.json`. Nobody typed them. This chapter reads the last of those files against
the first, measure by measure.

=== Conversion debt: the TODO burn-down {#scorecard-todo}

The converter leaves a `// TODO` line for every v1 construct it cannot carry. Counting those lines on every change
was the team's port burn-down ([[pipeline]]). Nothing was imported in sprint 1, so the first count arrived with
drop 1 in sprint 2:

The steep fall in sprint 4 is the Stations package: most of its level tags closed in one story. The lines left at
the end are behavior and analysis, the constructs the converter never carries: the BuoyModes state machine (carried
into sprint 7), the energy-balance expression, and the profile note. You can count them yourself on the integration

grep -rh "// TODO" port/model --include=*.sysml | grep -vc "generated by"

=== Flow: lead time, review and gating {#scorecard-flow}

These three are the measures that changed the program manager's mind, because the document-era numbers beside them
are measured in working days and weeks. A requirement change used to take
{{value: docs/tidewater/sprints/06/scorecard.json document_era_baseline_notional.ccb_change_working_days}} working
days through the CCB; a model change now goes from the first commit to merged in a median of
{{value: docs/tidewater/sprints/06/scorecard.json 2_lead_time_hours}} hours, and every one of them was checked by
the pipeline and approved by a code owner who did not write it. [[analytics]] shows the sprint-by-sprint trend.

Two cautions keep this honest. First, the comparison is not like for like: a CCB change carried a requirement
through several organizations, while a merge request carries one model change through one team. The fair claim is
that the team's own part of the work is now measured in hours. Second, the baseline numbers are notional, taken from
the program's last document-era cycle as the team remembered it; they were never measured the way the new numbers
are. The scorecard says so in its own file, under `document_era_baseline_notional`.

=== Trace: computed every time, and flat {#scorecard-trace}

The trace job compares the prime's DOORS export with the model on every change. Its measures did not move in twelve
weeks, and they were not supposed to.

|| Measure | Value at sprint 6 ||
| C: requirements linked | {{value: docs/tidewater/sprints/06/scorecard.json 5_trace.C}} |
| F: orphans (nothing satisfies them) | {{value: docs/tidewater/sprints/06/scorecard.json 5_trace.F}} |
| G: unverified (no verification case) | {{value: docs/tidewater/sprints/06/scorecard.json 5_trace.G}} |
| I: completeness | {{value: docs/tidewater/sprints/06/scorecard.json 5_trace.I}} |
| Text drift between DOORS and the model | {{value: docs/tidewater/sprints/06/scorecard.json 5_trace.drift}} |
Table: Trace measures at the end of sprint 6, identical to sprint 2.

The gaps were planted in the prime's model before the government touched it: two requirements nothing satisfies, a
set without verification cases, one requirement whose DOORS wording differs from the model. They are the prime's to
fix, through discrepancy reports, and the drops that close them arrive from January. A government team that edited
the v2 model to close them would have made its model disagree with the contractual baseline. So the right twelve-week
result is not "coverage went up"; it is "every gap is found by a program on every change, and every gap has a
discrepancy report with an owner and a due drop." That is what measure 5 asked for.

::: warning Two different "verify coverage" numbers
The scorecard counts requirements that have a *verification case* (G above: the rest are covered, which is the
seventy percent the reviews quote). The verification matrix, new in the last sprint, also counts requirements with a
*passing recorded result*, and on that definition almost none qualify yet (the "Verification" line of the baseline-0
release notes gives the count): the prime's test results only start arriving with drop 4. The `talk.pl review` draft for sprint 6 quoted the
matrix number as if it were the scorecard's and announced a collapse. Name the definition every time you say
"coverage."
:::

=== Discrepancy reports {#scorecard-drs}

By the end of sprint 6 the team had opened
{{value: docs/tidewater/sprints/06/scorecard.json 6_drs.opened}} discrepancy reports to the prime and closed
{{value: docs/tidewater/sprints/06/scorecard.json 6_drs.closed}}. Closed zero sounds bad and is correct: a
discrepancy report closes when a prime drop fixes the problem, and the first drop after the port started arrives in
January ([[prime-changes]]). What management should see is that every gap the trace found became a written,
numbered, dated request to the prime within a sprint, where in the document era such gaps went into an email, if
anywhere.

Three things happened. The worst block, ObservingStation, lost its trace spaghetti in sprint 5: the maximum
relationships per part fell to less than half. Sprint 4 attacked both package cycles from [[cameo]]. The Network and
Operations cycle is gone. The DataCenter and Stations cycle is not: the story removed the dependency it was written
against (QualityControl on SensorSuite), and the complexity job still reports `cycles: DataCenter <-> Stations`,
so another reference between the two packages remains. The team reported that at the review rather than declaring
the story's goal met, and finding the remaining edge went into the sprint 7 backlog. And the "duplicates" column
jumped in sprint 3, when the DOORS modules were
imported: the model now holds each requirement twice, once as the Cameo copy and once as the DOORS module's
conversion. That doubling is deliberate (DOORS is authoritative, and the trace compares the two), but the measure
cannot tell a deliberate copy from clutter. The team reported the number as it was and explained it, rather than
redefining the measure to make the column look better. [[no-spider-web]] covers what it would take to finish.

=== Measures beyond the twelve {#scorecard-beyond}

The sprint files carry three more numbers that the team found useful, though they were not on the signed scorecard:

- **Work mix.** The share of done points that was engineering, platform or learning, by sprint. It opens this book
  ([[bluf]]): engineering grew from a quarter of sprint 1 to most of sprint 6.
- **Stand-up outcome share.** The share of stand-up lines that stated an outcome and a number. It went from
  {{value: docs/tidewater/sprints/01/scorecard.json standup_outcome_share}} in sprint 1 to
  {{value: docs/tidewater/sprints/06/scorecard.json standup_outcome_share}} from sprint 3 on ([[talking]]). Sprint 2
  scored lower than sprint 1 because sprint 1's statuses happened to quote counts while sprint 2's described
  mechanism.
- **First-pipeline pass rate.** How many merge requests passed their checks on the first try:
  {{value: docs/tidewater/sprints/06/scorecard.json first_pipeline_pass.passed}} of
  {{value: docs/tidewater/sprints/06/scorecard.json first_pipeline_pass.mrs}} in sprint 6. A failure on the first
  pipeline is the system working; the measure shows how much the checks are doing.

=== What the scorecard does not say {#scorecard-not}

A scorecard is a set of chosen numbers. This one does not measure the quality of the engineering decisions (whether
the cycle break put ObservationRecord in the right package), it does not compare cost with the document era, and its
"before" column is memory, not measurement. The team said so in the briefing. A scorecard that admits its limits is
easier to believe than one that does not.

::: exercise Read it like a skeptic
Pick the three measures you would challenge first if you were the program manager. For each, write the question you
would ask and find the evidence file that answers it.
:::

::: exercise The measure that missed
Measure 7 was not met. Draft the two sentences Jim should say about it in the briefing: what happened, and what
the plan is. Compare with [[briefing]].
:::

::: exercise Your own baseline
For your program, write down the document-era equivalent of measures 2, 4 and 10, with where the number comes from.
Mark each one "measured" or "remembered."
:::

=== You can now {#scorecard-can-now}

- read a sprint's scorecard file and trace each value to the job or record that produced it;
- explain why trace coverage stays flat during a port, and why that is the right result;
- separate a measure that missed for a good reason from one that missed for a bad one, and say which is which;
- present a scorecard that includes its misses and its limits.

=== Applying this to your project {#scorecard-applying}

Write your scorecard before sprint 1, with a definition, a source and a target for each measure, and have the
program manager sign it. Make the pipeline and the journal write it at every sprint end, so no number is typed.
Include at least one measure you might miss, and one that measures the team rather than the model. Keep the
"before" column honest: label remembered numbers as notional, and replace them with measured ones the first time you
can.
