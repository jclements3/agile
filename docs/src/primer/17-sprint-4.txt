== Sprint 4: the model site, a cycle broken, and the demo that landed {#sprint-4}

::: bridge
You know: the program review where the slides are the only evidence.

Here it is called: the model site, and a demo.

What is new: management looks at the live model, not at screenshots of it.
:::

::: survival
What you have: the DOORS requirements in the model, a trace report on every change that lists the prime's gaps
([[sprint-3]]), a team that has seen the pipeline say no and survived it, and a pulse survey that says morale is low.

What you need: the structure of the model's biggest package tagged and checked, one of the inherited dependency
cycles gone, every gap the trace found sent back to the prime through the contract, and something management can
open without a briefing. You also need a demo that tells management what they got for two weeks, because the last
one did not.
:::

Sprint 4 ran from Monday 16 November to Friday 27 November 2026. Thanksgiving fell on Thursday 26 November, so the sprint
had fewer working days than a full one; the capacity line in the kickoff record accounts for the holiday and the
leave around it.

It is the sprint in which the work started to pay the team back. The pulse went from
{{value: docs/tidewater/sprints/03/scorecard.json 12_morale}} to
{{value: docs/tidewater/sprints/04/scorecard.json 12_morale}}. The TODO count fell from
{{value: docs/tidewater/sprints/03/scorecard.json 1_todo_lines}} to
{{value: docs/tidewater/sprints/04/scorecard.json 1_todo_lines}}. And at the review, the program manager opened the
model on a phone.

=== The question this sprint answered {#sprint-4-question}

Sprint 3 asked whether the requirements match. Sprint 4 asked three questions about structure and one about
communication:

> Is every part in the Stations package at the right level of the part tree? Which of the dependency cycles the prime
> handed us can we break? What exactly do we ask the prime to fix? And can management see the model without us?

The first three are systems engineering questions the team had always asked, in reviews, with screenshots. The fourth
is new: in the Cameo years nobody outside the team ever looked at the model directly. They looked at slides made from
it.

=== Week 1 {#sprint-4-week-1}

==== Monday 16 November: learning to review

All three closed their learning tasks on the first morning: Jim worked through the review checklist the team keeps in
`docs/conventions/review-checklist.md`, Bob practiced line comments and suggestions on a scratch merge request, and Tom
wrote the first draft of the demo plan for 27 November. Reviewing is a skill the team had in the document era, where
it meant red pen on a printed specification. It transfers better than they expected. What is new is that the change
under review is small, the reviewer sees exactly the lines that changed, and the pipeline has already checked the
mechanical things, so the reviewer's attention goes to whether the change is *right*.

==== Thursday 19 November: breaking a cycle in two lines {#sprint-4-cycle}

The prime's model came with two package dependency cycles, measured in [[cameo]]: Stations depends on DataCenter
and DataCenter on Stations, and Network depends on Operations and Operations on Network. A cycle means neither package
can be understood, reviewed or changed without the other. In a diagram-based tool they are hard to see; in text they
are a line each.

Tom took the Network and Operations cycle. The Operations package depended on Network for one reason only: a
`dependency` from ServiceVessel to Technician, a left-over trace from the document port that said nothing a systems
engineer would miss. The fix removed it, and the import of Network that existed only to serve it:

```sh run sandbox=tidewater cwd=work/bob expect="dependency from ServiceVessel to Technician"
git show --format='%h %an %ad %s' --date=short 3a743cb | grep '^[0-9a-f]\|^[-+] '
```

Two removed lines. Jim approved !12 the same day, and Tom merged it. The cycle count the scorecard tracks went from
{{value: docs/tidewater/sprints/03/scorecard.json 7_spider_web.cycles}} to
{{value: docs/tidewater/sprints/04/scorecard.json 7_spider_web.cycles}}.

> The cycle was two lines. In Cameo it was a week of looking at a diagram.
> -- Tom, sprint 4

::: note The thought process: is it ours to break?
Operations.sysml is a file the converter writes from the prime's drop. Editing it means the next drop will try to put
the dependency back, unless the prime removes it too. Tom asked that question before writing the change, and the
answer has two parts. First, the government's v2 model is the government's: if a dependency is meaningless, the v2
model should not carry it, and the vendor-branch merge will show the conflict when the prime's next drop arrives
([[prime-changes]] works through how such edits survive). Second, the prime should know, so the team keeps a list of
the clutter it removes for the discrepancy reports. The other cycle, Stations and DataCenter, is harder: it involves
the observation record that both packages use, and deciding who owns it is a design decision, not a cleanup. It went
to [[sprint-5]].
:::

==== Friday 20 November: the Stations tags, a red X, and a question for the prime {#sprint-4-stations}

Bob opened merge request !13, "Level tags in Stations", at ten in the morning. It replaced the package's
level-stereotype TODO lines with `@L` metadata, the same mechanical change Bob had made in Network, on a much larger
package. The grammar and lint jobs passed.

The pipeline failed anyway. Not grammar, not lint: the **outline** job. Here is the part of its log that matters:

{{include: docs/tidewater/sprints/04/pipelines/mr13-first-36.log | lines 18-25}}

and at the end of the same job:

{{include: docs/tidewater/sprints/04/pipelines/mr13-first-36.log | lines 48-50}}

Two findings. The outline violation count went up by one, which the ratchet does not allow. And the level-tag check
found a part that is tagged at the wrong level: WaveSensor is tagged L5, an assembly, but the first place it appears in
the part tree is directly inside MooredBuoy, an L3 station, which makes it an L4 subsystem. The extra outline violation
comes from the same root.

You can reproduce the finding from Bob's commit:

```sh run expect="WaveSensor: tagged L5, first reached at L4"
git worktree add -q --detach ../../s4-stations 1c11bb3
cd ../../s4-stations && perl "$KIT/tools/sysml/model.pl" check | grep -A1 "tag mismatches"
```

Bob's tag was not a typo. Bob had copied the level from the prime's Cameo stereotype, exactly as the TODO line said.
The prime's model is inconsistent with itself: the stereotype says L5, the structure says L4. In the document era this
kind of inconsistency was found, if it was found at all, by someone reading two documents side by side.

This is the decision point the sprint is remembered for. Bob and Jim had three options:

1. Tag WaveSensor L5 as the prime says, and raise the outline allowance so the pipeline passes. The pipeline's own
   message forbids this: "fix them, do not raise .ci/outline-max".
2. Wait for the prime to fix its stereotype, leaving the Stations tags unmerged until a later drop.
3. Tag it L4 in the government's copy, where the structure says it belongs, and send the inconsistency to the prime
   as a discrepancy report so their model and the government's converge.

They chose the third, and the commit says so in the model itself:

```sh run cwd=work/bob expect="DR-7"
git show --format='%h %s' 8686239 | grep '^[0-9a-f]\|^[-+] '
```

Jim opened DR-7 the same day. The second pipeline was green, Jim approved, and Bob merged !13 that afternoon.

==== Reviewing !13: what Jim actually checked {#sprint-4-review-13}

Jim's approval came after the second pipeline, not the first. That is the first line of the team's review checklist,
learned on the Monday of this sprint:

{{include: tools/gitlab/templates/docs/conventions/review-checklist.md | lines 13-17}}

A red pipeline means the change is not finished; reviewing it wastes the reviewer's time and invites the author to
argue with the machine through the reviewer. Once the pipeline was green, Jim's review was about the engineering. The
size was fine: the change touched one file, mostly one kind of line, about fifty lines in all. The levels matched the
part tree, which the outline job had now confirmed mechanically, so Jim did not need to count levels by eye. And one
rule of the checklist applied directly to this change:

{{include: tools/gitlab/templates/docs/conventions/review-checklist.md | lines 39-40}}

The WaveSensor line changed something the prime wrote, beyond closing a TODO. The rule allows such a change only with a
discrepancy report behind it, and the line names one in its comment. Jim's review took minutes, and most of those minutes went to the
one line that mattered. That is what a review checklist is for: it moves the reviewer's attention from the mechanical
to the consequential.

::: objection We should not change the prime's model
The objection, in Bob's words: "If we change their levels, we own a model that disagrees with the contract deliverable."

Answer: the government's v2 model already disagrees with the prime's v1 model in every place the team has closed a
TODO; that is the point of the port. What must not happen is a *silent* disagreement. The change carries the DR
number in a comment on the line itself, the DR goes to the prime through the contract, and when the prime's drop
fixes the stereotype the vendor-branch merge brings the two back together. A disagreement that is written down,
reported and tracked is configuration management. One that is not is the spider web again.
:::

==== Friday 20 November, continued: discrepancy reports to the prime {#sprint-4-drs}

While Bob dealt with WaveSensor, Jim did the sprint's most important piece of systems engineering, and it involved no
model edits at all. Jim turned each gap the trace report had found the sprint before into a discrepancy report: an issue in
GitLab, from the team's Discrepancy Report template, labeled for the prime, and transmitted through the contracting
channel.

```sh run cwd=. expect="DR-7: WaveSensor"
perl "$KIT/bin/gitlab.pl" --local gitlab --project tidewater/model issue list --state all --label type::discrepancy | sed 's/~[^ ]* //g'
```

(The list is the team's GitLab today, at the end of sprint 6; the reports about the Legacy Import clutter and the DOORS wording were opened in [[sprint-5]].) Here is the
first of them, as Jim wrote it:

{{include-md: docs/tidewater/sprints/04/samples/discrepancy-report-DR-1.md}}

|| Part of the report | What goes in it | Why ||
| DR number | the team's own sequence, assigned when the issue is opened | so every later reference, in a model comment or a drop's MANIFEST, can cite it |
| Prime tracking | left blank until the prime assigns its own number | the two organizations will each track it; both numbers end up on the issue |
| Found in drop | the drop version and its delivery date | the prime fixes what it delivered, not what it has now |
| DOORS id | the requirement, if there is one | the trace job reports by DOORS id, so closure can be checked mechanically |
| Severity | the team's scale, from the template | so the prime and the program office can order their work |
| What we found | one or two sentences, in engineering words, and how it was found | the prime must be able to reproduce it without the government's tools |
| What we ask | exactly what the government wants changed, not how | the model is the prime's to change |
| Closure | checkboxes: sent, by whom, when; fixed in which drop | stays open until a drop actually fixes it |
Table: Anatomy of a discrepancy report

The report says what was found, where, how it was found, and exactly what the government asks the prime to do. It does
not say how to fix it inside Cameo; that is the prime's business. And it has a closure section that stays open until a
drop actually fixes the problem, which is how the team will know, months later, whether the prime did what it was
asked.

::: note The thought process: why not just fix their model?
Every gap on that list would take Bob a minute to "fix" in the government's v2 model: add a satisfy for TW-SYS-012,
copy TW-SYS-016 in from DOORS, write the missing verification case for TW-SYS-011. Jim said no to all of them, and the
retrospective agreed (see below). The requirements and their satisfaction are the prime's contractual responsibility.
If the government quietly patches its copy, the prime's model stays wrong, the next drop reintroduces every gap, and
the government has taken on design responsibility nobody funded. The discrepancy report puts the fix where it
belongs. The trace job will show, drop by drop, which reports the prime has closed.
:::

The scorecard shows {{value: docs/tidewater/sprints/04/scorecard.json 6_drs.opened}} discrepancy reports opened in
the sprint and none closed yet; the first closure comes with drop 2 in January.

==== Tuesday 24 November: the model site {#sprint-4-site}

Tom's !14 added the `pages` job to the pipeline. On every merge to `develop`, and every night on a schedule, the job
builds a small web site from the model and publishes it with GitLab Pages: the generated documentation (the part tree,
the function tree, the IDEF0 plates, the trace report), the diagrams the kit draws, and the TODO burn-down. Nothing on
the site is written by hand. If the model changes, the site changes with it, the same day.

```sh run cwd=. expect="https://tidewater.pages"
perl "$KIT/bin/gitlab.pl" --local gitlab --project tidewater/model pages | head -1
```

The site is small on purpose. Each page answers a question someone outside the team used to ask the team, usually
the week before a review:

|| Page | The question it answers | Who asks it ||
| The part tree (`docs/`, the generated Nouns page and the tree view) | What is the system made of, and at what level? | the program manager, new engineers, the prime |
| The function tree and the IDEF0 plates | What does the system do, and what does each function take and produce? | the test organization, the operations community |
| The trace report | Which requirements have a design and a verification, and which do not? | the program office, the independent reviewers |
| The diagram views | What connects to what? | everyone, in a review |
| The TODO burn-down | How much of the port is left? | the program manager, every Friday |
Table: The model site in sprint 4, page by page

None of the pages is a new kind of information. Every one of them used to be a slide, a table in a document, or an
answer to an e-mail, prepared by hand from the model. What is new is that they are never out of date and nobody has to
prepare them.

The nightly rebuild is a pipeline schedule, the first the team created:

```sh run expect="Nightly full check"
perl "$KIT/bin/gitlab.pl" --local gitlab --project tidewater/model schedule list | head -1
```

It runs at 02:00 Eastern on weekdays, against `develop`, with every check switched on. If anything broke during the
day, it is red before the stand-up. Chapter [[tools]] explains why the site was the single most valuable tool the
team adopted, for an audience the team had never served directly: everyone else.

The same Tuesday, Jim prepared the sprint review with the program office (task #82), and on Wednesday Jim met the
prime's modeler about how future drops should be delivered (task #83). That conversation, about stable element ids,
change notes, and eventually letting the prime's Jenkins deliver straight into GitLab, is the subject of
[[prime-changes]].

The review notes record how the rehearsed demo ran: it led with the question, showed the model site, gave one
before-and-after, made one live change, and closed with the ask. Those are the six moves of [[talking]], and here is
what each one looks like for a sprint like this one:

1. **The question first.** What did this sprint settle about the model, in the audience's words: is the structure
   right, and can they see it without the team? Not "here is what we built".
2. **The output, not the tool.** The model site, not the terminal. The part tree, the trace report and the burn-down,
   as pages anyone can read.
3. **One before-and-after.** The notes name the one used: the cycle, two lines removed, against a week of reading a
   diagram in the Cameo years.
4. **One safe live change.** The script's suggestion: on a scratch branch, delete one satisfy line and open a draft
   merge request; a couple of minutes later the trace report names the requirement that lost its design. Then close
   the merge request.
5. **What it means for the next milestone.** How far the structure TODOs have to go, and when the interim baseline
   for the program review is due.
6. **The ask.** One decision or one kind of help, with a date.

The program manager's reaction is in the review notes, and it is the moment the team remembers:

> -- Program manager, sprint 4 review

=== You can now {#sprint-4-you-can-now}

- Break a package dependency cycle by finding the one dependency that creates it, and decide whether it is yours to
  break.
- Read an outline and level-tag finding, reproduce it on your own checkout, and decide between fixing your copy,
  waiting for the prime, and doing both.
- Write a discrepancy report that says what was found, how, and what you ask the prime to do, and keep it open until a
  drop closes it.
- Explain why verify coverage can stay flat while the model improves.
- Set up a model site and a nightly schedule, and say what each one is for.
- Run a demo with the six moves, and carry a story honestly when it does not fit.

=== Applying this to your project {#sprint-4-applying}

- **Publish the model where management can open it,** generated from the model on every merge, as early as you can.
  It changes the conversation more than any metric.
- **Run a nightly full check** so problems are red before the stand-up, not found next week.
- **Send the prime's gaps back as discrepancy reports,** one per gap, with a closure section. Do not patch the prime's
  model quietly in your copy; when you must diverge, write the DR number on the line.
- **Rehearse the demo,** with the question first and the ask last. Rotate the driver.
- **Expect your first carryover** when a task depends on someone outside the team. Carry it visibly with its remaining
  points; the quad and the journal are built for it.
