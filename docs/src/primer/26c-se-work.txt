== Systems engineering work, not pipeline work {#se-work}

::: bridge
You know: the engineering itself: requirements, architecture, interfaces, analyses and verification.

Here it is called: work::engineering, as distinct from work::platform and work::learning.

What is new: the board shows how much of the team's time goes to engineering, sprint by sprint.
:::

::: survival The work is the engineering
What you have: a backlog full of stories, and a nagging feeling that too many of them are about GitLab, runners and
YAML, and too few are about the system.

What you need: a clear line between systems engineering work (deciding and proving what the system is) and platform
work (building and keeping the machinery that checks and renders it), a way to see the balance sprint by sprint, and a
catalog of real engineering stories to fill the backlog with once the machinery runs.
:::

=== The line {#se-work-line}

Bob asked the question in the second sprint, and the question was a fair one:

> Two sprints in, and I have written a pipeline description, a runner request and a branching guide. When do I do
> systems engineering again?
> -- Bob, sprint 2

It is the right question, and the answer has to be a number, not a reassurance. Every story on the Tidewater board
carries one of three labels:

::: definition The three kinds of work
work::engineering: deciding or proving what the system is. Requirements, architecture, interfaces, behavior,
  analysis, verification, change decisions. The output is model content or an engineering judgment someone signs.
work::platform: building or keeping the machinery that checks, renders and protects the model. Pipelines, runners,
  GitLab settings, templates, access, the kit version. The output is a working tool.
work::learning: becoming able to do either. Practice lessons, chapters of this book, pairing at the terminal. The
  output is a person who can do something they could not do last sprint.
:::

A quick test for a story you are not sure about: **would a systems engineer from the document era recognize the
output as engineering?** A closed TODO line that adds `@L2Segment;` to a part: yes, it is a configuration decision
about the system's structure. A new pipeline job that counts TODO lines: no, it is a tool, however useful. A data
exchange table generated from the interface items: yes, it is the interface control document, produced differently.
Deciding who owns `ObservationRecord` so a dependency cycle can be broken: very much yes, it is an architecture
decision.

The label is not a value judgment. Platform work is how the engineering becomes cheap to check, and learning is how a
team that had never seen a merge request learned to run one. The point of the label is to make the balance visible, so
that platform and learning shrink as they should, and so that nobody has to argue from feelings.

=== The balance, sprint by sprint {#se-work-balance}

The split comes straight out of the plan file the simulation runs (`sim/tidewater-plan.pl`), so it cannot be rounded
in anyone's favour:

```sh run sandbox=kit expect="Sprint 6: .*engineering"
perl -e 'require "$ENV{KIT}/sim/tidewater-plan.pl"; for my $s (grep { $_->{n} <= 6 } @{ tw_plan() }) { my %w; for my $st (@{ $s->{stories} }) { $w{ $st->{work} } += $_->{p} for @{ $st->{tasks} } } my $t = 0; $t += $_ for values %w; printf "Sprint %d: %4.1f points  engineering %3.0f%%  platform %3.0f%%  learning %3.0f%%\n", $s->{n}, $t, map { 100 * ($w{$_} // 0) / $t } qw(engineering platform learning) }'
```

The shape is the one to aim for. In the first two sprints platform and learning take most of the points: there is a
project to create, a runner to get approved, three laptops to set up, git and Vim to learn. From sprint 3 engineering
takes the larger share, and by sprint 6 most of the work is engineering again, which is the answer to Bob's question, and the one
to give with these numbers on the screen. GitLab can show the same split live: filter the board or the
issue list by `work::engineering`, and the milestone page sums the points.

Two warnings about the number. It is a planning measure, not a productivity score: a sprint that is half platform
because the runner died is a sprint that kept the team working. And it does not go to zero: maintenance sprints still
carry a story or two of platform work (a runner upgrade, a token rotation, a kit version bump), which is how the
machinery stays trustworthy.

the model, the check that proves it, and the artifact someone outside the team can look at. Branch names follow
`feature/<issue number>-<a few words>` ([[style]]); the issue numbers are left out here because they differ from run to
run. Sprints are the plan's ([[operating-model]]).

Every behavior story here starts from a converter TODO line, because the converter does not carry v1 state machines,

A prime drop is the clearest example of engineering hiding inside a tooling-shaped story. The import itself is a
command. The work is the disposition: which of the prime's changes the government accepts, which it questions, which
cost money, and what each one does to requirements and verification. That is change-board work, and it is the reason
the drop stories are labelled engineering ([[prime-changes]]).

Notice how many of them exist to make an engineering story cheaper: the trace ratchet makes "Trace completeness"

=== A generic catalog for your project {#se-work-generic}

The catalog below is the pattern to build your own backlog from. It follows a list of thirty-eight planned-work items
collected while this book was written, from a small team modeling a different system; the list mixed engineering and
platform work in roughly the proportion a team reaches after its first semester, which is itself a useful benchmark.
Each line is a story title, the model change, and the check that makes it done.

==== Concept and requirements

- **Capture stakeholder needs from the concept of operations.** A requirements package per stakeholder group, each need
  tagged with its source document. Check: the package resolves (lint).
- **Define the top-level use cases.** A use case per mission thread, with actors and objectives. Check: a generated
  use-case table for the customer walkthrough.
- **Derive system requirements from the needs.** Requirements with measurable constraints and derivation links. Check:
  the trace fails any system requirement without a parent.
- **Add a requirement metadata schema.** Verification method, priority, owner, status as metadata. Check: a job fails a
  requirement missing a required field.
- **Import the legacy requirements spreadsheet.** A script that turns the CSV into requirement text, run once, kept for
  the next import. Check: the merge request's diff, reviewed.
- **Establish the units and quantities library.** Shared value types on ISQ quantities; requirements refactored to use
  them. Check: lint (no unresolved types).

==== Architecture

- **Define the logical decomposition.** Part definitions for the function blocks, composed into the system context.
  Check: the part tree view.
- **Model a subsystem's interfaces.** Port and interface definitions, connections, a satisfy to the interface
  requirement, a verification case stub. Check: trace coverage for the subsystem.
- **Allocate functions to physical components.** Allocations at the agreed level. Check: the trace flags unallocated
  functions.
- **Model the power subsystem and its budget.** Power draw per part and a roll-up per mode. Check: a job fails when a
  mode exceeds supply.
- **Model the mass properties.** Mass with margin per part, rolled up. Check: the mass report.
- **Model the data bus and its messages.** Item definitions with fields and rates. Check: the generated ICD table.
- **Model the thermal interfaces.** Heat flows and dissipation. Check: the values handed to the thermal team as a file.
- **Define an external interface.** The external system as a part, with the agreed protocol and timing. Check: the
  interface lead is a code owner of the package.

==== Behavior

- **Model the operating modes.** A state definition with transitions and guards, linked to the budgets per mode.
  Check: grammar and lint; the budgets run per mode.
- **Model the startup sequence.** An action with ordered steps and timing. Check: total startup time against its
  requirement.
- **Model fault detection and recovery.** Fault states and recovery actions, each linked to the hazard it mitigates.
  Check: a fault-to-response table for the safety review.
- **Model the end-to-end data timeline.** Latency on each step. Check: the sum against the end-to-end requirement.

==== Analysis and trades

- **Run a trade study.** Candidate variants with their attributes and a weighted scoring. Check: the trade table.
- **Hook up an external simulation.** A wrapper that reads parameters from the model, runs the simulation, writes
  results back. Check: the job's artifact.
- **Allocate reliability.** Failure rates and a series-parallel roll-up. Check: a job fails below the requirement.
- **Run a sensitivity analysis.** Sweep the key parameters through a budget. Check: the chart for the review package.

==== Verification

- **Write the verification cases for a subsystem.** A case per requirement with method, objective and criteria.
  Check: verify coverage for the subsystem reaches 100%.
- **Ingest test results.** A script that maps results to verification cases. Check: the VCRM regenerates.
- **Record analysis and inspection verdicts.** A signed results format for what is not automated. Check: the VCRM
  merges them.

==== Baselines and sustainment

- **Prepare a review baseline.** Close the trace gaps, defer what remains, all checks green. Check: the baseline tag
  and its document set.
- **Process an engineering change after a review.** The change, its impact report and the change-board approval on the
  merge request. Check: a minor baseline tag.
- **Branch for a delivered configuration.** A protected release branch from the qualification tag; fixes land upstream
  first. Check: the branch rules.
- **Model a field discrepancy and its fix.** The discrepancy linked to parts and verification cases, a regression case
  added. Check: the regression passes on both branches.
- **Retire obsolete requirements.** Mark them retired rather than deleting them. Check: coverage metrics exclude them.

==== And the platform items in the same list

Model style checks; generating the review document package; pinning the tool image; enabling secret and dependency
scans; code owners per subsystem; an impact report posted on each merge request. Six of thirty-eight: about one in six.
That is roughly the steady state to expect once the machinery exists, and a useful target when you plan the second
semester.

=== Splitting an engineering story into tasks {#se-work-splitting}

A story fits in a sprint; a task fits in a week, at most five points ([[operating-model]]). Engineering stories split
along the engineering, not along the tools. "Close the Stations level tags", split by what an engineer decides, could look like this:

1. Tag the segment and the station parts (L2, L3): 1 point. The levels everyone agrees on.
2. Tag the subsystems and assemblies (L4, L5): 1 point. Where the v1 profile is most often inconsistent, and a question to
   the prime's modeler may be needed.
3. Tag the components (L6) and run the level check: 1 point. The leaves, and the proof.

A split along the tools ("edit the file", "run lint", "open the MR") produces tasks nobody can demo and nobody can
estimate. A split along the decisions produces tasks a reviewer can check one at a time.

=== Applying this to your project {#se-work-applying}

- **Label every story** `work::engineering`, `work::platform` or `work::learning` from the first sprint, and show the
  split at every review. The trend answers "when do we do engineering again?" better than any promise.
- **Expect the first two sprints to be mostly platform and learning,** and say so to management before they start.
- **Fill the backlog from the catalog,** starting with the requirements and architecture stories your converted model
  already demands: every TODO line is an engineering decision waiting for a story.
- **Look for the engineering inside tooling-shaped stories** (prime drops, baselines, hotfixes) and label them by
  their decisions, not their commands.

::: exercise {#ex-se-work-label} Label your backlog
Take the next ten stories in your backlog (or Tidewater's sprint 7 plan in `sim/tidewater-plan.pl`) and give each one
of the three labels. For every story you labelled platform, write one sentence naming the engineering story it makes
cheaper. If you cannot, ask whether the story is needed now.
:::

::: exercise {#ex-se-work-split} Split along the decisions
Split "Break the Stations to DataCenter cycle" into tasks of at most five points each, along the decisions an engineer
makes rather than the commands they type. Compare your split with the plan's.
:::
