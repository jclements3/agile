== Measuring with GitLab analytics {#analytics}

::: bridge
You know: the metrics slide assembled the night before a review.

Here it is called: GitLab analytics, and gitlab.pl analytics.

What is new: the numbers come from the work itself, every day, and nobody counts by hand.
:::

::: survival Before you start
What you have: twelve weeks of issues, merge requests and pipelines in one GitLab project, and the kit's journal of
stories.

What you need: to know which of GitLab's analytics views answer a question a systems engineer or a program manager
actually asks, which of them your license includes, and how to get the same numbers when it does not.
:::

Jim's first instinct, when the program manager asked for "the metrics," was to open GitLab's Analytics menu and
screenshot whatever was there. Tom talked Jim out of it. Half the menu was greyed out on their license tier, and the
other half answered questions nobody on the program had asked. "Deployment frequency" sounds impressive until
someone asks what Tidewater deploys. This chapter is the sorting Jim and Tom did in sprint 6: what each view
measures, what it means for a team whose product is a model, and how to get the numbers without buying a tier you
do not need.

=== Two kinds of numbers {#analytics-two-kinds}

The team keeps two records, and each one is good at different questions.

- **The journal** (the kit's `scrum.txt`, [[journal]]) holds stories: what was committed, done, carried, blocked,
  and how many points each was worth. It answers planning questions: are we delivering what we commit to, and how
  fast does the backlog shrink?
- **GitLab** holds tasks, merge requests, approvals, pipelines and tags. It answers flow and quality questions: how
  long does a change take from proposal to acceptance, how often does a check catch something, how long are we red?

Neither replaces the other. The scorecard in [[scorecard]] takes its delivery and morale measures from the journal
and the retrospectives, and its flow and quality measures from GitLab. This chapter is about the second kind.

=== The views GitLab offers {#analytics-views}

GitLab's analytics are spread over several pages, and which ones you see depends on the license tier. Tiers and the
features in them change from release to release, so treat the tier column below as a guide and check GitLab's
current pricing page before you plan around a view.

|| View | Tier (check current pricing) | What it measures | The question it answers for a model team ||
| Value stream analytics | Free (basic), Premium (custom stages) | time an issue spends in each stage, from created to closed | where do our changes wait: before work starts, in review, or after merge? |
| CI/CD analytics | Free | pipeline success rate and duration over time | do our checks run reliably, and how long does a change wait for them? |
| Repository analytics | Free | commits, languages, contributors | who is touching the model, and how often? |
| Merge request analytics | Premium | throughput, time to merge | how many changes did we accept, and how long did each take? |
| Code review analytics | Premium | open merge requests and their review activity | which proposed changes are stuck waiting for a reviewer? |
| Issue analytics | Premium | issues opened and closed per month | is the backlog of discrepancies and tasks growing or shrinking? |
| Contribution analytics | Premium (group level) | events per member | is the work spread across the team or resting on one person? |
| Milestone burndown and burnup | Premium | issues and weight closed against a milestone | are we on track for this sprint? |
| DORA metrics | Ultimate | deployment frequency, lead time, change failure rate, time to restore | how fast and how safely do changes flow? |
| Insights and analytics dashboards | Ultimate | charts you configure in YAML | anything above, on one page |
Table: GitLab's analytics views, as a systems engineer reads them.

The Tidewater project ran on the Free tier for the first twelve weeks; the tier decision was one of Jim's asks in the
request for enablers ([[memos]]). That meant value stream, CI/CD and repository analytics in the web pages, and
everything else from the API.

=== The Free-tier route: gitlab.pl analytics {#analytics-free}

Every number in the paid views is computed from data that every tier exposes through GitLab's REST API: merge
requests with their commits, notes and approvals; issues with their labels and dates; pipelines and their jobs;
deployments and releases. The kit's `gitlab.pl analytics` reads that data and prints the same five sections a
manager would want from the paid pages. It works the same way against a real GitLab server and against the offline
stand-in the book uses.

Here it is on the Tidewater project at the end of sprint 6. The sandbox is a copy of the simulated project, with its
GitLab state in `gitlab/`:

```sh run sandbox=tidewater expect="DORA, for a model repository"
perl "$KIT/bin/gitlab.pl" --local gitlab --project tidewater/model analytics --since 2026-12-14 --until 2026-12-24
```

The same report, as the team filed it with the sprint 6 evidence:

{{include: docs/tidewater/sprints/06/analytics.txt}}

Against a real server, the only change is the first two options: `--url https://gitlab.example.gov --token-env
GITLAB_TOKEN` instead of `--local gitlab`. The token needs only the `read_api` scope.

=== Reading the five sections {#analytics-reading}

**Value stream.** Each row is one stage of a change's life, with the median and the 85th percentile in hours. The
stage that matters most for a model team is *MR opened -> first review*: that is the wait for a colleague's eyes,
and it is where document-era reviews used to take weeks. In sprint 6 the median was
{{value: docs/tidewater/sprints/06/scorecard.json 10_review_turnaround_hours}} hours. The long stage at the top,
*issue created -> first commit*, is not a problem: stories are filed at refinement, a week before work starts, so
that stage measures planning lead, not delay.

**DORA, for a model repository.** The four DORA measures were defined for software teams that deploy services. A
model team deploys too, just not to production servers. The table below is the translation the kit uses.

|| DORA measure | Software meaning | Model meaning in `gitlab.pl analytics` ||
| Deployment frequency | releases to production | publishes of the model site (the `pages` environment) and baseline releases |
| Lead time for changes | commit to running in production | first commit on a branch to merged on `develop` |
| Change failure rate | deployments that caused an incident | merges that needed a fix-forward or a revert merge request |
| Time to restore | incident to recovery | `develop` pipeline red to the next green |
Table: The four DORA measures, translated for a model repository.

Two of these belong in front of management and two do not. Lead time
({{value: docs/tidewater/sprints/06/scorecard.json 2_lead_time_hours}} hours median in sprint 6) is the direct
answer to "how long does a change take now?", and time to restore (the worst case in sprint 6 was
{{value: docs/tidewater/sprints/06/scorecard.json 8_pipeline_health.worst_restore_hours}} hours, the night the
runner's disk filled up) shows the safety net works. Deployment frequency is mostly the nightly schedule rebuilding
the site, so it measures a cron line, not the team. Change failure rate was zero in the first twelve weeks, which
says more about the small number of merges than about the team's skill; quote it only with its denominator.

**CI/CD.** Pipeline runs, failures and duration. The job failure line is the one to read: it says which checks
caught something. In sprint 6 most failures were the `secrets` job, which is the planted test token in Bob's day in
the life ([[day-in-life]]) doing exactly what it should.

**Merge requests.** Throughput, time to merge and review turnaround per sprint, and merged-over-opened per author.
The per-author line is useful to the team and dangerous in a briefing: it reads as a productivity ranking, and in
sprint 6 it mostly shows that Tom prepared the baseline and the release.

**Issues.** Opened and closed by type, and the oldest open items. The discrepancy reports to the prime sit at the
top of the oldest list. That is correct: they are the prime's to close, with the next drop.

=== The trend across the twelve weeks {#analytics-trend}

One sprint is a snapshot. The case for management is the trend, so run the report over the whole period, by sprint,
and write the tables as CSV files for a spreadsheet:

```sh run expect="Sprint 1"
perl "$KIT/bin/gitlab.pl" --local gitlab --project tidewater/model analytics --since 2026-10-05 --until 2026-12-24 --by sprint --csv analytics-csv
ls analytics-csv
```

Read it the way the program manager will. Lead time and review turnaround are measured in hours from the first
sprint, against a document-era baseline measured in working days and weeks. They do not improve much after sprint 2,
and that is the honest finding: the gain came from the way of working, not from the team getting faster at it. The
one ungated change is merge request !1 in sprint 1, made before the pipeline file existed; its description says so.

=== Small numbers and other traps {#analytics-traps}

A three-person team produces a few merge requests a week. Statistics on numbers that small are fragile, and the
team learned to say so before anyone else did.

- **Quote the count with the median.** "Median {{value: docs/tidewater/sprints/06/scorecard.json 2_lead_time_hours}}
  hours over {{value: docs/tidewater/sprints/06/scorecard.json merges}} merges" is honest; the median alone invites a
  comparison with a team of fifty.
- **Do not chase a measure.** If review turnaround becomes a target, reviewers approve faster and catch less. The
  team watched it as a symptom, never as a goal.
- **Watch the definitions.** The `talk.pl review` draft for sprint 6 reported "verify coverage" from the
  verification matrix, which counts requirements with a *passing recorded result*. The scorecard reports
  requirements with a *verification case*. The draft therefore showed coverage collapsing from seventy percent to
  almost nothing, the week the matrix job arrived. Tom caught it in the Day-9 rehearsal; [[scorecard]] explains the
  two measures.
- **Separate the prime's numbers from the team's.** Open discrepancy reports and unverified requirements belong to
  the prime until a drop closes them. A government team that reports them as its own backlog looks stuck.

=== What goes in the briefing {#analytics-briefing}

Jim put three numbers from GitLab into the December briefing, and left the rest in the appendix:

1. **Lead time for a change**, with the document-era baseline beside it.
2. **Changes gated**: every change since sprint 2 passed the checks and a code owner's review.
3. **Pipeline health**: share of `develop` pipelines passing, and the worst time to restore.

Value stream stages, DORA deployment frequency, per-author counts and issue ages stayed in the team's own review.
The reasoning is in [[briefing]]: management can act on three numbers with a trend; it cannot act on twenty.

=== How this relates to the kit's other measures {#analytics-kit}

The kit already had measures before GitLab arrived. `daily.pl health` checks the journal against the thresholds of
the operating model (blocked items, load, velocity, carryover). The weekly quad ([[communicating]]) reports sprint
progress and on-time delivery. `bin/status-metrics.pl` computes the A to Z status metrics of a port from a model
repository's git history. GitLab analytics adds the flow view on top: the same project seen through its merge
requests and pipelines. When two sources disagree, believe the one that is closest to the work, and find out why.

=== You can now {#analytics-can-now}

- name the GitLab analytics views, what each measures and roughly which tier it needs;
- get the same numbers on the Free tier with `gitlab.pl analytics`, by sprint, with CSV output;
- translate the DORA measures into model terms, and say which belong in front of management;
- spot the traps of small numbers and mismatched definitions before management does.

=== Applying this to your project {#analytics-applying}

Decide the three flow numbers you will report before the first sprint, write them into your scorecard, and run
`gitlab.pl analytics --by sprint` at every sprint end into the sprint's evidence folder, as Tidewater did. Do not
buy a tier for its dashboards until you have used the Free-tier report for a few sprints and know which questions
you keep asking. If your license includes the paid views, use them for the team's own retrospectives and keep the
briefing to the few numbers you defined in advance.
