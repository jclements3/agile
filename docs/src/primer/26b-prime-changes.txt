== The prime's model changes: receive, assess, disposition {#prime-changes}

::: survival The prime keeps changing the model you just ported
What you have: a SysML v2 model the team built from the prime's Cameo export, a contract that says the prime
maintains the authoritative v1 model, and a calendar with a new drop on it every quarter.

What you need: a way to take each drop in without losing a week, without losing your own work, and without
accidentally accepting a change nobody authorized. This chapter is that way, end to end, with every command run
against real drops.
:::

The port was never a one-time conversion. Northwind Marine Systems, the prime, kept its Cameo model and kept
changing it: a new sensor, a new operating mode, a deleted requirement, a renamed activity, test results from its
lab. Seven drops arrive over the eighteen months of the Tidewater project, and every one of them lands on a model
that Jim, Bob and Tom have been editing in the meantime.

In the document era this was the problem everybody dreaded and nobody owned. A new revision of the SRS arrived as
a PDF with change bars, somebody compared it by eye with the old one, and the differences that mattered to the
government's own documents were found weeks later, often at a review, often by the customer. Bob remembered it
well:

> Rev D of the ICD came in on a Friday. By the next Friday I had found eleven changes. The twelfth one we found at
> the PDR, in front of the program manager.
> -- Bob, at the sprint 6 retrospective

This chapter shows the loop the team built instead. It has six steps, each with a tool, and each step produces a
record someone can audit later:

1. **Receive** the drop and check it is exactly what the prime sent.
2. **Archive** it unchanged, where nobody can edit it.
3. **Compare** it with the previous drop, element by element, by the prime's own element IDs.
4. **Assess** what each change touches in the government's model.
5. **Disposition** each significant change at the change board, as an issue with a decision.
6. **Implement** the accepted changes through ordinary merge requests, and report problems back to the prime.

Before any of that, there is one question that is not about tools at all.

=== Contract first {#prime-changes-contract}

**A change in the prime's model is not direction.** This is the single most important sentence in this chapter.
The prime maintains its Cameo model under a contract data requirement (CDRL A003 on Tidewater). When the prime adds
a hygrometer to the sensor suite in its model, that tells you what the prime has done. It does not tell the
government team to do anything. If Bob updates the government model because "the prime's model changed," and the
change was never approved through the contract, Bob has just spent government effort on work nobody authorized, and
possibly accepted a change the program office would have rejected.

So before the first drop, three questions go to the program office and the contracting officer's representative,
in writing:

- **What is the prime's model, contractually?** Is it the authoritative source for requirements and interfaces,
  or supporting data? On many programs the specifications and interface control documents still govern, and the
  model is a deliverable that illustrates them. On Tidewater the System Requirements Specification in DOORS
  governs; the Cameo model is "supporting data, maintained by the contractor."
- **Which version is on contract?** The drop that was delivered last is not necessarily the one the program
  accepted. Tidewater's rule: a drop becomes the reference only after the change board has dispositioned it.
- **Through which channel do changes become direction?** A contract modification, technical direction letter,
  an approved engineering change proposal, or an interface change approved by the interface control working group.
  The drop itself is never the channel.

Jim's answer to the third question became the rule the team works by: **every drop goes through the change board,
with the program office and contracts represented, before anything in it changes the government model.** The board
is small on Tidewater (Jim and the deputy program manager), and it meets in GitLab, but it exists, and its
decisions are recorded.

::: objection "We are the government. We can just change our model."
Bob said it in sprint 2: the government team owns the v2 model, so why ask anybody's permission to keep it in step
with the prime?

Answer: you can change your model whenever you like. What you cannot do is let the prime's model decide what your
model says without a decision on record. If the prime renames an activity, follows it with three more changes,
and one of them conflicts with a requirement the program office cares about, the record has to show who looked at
it and what they decided. The change board costs Jim and the deputy program manager twenty minutes per drop. The
alternative is an audit finding, or an unfunded change.
:::

==== The model exchange agreement {#prime-changes-agreement}

The second thing to settle before the first drop is how drops arrive. Tidewater wrote it down as a short model
exchange agreement, a page in the repository's `docs/` folder that both sides signed in week zero. It is the
interface control document for the interface between two organizations' engineering processes, and like any ICD it
says what crosses, in what format, how often, and what happens when something is wrong:

{{include: tools/gitlab/templates/docs/model-exchange-agreement.md | lines 1-40}}

Two clauses in that agreement made every later step possible, and they are worth asking for on any program:

- **Stable element IDs.** Cameo gives every element an ID (`xmi:id`). If the prime's export keeps those IDs from
  one drop to the next, a program can compare drops element by element and know that a renamed activity is the
  same activity. If the export generates new IDs each time, every comparison degrades to guessing by name. The
  prime's modeler agreed in week zero; it cost them a checkbox in the export settings.
- **Change notes with every drop.** A list of the prime's own change requests, each with an ID, a one-line
  rationale and the elements it touches. The prime already had this list for its own change control; the
  agreement just asks for it to be delivered.

=== Receive: what arrived, and is it what was sent? {#prime-changes-receive}

To follow along you need drops. The kit's generator writes the same seven drops the Tidewater team received, each
with the prime's manifest and change list. Make the first five:

```sh run sandbox=scratch expect="CHANGES\.csv" timeout=180
for n in 1 2 3 4 5; do perl "$KIT/sim/tidewater-v1.pl" --drop $n drops/d$n > /dev/null; done
ls drops/d2
```

A drop is four or five files: the Cameo model as XMI (`tidewater.xmi`), the DOORS modules as ReqIF and as CSV, the
prime's change list (`CHANGES.csv`), and a manifest. From drop 4 on there are also the prime's test results. The
manifest is the transmittal record. Read it first, every time:

```sh run expect="NWM-CR-0012"
cat drops/d2/MANIFEST.txt
```

It names the delivery (version, date, CDRL), who built it and with what (the prime's Jenkins job and build number,
its repository commit, the tool versions), the change requests in this drop, and a SHA-256 checksum for every file.
The checksums are what make "is this what was sent?" a question with a yes-or-no answer instead of an opinion.
Check them before anyone opens a file:

```sh run expect="tidewater\.xmi: OK"
cd drops/d2
sed -n '/^Files/,$p' MANIFEST.txt | tr -d '\r' | awk 'NF == 2 { print $1 "  " $2 }' | sha256sum -c
```

(The `tr -d '\r'` is there because the prime's Windows build writes the manifest with Windows line endings; without
it `sha256sum` looks for a file whose name ends in an invisible carriage return.) Every line must say `OK`. A single `FAILED` means the file is not what the prime's build produced: it was damaged
in transit, edited on the way, or the manifest itself is wrong. Either way the drop is not imported. The team does
not fix it; the prime sends it again.

::: tip Why a checksum and not a careful look
A checksum is a fingerprint of the file's exact bytes. Change one character anywhere in a 120 KB XMI file and the
fingerprint changes completely. It is the same idea as the configuration audit's check that the delivered
configuration matches the documented one, done by a program in a second instead of by a person in an afternoon.
:::

=== Archive: keep it exactly as delivered {#prime-changes-archive}

The received drop is evidence. Months later someone will ask "what exactly did the prime deliver on 2 April?", and
the answer has to be the files themselves, not a converted copy that has since been edited. Tidewater keeps every
drop in two places, neither of which anybody edits:

- **The vendor branch** (`vendor/prime`) of the model repository. Each drop is converted there, as delivered, and
  committed with the raw files under `imports/<version>/`. Nobody commits anything else to that branch; its
  protection rule allows only the configuration manager to push, and nobody to merge into it. The vendor branch
  is the reason a new drop can be merged into a model the team has been editing: git compares the new drop with
  the old drop *as converted*, and carries only the prime's changes across. [[git]] explains the idea, and
  practice lesson 12 lets you do it.
- **The Generic Package Registry** of the GitLab project, as `prime-drop/<version>`. The pipeline's `drop-package`
  job files every drop there automatically, with its checksum, so the CDRL deliverable sits next to the model it
  became and can be downloaded by version.

If your program's access rules for the prime's data differ from those for the model, the archive moves to its own
restricted project and the model project references it by version. The loop stays the same.

=== Compare: what changed, element by element {#prime-changes-delta}

Converting a drop is the easy part. The hard question is what changed since the last drop, because that is what
the team has to assess. The converter answers it directly from the two XMI files, matching elements by the prime's
IDs:

```sh run expect="7 added, 0 removed, 1 changed"
perl "$KIT/bin/xmi2sysml.pl" --delta drops/d1/tidewater.xmi drops/d2/tidewater.xmi | head -25
```

Read the first four lines before anything else. They say how big the change is, and they split it in two:

- **Carried by the converter.** Re-converting the drop reproduces the change in SysML v2 text. The vendor-branch
  merge brings it across without anyone retyping it.
- **Manual.** The converter does not carry this kind of element (state machines, interactions, constraint
  expressions, which it leaves as TODO lines). A person has to rework the government's v2 text by hand. These are
  the expensive changes, and the report lists them at the end as a checklist.

Drop 2 is all carried. Drop 3 is not. Its change to the buoy's operating modes (a new Storm state) sits inside a
state machine, which the converter leaves as a TODO; the government team wrote the state machine by hand in
sprint 6. Look at the manual list:

```sh run expect="manual rework list"
perl "$KIT/bin/xmi2sysml.pl" --delta drops/d2/tidewater.xmi drops/d3/tidewater.xmi | sed -n '1,4p;/manual rework list/,$p'
```

Five manual items, all inside `BuoyModes`: the Storm state, two transitions, and their two triggers. Without the
element-level comparison this change would have been invisible: the converted v2 text of drop 3 is the same as
drop 2's around the state machine, because both just say TODO there. A diff of the converted files would have shown
nothing. This is the strongest argument for stable IDs and an element-level delta.

::: note Why compare v1 with v1, not v2 with v2
It is tempting to convert both drops and diff the SysML v2 text. That catches everything the converter carries and
misses everything it does not, which is exactly the part that needs a person. The comparison has to happen on the
prime's model, in the prime's terms, with the converter's coverage reported per change.
:::

=== Assess: what does it touch in our model? {#prime-changes-impact}

The delta says what changed in the prime's model. The change board needs to know what that touches in the
government's model: which requirements, which verification cases, which interfaces, and which hand edits the team
made that now need redoing. That is impact analysis, and it needs one more piece of information: where each of the
prime's elements ended up in the government's v2 text.

The converter writes that as a map when it converts a drop. Start the government's model from drop 1, laid out in
layers with the same layout map the team uses ([[repo-layout]]), and keep the map:

```sh run expect="206 of 206 in scope ported"
mkdir -p maps
perl "$KIT/bin/xmi2sysml.pl" --out gov/model --layout-map "$KIT/examples/tidewater-v1/layout.map" \
    --map maps/v1.0-map.csv drops/d1/tidewater.xmi
head -4 maps/v1.0-map.csv
```

Each row ties one of the prime's elements (its `xmi_id`) to the v2 name, kind, file and line it became. Now ask
the question the board needs answered, for drop 2:

```sh run expect="requirements touched +3"
perl "$KIT/bin/xmi2sysml.pl" --delta drops/d1/tidewater.xmi drops/d2/tidewater.xmi \
    --impact gov/model --map maps/v1.0-map.csv --impact-csv impact-v1.1.csv | sed -n '/impact summary/,$p'
```

The summary is the change board's agenda in eight lines: three requirements touched (the new humidity requirement
TW-SYS-017, the stakeholder need it derives from, and TW-SYS-006, whose wording the prime aligned with DOORS), two
verification cases, one interface and one allocation, grouped under three of the prime's change requests. Above
the summary, the full report walks through each change request with its rationale and every v2 element it touches,
with file and line, so a reviewer can open exactly those lines.

==== Change requests: the prime's own reasons {#prime-changes-crs}

Because the drop carries the prime's change list, the impact report is grouped by the prime's change requests
rather than by element. That turns a list of forty element changes into three or four decisions:

```sh run expect="NWM-CR-0025"
cat drops/d5/CHANGES.csv
```

Drop 5 has two change requests. NWM-CR-0024 adds a reserve term to the energy balance constraint, with a reason.
NWM-CR-0025 renames the activity `PublishBulletin` to `IssueBulletin`, and the rationale column is empty. The
impact report says so plainly:

```sh run expect="without a rationale +1 +NWM-CR-0025"
perl "$KIT/bin/xmi2sysml.pl" --delta drops/d4/tidewater.xmi drops/d5/tidewater.xmi --impact gov/model \
    | sed -n '/^== NWM-CR-0025/,/^$/p;/impact summary/,$p'
```

A rename with no reason is not something to accept by reflex. Names in the model are referenced from requirement
text, from test procedures, from the government's own hand-written behavior and from documents outside the model.
The board needs to know why before it agrees. That case, a change request with no rationale, is the textbook
reason for the "reject or clarify" disposition below.

==== Hand edits that need redoing {#prime-changes-hand-edits}

The most valuable line in the impact summary is the last one: **hand edits to redo**. The government team closes
the converter's TODO lines by hand, writing the state machines, interactions and constraint expressions the
converter could not carry. When the prime then changes one of those constructs, the vendor-branch merge brings
nothing across (the converter carried nothing), and the team's hand-written version silently goes stale. The impact
analysis looks for exactly that: a TODO line the converter would write, which is no longer in the government's
model because someone replaced it.

Play the part of Bob in sprint 6, closing the `BuoyModes` TODO by hand:

```sh run expect="exhibit state buoyModes"
perl -i -pe 's{^(\s*)// TODO uml:StateMachine .BuoyModes.*$}{$1exhibit state buoyModes : BuoyModes;   // written by hand}' \
    gov/model/physical/stations/Stations.sysml
grep -n "buoyModes" gov/model/physical/stations/Stations.sysml
```

Then drop 3 arrives, with the prime's new Storm state:

```sh run expect="hand edits to redo +1"
perl "$KIT/bin/xmi2sysml.pl" --delta drops/d2/tidewater.xmi drops/d3/tidewater.xmi --impact gov/model \
    | sed -n '/impact summary/,$p'
```

The report names the hand edit, the file and the line: Bob's state machine needs a Storm state, and nothing else in
the loop would have said so. On Tidewater this became a task in the drop-3 intake story, assigned to the person who
wrote the original edit.

=== Disposition: one issue per significant change {#prime-changes-disposition}

The impact report is the input to the change board. The board's output is a decision per change request, recorded
where the work happens: in GitLab issues. Tidewater opens one issue per drop (the intake, from the Prime Drop
template) and one issue per significant change request, labeled `prime-change`, with exactly one of four
disposition labels:

|| Disposition | Meaning | What happens next ||
| `disposition::accept` | Accepted as delivered | The vendor-branch merge brings it in; nothing more |
| `disposition::accept-with-impact` | Accepted, and it adds government work | Linked tasks for the extra work; the cost and schedule effect noted on the issue |
| `disposition::reject-clarify` | Not accepted yet | A discrepancy report asks the prime for the rationale or a correction; the issue stays open until the prime answers |
| `disposition::no-impact` | Cosmetic | Nothing for the government model to do |
Table: The four dispositions a change request can get

On Tidewater, drop 4's drifting buoys were accepted with impact (new verification cases, a new part of the station
segment to review); drop 5's unexplained rename was sent back for clarification; most change requests were simply
accepted; and the documentation edits in drop 7 had no impact. Which drops and change requests got which
disposition is part of the simulation's later sprints, and the counts belong to its evidence rather than to this
page.

The issues themselves take a minute to open. Create a scratch GitLab and the project from the team's project file:

```sh run sandbox=scratch expect="protected branch main"
gl() { perl "$KIT/bin/gitlab.pl" --local gitlab --project tidewater/model "$@"; }
for u in jim bob tom; do gl user $u; done
gl --as tom setup "$KIT/tools/gitlab/templates/project.conf" | grep -E "^(created|protected branch main)"
```

Then open the intake issue and one issue per change request, straight from the impact CSV the converter wrote:

```sh run expect="NWM-CR-0014"
gl() { perl "$KIT/bin/gitlab.pl" --local gitlab --project tidewater/model "$@"; }
gl --as jim issue new "Prime drop v1.1: intake and disposition" --label type::drop --label prime-change
perl -ne 'chomp; my @c = split /,/; print "$c[-1]\n" if $. > 1 && $c[-1]' impact-v1.1.csv | sort -u |
while read cr; do
  title=$(grep "^$cr," drops/d2/CHANGES.csv | cut -d, -f2)
  gl --as jim issue new "$cr $title" --label prime-change --description "Drop v1.1. See the impact report on the intake issue."
done
gl issue list --label prime-change
```

Each disposition is a quick action in a comment, so the decision, who made it and when are all in the issue's
history:

```sh run expect="disposition::accept"
gl() { perl "$KIT/bin/gitlab.pl" --local gitlab --project tidewater/model "$@"; }
gl --as jim issue note 2 '/label ~"disposition::accept"'
gl issue show 2 | head -3
```

The deputy program manager concurs in a comment on the intake issue. That comment is the change board's minutes.

::: objection "This is a lot of ceremony for a sensor."
Tom raised it at the drop-2 intake: three issues and a board decision for one hygrometer and a requirement.

Answer: the ceremony is proportional. A drop with three change requests takes three issues and one twenty-minute
conversation. The issues are generated from the impact report, so nobody types the list. And the record is what
lets the team answer, six months later, "when did we accept the humidity requirement, and who agreed?" in one
search instead of an archaeology project. When a drop is truly trivial, the board dispositions everything as
accept in one comment, and the intake closes the same day.
:::

==== prime-tbd: when you cannot wait for the drop {#prime-changes-tbd}

Sometimes the government team needs a change before the prime delivers it. In sprint 6 the team knew from the
prime's modeler that a humidity sensor was coming, and Bob needed something in the model for the data exchange
table. Waiting for drop 2 would have stalled the table for a month. So Bob modeled an interim `HumiditySensor` on
develop, and the team opened an issue labeled `prime-tbd`: work that rests on an assumption about the prime's next
drop, closed when the drop lands.

When drop 2 arrived, the vendor-branch merge conflicted exactly where Bob's interim sensor sat, which is the
point: the conflict forces the decision. The team kept the prime's hygrometer, deleted the interim, and closed the
`prime-tbd` issue in the same merge request. Practice lesson 12 replays that merge, and you can run its first steps
here:

```sh run sandbox=practice-12 cwd=work expect="conflicts:" timeout=240
. ../env.sh
perl "$KIT/bin/xmi2sysml.pl" --delta ../drops/v1.0/tidewater.xmi ../drops/v1.1/tidewater.xmi | head -4
gl flow vendor ../drops/v1.1 v1.1 || echo "exit status $?"
```

The conflict is the system working: two teams changed the same thing, and git stopped to ask a person which one
wins. Finish the lesson to resolve it in Vim (`agile.pl practice 12`).

=== Implement, and report back {#prime-changes-implement}

Accepted changes reach the government model through the normal merge request flow, nothing special:

- The vendor-branch import and its merge into a `feature/drop-<version>` branch are done by the configuration
  manager (`gitlab.pl flow vendor`), and the merge request carries the delta and impact reports in its pipeline
  (the `delta` job runs on drop branches automatically).
- Each hand edit to redo becomes a task, linked to its change request issue, and goes in on its own branch with its
  own review. The merge request description cites the prime's change request ID, so the history reads
  "Storm state added to BuoyModes (NWM-CR-0015)."
- Conflicts are resolved upstream-first: the prime's version wins, then the government's edit is reapplied on top
  of it. Never the other way around, or the next drop will conflict again.

Problems the team finds go back to the prime as **discrepancy reports**: GitLab issues labeled
`type::discrepancy` and `owner::prime`, transmitted through the contracting channel with the memo template in
`docs/memos/templates/` ([[memos]]), and kept open until a later drop fixes them. The prime's change requests cite
them when they do, which closes the loop visibly. Drop 3's NWM-CR-0018 and NWM-CR-0019 say "Answers government DR"
in their rationale; drop 4's NWM-CR-0022 brings TW-SYS-016 into the model in answer to another. The discrepancy
report numbers in this paragraph are the ones the prime's change notes cite; the simulation's issue numbers for
them are in its evidence.

==== Returning the government's model {#prime-changes-return}

The exchange runs both ways. When the prime needs the government's design data (at a baseline, or for an interface
the government owns), the team returns it as a controlled deliverable: built from a baseline tag, never from a
working branch, with a transmittal record just like an incoming drop. Here is the mechanics, on a scaffolded model
repository:

```sh run sandbox=scratch expect="sha256:"
perl "$KIT/tools/gitlab/kickoff.pl" scaffold model --project tidewater/model --title Tidewater > /dev/null
cd model
git tag -a baseline-0 -m "baseline-0 (practice)"
mkdir -p ../deliverable
git archive --format=tar.gz --prefix=tidewater-model-baseline-0/ -o ../deliverable/tidewater-model-baseline-0.tar.gz baseline-0 model docs
cd ../deliverable && echo "sha256: $(sha256sum tidewater-model-baseline-0.tar.gz)"
```

On Tidewater the pipeline does this on every baseline tag (the `publish-package` job puts the archive, the
verification matrix and the toolchain inventory in the package registry), and the transmittal memo goes out with
it. Be honest with the prime about one limit: **the kit has no SysML v2 to SysML v1 converter.** The government
returns SysML v2 text, the generated tables and the verification matrix. Whether the prime's Cameo can import SysML
v2 text depends on the prime's tool version; settle it in the model exchange agreement, not on delivery day.

=== Two strategies at the v1-to-v2 boundary {#prime-changes-strategies}

Tidewater mirrors the prime's whole model: every drop is converted in full onto the vendor branch, and the
government's model is that conversion plus the government's edits. There is a second strategy, and on many programs
it is the better one.

|| | Full mirror (Tidewater) | Interface surface ||
| What is converted | the whole v1 model, every drop | only what you consume: your allocated requirements, your external interfaces, the parameters you depend on |
| Where it lives | the government model itself, layered folders | one generated package (for example `model/external/prime/`), never hand-edited |
| How your design uses it | you edit the converted text in place, closing TODO lines | your design satisfies and connects to elements of the generated package |
| What a new drop does | a 3-way merge on the vendor branch; conflicts where both sides edited | the package is regenerated; validate and trace checks show what broke |
| Best when | the government needs the whole system model in v2 (the port is the goal) | you are a subsystem or segment team consuming a prime's model |
Table: Mirroring the whole model versus translating only the interface surface

Tidewater chose the full mirror because the government's goal was the system model itself. It already uses the
interface-surface pattern in one place: the DOORS modules. They are converted by script into
`model/requirements/` on every drop and never hand-edited; the government's design satisfies them. If your team
owns a subsystem and the prime owns the system, start with the interface surface: it is smaller, the generated
package never conflicts, and the boundary is explicit.

=== The prime's Jenkins: a delivery interface {#jenkins}

Northwind builds its drops in Jenkins, a continuous integration server older than GitLab's and still common in
industry. The government team uses GitLab. Nobody on Jim's team needs to run Jenkins, and the government never asks
the prime to migrate. What the team does need is to understand what Jenkins hands them, and to agree how.

==== Jenkins in GitLab terms {#jenkins-terms}

Most Jenkins words have a GitLab counterpart. You need them to read the prime's build records and to talk to the
prime's build engineer:

|| Jenkins | GitLab | In systems-engineering words ||
| Jenkinsfile | `.gitlab-ci.yml` | the procedure, written down and versioned |
| agent (a build machine, by label) | runner (by tag) | the bench the procedure runs on |
| stage / steps | stage / `script` | the procedure's sections and lines |
| `environment { }` | `variables:` | the procedure's settings |
| `withCredentials` | a masked CI/CD variable | a credential handed over for one step, never written down |
| JUnit plugin (`junit 'results.xml'`) | `artifacts: reports: junit:` | test results read into a trend |
| `archiveArtifacts ... fingerprint: true` | job artifacts, package registry | the delivered items, kept with their checksums |
| build number, job URL | pipeline ID, pipeline URL | the record's serial number |
| shared workspace between stages | a fresh workspace per job | GitLab starts each job clean; pass files on as artifacts |
Table: Jenkins and GitLab, side by side

The last row is the one that surprises people moving between the two: a Jenkins job usually keeps one working
folder for all its stages, while GitLab gives each job a fresh checkout. [[cicd]] explains why.

==== Three ways a drop can arrive {#jenkins-patterns}

From least to most automated, these are the delivery patterns a model exchange agreement can choose between:

1. **File delivery.** The prime's Jenkins builds the drop and archives it; someone transfers the files under the
   CDRL to approved government storage; Tom runs the intake by hand. This is how Tidewater's first three drops
   arrived, and it is the right place to start: nobody has to grant anybody network access.
2. **Push into GitLab.** The prime's Jenkins uploads the drop to the government project's Generic Package
   Registry, using a deploy token that can do nothing but write packages, and then fires a pipeline trigger with
   the drop's version. The government's `intake` job runs on its own. Tidewater switched to this from drop 4.
3. **GitLab pulls from Jenkins.** A scheduled government job fetches the prime's last successful build over the
   prime's API. Possible, and usually blocked: it needs a network path from the government enclave into the
   contractor's, and credentials for the prime's server held by the government. Few security officers approve it.

The illustrative Jenkinsfile in the kit shows what pattern 2 looks like on the prime's side. The kit does not run
it (there is no Jenkins here); read it for the shape:

{{include: tools/gitlab/templates/prime/Jenkinsfile | lines 1-17}}

==== Two tokens, each able to do one thing {#jenkins-tokens}

Pattern 2 needs two credentials, created by a maintainer of the government project and handed to the prime
through the contracting channel. Each can do exactly one thing, and each expires:

```sh run expect="write_package_registry"
gl() { perl "$KIT/bin/gitlab.pl" --local gitlab --project tidewater/model "$@"; }
gl --as tom token deploy new prime-jenkins --scope write_package_registry --expires 2027-12-31
```

The deploy token can upload packages. It cannot read the model, push code, open issues or see anything else. If
it leaks, the worst an attacker can do is upload a fake drop, which the intake job will reject because its
checksums will not match the manifest the prime's build wrote.

```sh run expect="glptt-"
gl() { perl "$KIT/bin/gitlab.pl" --local gitlab --project tidewater/model "$@"; }
gl --as tom trigger new "prime Jenkins delivery"
```

The trigger token can start a pipeline and pass it variables. It cannot change anything. The triggered pipeline
runs the government's own `intake` job, as the token's owner, with the government's rules. Both tokens live in
Jenkins' credential store on the prime's side, and in the government's token inventory ([[admin-tokens]]) with an
owner and an expiry date.

With the deploy token, the prime's build uploads the drop; in the scratch GitLab you can play that part:

```sh run expect="prime-drop/v1.3"
gl() { perl "$KIT/bin/gitlab.pl" --local gitlab --project tidewater/model "$@"; }
for f in drops/d4/*; do gl --as tom package put prime-drop v1.3 "$f" > /dev/null; done
gl package list
```

==== The intake job: the government's gate {#jenkins-intake}

The triggered pipeline runs one job: `intake`. It is in the team's pipeline file, and short:

{{include: tools/gitlab/templates/gitlab-ci.yml | lines 262-276}}

It fetches every file of the drop, checks each one against the checksum in the manifest the prime's build wrote,
keeps the drop as received (an artifact that never expires: it is formal evidence), and opens the Prime Drop issue.
A green build on the prime's side is evidence that the prime's checks passed. It is not acceptance. Acceptance is
the government's own gates on the import merge request, and the board's disposition.

You can run the intake exactly as the trigger would, locally, against a folder instead of the registry. It needs a
model repository with the team's pipeline, which the scaffold you made above has:

```sh run expect="pipeline: success" timeout=120
cd model
perl "$KIT/bin/gitlab.pl" ci --event trigger --branch develop --job intake \
    --var DROP_VERSION=v1.3 --var INTAKE_DROP_DIR="$PWD/../drops/d4" --logs ../intake-logs
grep -E "^(ok|ACCEPTED)" ../intake-logs/intake.log
```

Now the case the intake exists for: a file changed somewhere between the prime's build and the government. One
line added to the XMI, the kind of edit nobody would notice by eye:

```sh run expect="REJECTED" timeout=120
cp -r drops/d4 drops/d4-altered && echo "<!-- edited in transit -->" >> drops/d4-altered/tidewater.xmi
cd model
perl "$KIT/bin/gitlab.pl" ci --event trigger --branch develop --job intake \
    --var DROP_VERSION=v1.3 --var INTAKE_DROP_DIR="$PWD/../drops/d4-altered" --logs ../intake-bad \
    || echo "exit status $?"
grep -E "^(MISMATCH|REJECTED)" ../intake-bad/intake.log
```

The pipeline fails, the log names the file and both checksums, and the drop is not imported. The team tells the
prime, and the prime's build sends it again. Nobody spends an afternoon working out what changed.

==== The ICD between the two pipelines {#jenkins-icd}

Everything above is an interface between two organizations' processes, and it deserves the same treatment as any
other interface: written down, agreed, and changed under control. Tidewater's version is a section of the model
exchange agreement. Its checklist, which you can lift for your own program:

::: checklist The pipeline interface, agreed with the prime
[ ] What crosses: the file list per drop (XMI, ReqIF, CSV, CHANGES.csv, results, MANIFEST.txt)
[ ] Formats and versions: Cameo version and export settings (stable IDs on), DOORS export options
[ ] The manifest fields: version, date, CDRL, Jenkins job URL and build number, prime repository commit, tool versions, a checksum per file, the change requests
[ ] How it crosses: file delivery, or registry upload plus trigger (and who may change the pattern)
[ ] Who holds which credential: the deploy token and the trigger token, their scopes, expiry and rotation
[ ] What the government does on receipt: the intake checks, the board, the disposition time
[ ] What happens when something is wrong: rejected drop, resend, discrepancy reports and their return path
[ ] Markings: the prime's proprietary and export markings, preserved through every conversion
:::

=== Cautions {#prime-changes-cautions}

A few things went wrong, or nearly did, on Tidewater and on programs like it:

- **You will probably still need a Cameo seat.** If the prime delivers `.mdzip` project files instead of XMI, or
  if an export goes wrong and someone has to look at the original, the government needs at least one Cameo licence
  to open it. Budget for one during the transition rather than discovering the need on drop day. The model
  exchange agreement should ask for XMI and ReqIF so the seat is rarely used.
- **The prime's markings travel with the data.** Proprietary and export-control markings in the prime's model
  apply to every conversion of it: the v2 text, the generated documents, the packages. Preserve them, mark the
  vendor branch and the archive accordingly, and restrict access to the people the markings allow. If those
  people are fewer than the model team, the archive goes in its own project ([[security]]).
- **Don't freeze waiting for the prime.** If a drop is late, keep working against the last accepted one, and
  record every assumption about the next drop as a `prime-tbd` issue. The conflict at the next merge will make the
  team revisit each one.
- **Never edit the vendor branch by hand.** It is tempting to "just fix" a converter quirk there. The next drop's
  import overwrites it, or worse, merges around it. Fix converter problems in the converter, and government
  changes on develop.
- **Keep the drop loop's records in GitLab, not in email.** The intake issue, the change-request issues, the
  dispositions and the discrepancy reports are the configuration management record of the boundary. An email
  thread with the prime's modeler is fine for the conversation; the decision goes in the issue.

=== Applying this to your project {#prime-changes-apply}

- **Before the first drop:** get the three contract answers in writing, write the model exchange agreement, and ask
  for stable IDs and change notes. Those two requests cost the supplier almost nothing and make everything else in
  this chapter possible.
- **Choose your boundary strategy:** full mirror if the model itself is your deliverable, interface surface if you
  consume someone else's model.
- **Start with file delivery,** move to registry-and-trigger once the intake job has proven itself on a few drops.
- **Copy the six steps** (receive, archive, compare, assess, disposition, implement) and the four disposition
  labels. The tools are in the kit; the discipline is the change board meeting on every drop.
- **Measure the loop:** the time from delivery to merged on the integration branch, the share of change requests
  with a disposition within a week, and the open discrepancy reports. These are the numbers that tell management
  the boundary is under control.

::: exercise {#ex-prime-delta} Read a drop like a change board
Generate drops 3 and 4 (`perl "$KIT/sim/tidewater-v1.pl" --drop N DIR`) and run the delta between them with
`--impact` against a model converted from drop 3. For each change request, propose a disposition and write one
sentence of justification. Which change adds government work, and what work?
:::

::: exercise {#ex-prime-reject} Write the clarification
Draft the discrepancy report for NWM-CR-0025 (the rename with no rationale) from the Discrepancy Report issue
template: what you need from the prime, why the rename matters to the government model, and by which drop you need
the answer.
:::

::: exercise {#ex-prime-tamper} Break the intake on purpose
Copy a drop, change one character in `tidewater.reqif`, and run the intake job locally against the copy. Then fix
the copy by regenerating the file instead. What does the intake report each time, and why is "regenerate" the only
acceptable fix?
:::

::: exercise {#ex-prime-boundary} Choose a strategy
Your team owns the data center segment; the prime owns the system model. Sketch which elements of the Tidewater
model would go in an `external/prime` package under the interface-surface strategy, and which the data center
team would own.
:::
