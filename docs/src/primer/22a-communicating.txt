== Telling management {#communicating}

::: survival Before you start
What you have: a journal of stories, a GitLab project, and a program manager who will not open either.

What you need: a rhythm of short reports, each built by the system from the same records, so that management sees
progress every week and the December briefing is a summary rather than a surprise.
:::

The briefing in [[briefing]] worked because nothing in it was new to the program manager. Every number on its slides
had appeared, a few at a time, in reports that arrived every Friday and every second Friday for twelve weeks. This
chapter is about that rhythm: what each report is, what is in it, how it is produced, and how the team learned to
write the parts a program cannot produce.

=== Three rhythms {#communicating-rhythms}

Jim agreed three kinds of report with the program manager in week zero ([[directive-management]]):

|| Report | When | What is in it | Built from ||
| Weekly note and quad | every Friday | one paragraph of headline, three numbers, risks, asks; the one-page quad behind it | the journal (`daily.pl quad`), GitLab (`talk.pl weekly`) |
| Sprint review | every second Friday | what was finished, shown live; the four headline numbers; the ask | GitLab, the journal, the pipeline; the demo script |
| Program review | end of sprint 6, then quarterly | the scorecard, before and after, a demonstration, the ask | everything above ([[briefing]]) |
Table: The reporting rhythm agreed in week zero.

The rule behind all three: the system produces the numbers, a person writes the sentences. Nobody types a count into
a report.

=== The weekly quad {#communicating-quad}

The quad is a one-page weekly status the program already used before Tidewater, in a slide template the team's lab had
used for years. The template, *Quad Explanation*, describes four quadrants under a "Weekly Status" title with the lab's
name and an "As of" date. The kit's `daily.pl quad` produces the same page from the journal. Here is the template's
meaning, element by element, and where each element comes from in the kit.

==== The four quadrants

|| Quadrant | What the template says it holds | What the kit puts there ||
| Technical Priorities | the current sprint backlog: every task, tagged with its state | this sprint's stories from the journal, each with a four-letter word, points and owner |
| Watch Items / PM Help Needed | tasks with a blocker or that could not be completed on time, marked for the program manager | blockers older than three days as (PM), fresh blockers, holds, carryovers and unassigned work as (WI) |
| Schedule Milestones | the 30-60-90 day backlog: what is due 30, 60 and 90 days out, priority 1 to 3 | epics whose forecast finish falls in each window, with progress and the forecast date |
| Accomplishments | items from the DONE list, marked on time or late | stories done this week, marked on time unless they were ever carried over or reworked |
Table: The quad's quadrants, the template and the kit.

==== The four-letter words

The Technical Priorities quadrant tags every item with one word, so the program manager can read the state of the
sprint at a glance:

|| Word | Meaning in the template ||
| TODO | a task that needs to be completed: on the backlog, not started |
| OPEN | a task that has begun |
| DONE | a completed task |
| WAIT | a task that cannot proceed because of a blocker outside the team |
| HOLD | an open task interrupted by a higher priority |
| PASS [team] | handed to another team, to overcome a blocker or because they can do it better |
| DROP | a task that should not be done after all |
| REDO | done, but the demonstration showed it was wrong, so it is back in the sprint |
| PUNT | too hard as written: back to the backlog to be re-cut |
| SYNC | coordinated across teams, with a shared DONE |
Table: The quad's four-letter words.

The stand-up verbs map onto them: `hold` and `resume`, `punt`, `redo`, `pass`, `sync` in the stand-up file set the
word the quad prints ([[journal]]).

==== The two SWAT marks

Watch items carry one of two marks, and the difference matters to a program manager:

- **(PM) SWAT**: the program manager needs to *help remove* a blocker. This is an ask.
- **(WI) SWAT**: the program manager needs to *be aware* of an item. This is information.

The kit raises an item to (PM) when a blocker is older than three days or a team is loaded over a hundred and ten
percent of its capacity; everything else on the watch list is (WI).

==== The legend

The template's legend has three groups. Schedule movement: *pushed right* (a milestone slipped later), *pulled left*
(it moved earlier). Issue level: *minor or no issues*, *significant issues requiring PM awareness*, *show stopper
issues*. Trend: *improving*, *degrading*, *no change*. The kit prints the first and third group as marks (`->`, `<-`,
`=`, and the sprint progress trend in the header). It does not draw the three issue levels; the (PM) and (WI) marks
carry most of that meaning, and a show stopper on a three-person team is a phone call, not a quad entry.

==== The two metrics

The template's second slide makes the quad do double duty as a metrics report: "Weekly Status satisfies both
Metrics."

- **Sprint Progress**: the share of the current sprint backlog (the Technical Priorities quadrant) that is done. The
  kit prints it in the header, with its trend against the previous week.
- **On-Time Delivery of Milestone Products**: the share of accomplishments delivered on time, marked with a tick or a
  cross in the Accomplishments quadrant. The kit prints this week's and this sprint's counts beside it.

Read them the way the program manager did. The header gives the two metrics. Technical Priorities shows the sprint;

That empty watch list is worth a second look. In sprint 3 the toolchain review waited three days for the ISSM, and
the quad of that Friday does not show it, because the wait began and ended between two Fridays. A weekly snapshot can
miss a short block; the daily stand-up record ([[journal]]) has it.

You can produce a quad yourself from the Tidewater journal, as of any date:

```sh run sandbox=tidewater expect="TECHNICAL PRIORITIES"
perl "$KIT/bin/daily.pl" --conf journal/scrum.conf --today 2026-12-18 quad
```

The numbers will differ from the evidence copy above: the journal now holds the whole sprint, so "as of 18 December"
is computed with the later stand-ups in it. The evidence copy is the page Jim actually sent that Friday afternoon.
That is a lesson in itself: file the report you sent, not one you can regenerate.

=== The weekly note {#communicating-note}

The quad is the detail. The note is the headline: one paragraph a program manager reads on a phone between meetings.
`talk.pl weekly` drafts it from GitLab, with a conclusion as its first line ("On track," "At risk" or "Needs help"),
three numbers, the risks and the asks:

```sh run expect="Weekly note"
perl "$KIT/tools/gitlab/talk.pl" --local gitlab --project tidewater/model weekly --date 2026-12-18
```

Jim edited every draft before sending it. The risks section of this one lists the discrepancy reports to the prime
by age, each on its own line; in the note Jim sent, Jim folded them into one sentence ("our discrepancy reports to the
prime, the oldest four weeks old, all due with the January drop") because the program manager reads a list of
identical lines as that many separate problems. The draft is a starting point; the sentence is the lead's job ([[talking]]).

=== The sprint review report {#communicating-review}

Every second Friday the review notes go to the program manager and the deputy: the sprint goal, what was
demonstrated, what carried over, the artifacts shown, any feedback, and the four headline numbers. Sprint 6's review,
as filed:

{{include: docs/tidewater/sprints/06/review.txt}}

The four headline numbers were the same at every review from sprint 2 on: TODO lines left, verify coverage, open
discrepancy reports, blocked items. Keeping them identical was deliberate. By sprint 4 the deputy program manager
stopped asking what they meant and started asking why one had changed.

The reviews also recorded the feedback, including the feedback that hurt. Sprint 2's says the deputy did not follow
the demo; sprint 4's records the program manager asking whether the model site works on a phone. Both are quoted in
[[talking]], and the change between them is the reason the team rehearses.

=== How the reports changed management's questions {#communicating-effect}

In September the program manager's question about the model was "Is it done?" By November it was "Which of these
watch items needs me?" By December it was "Show me the same numbers in March." The reports did not just inform
management; they changed what management asked for, from a yes or no to a trend. That is the point of a rhythm of
small, regular, system-built reports: the conversation moves from whether the team is working to what the work
shows.

=== You can now {#communicating-can-now}

- read and explain every element of the weekly quad: quadrants, four-letter words, SWAT marks, legend and metrics;
- produce a quad from the journal and a weekly note from GitLab, and edit the draft into what you send;
- keep a sprint review report with the same headline numbers every time;
- explain why a weekly snapshot can miss a short block, and where to look instead.

=== Applying this to your project {#communicating-applying}

Use the status template your management already reads, and have the system fill it, rather than inventing a new
format they must learn. Agree the rhythm in week zero, and keep the headline numbers identical from report to report.
Let the tools draft and let a person write the sentences. File every report you send with the sprint's evidence, so
the briefing at the end of the quarter is assembled, not written.
