== The directive {#directive}

::: bridge
You know: a directive that arrives with a date and no resources, and the meeting where the team works out what it
really means.

Here it is called: the DevSecOps directive and the twelve-week scorecard.

What is new: agreeing in week zero on what will be measured, so success is a number, not an opinion.
:::

::: note What you have, what you need
**What you have:** a memo telling you to adopt something you have never done, a skeptical team and twelve weeks.

**What you need from this chapter:** a precise reading of what the memo asks, the team's objections written down so
none of them get lost, and a definition of "measurable progress" that everyone, including management, agrees to
before the work starts.
:::

=== The memo {#directive-memo}

The memo arrived on a Thursday in late September 2026, attached to an email from the program manager with the
subject line "FYSA -- DevSecOps for SE artifacts -- action required." It was two pages long. The first page is
reproduced here because the team read it so many times that Bob could later quote it from memory.

```text
MEMORANDUM FOR TIDEWATER SYSTEMS ENGINEERING

SUBJECT: Adoption of DevSecOps Practices for Systems Engineering Artifacts

1. Effective FY27, program systems engineering artifacts, including the system model, will be
   developed and maintained using DevSecOps practices on the enterprise DevSecOps platform (GitLab).

2. The Tidewater system model will be transitioned from SysML v1 to SysML v2 textual notation and
   placed under configuration control in the enterprise GitLab instance. The prime contractor's
   SysML v1 deliveries under CDRL A003 remain the contractual baseline until further notice; the
   government will maintain a derived SysML v2 model and keep it current with each delivery.

3. Systems engineering leads will demonstrate:
   a. automated quality checks on every model change;
   b. traceability from DOORS requirements to the model, computed rather than hand-maintained;
   c. peer review and approval of every change, with a complete audit record;
   d. reproducible baselines;
   e. measurable progress reported at regular intervals.

4. The Tidewater MBSE lead will brief initial results at the December program management review.

5. Tools and data flows require ISSM approval before use with program data.
```

The second page listed training resources (a link to the enterprise platform's documentation and a recorded
webinar), a point of contact in the enterprise DevSecOps office, and a sentence that Tom underlined twice: "Teams
are encouraged to start small and iterate."

=== Reading it carefully {#directive-reading}

Jim read the memo three times before showing it to anyone. On the third reading Jim did what twenty-six years of
requirements work had trained Jim to do: treat it as a specification, and separate what it requires from what it
assumes and what it leaves open.

|| The memo says | What it actually requires | What it leaves open ||
| model "developed and maintained using DevSecOps practices" | changes made, reviewed, checked and recorded through the platform | which practices; how much is enough |
| "transitioned ... to SysML v2 textual notation" | a SysML v2 text model exists and is the government's working copy | how faithful the port must be; what to do with what the converter cannot carry |
| prime's v1 "remains the contractual baseline" | the government cannot change the authoritative model | how the derived v2 model stays current with each drop |
| "automated quality checks on every model change" | a pipeline that runs on every change and blocks bad ones | which checks |
| "traceability ... computed rather than hand-maintained" | a trace report produced by a program from the model and DOORS | which measures |
| "peer review and approval of every change, with a complete audit record" | merge requests, approvals, protected branches | who approves what |
| "reproducible baselines" | tags that can be rebuilt identically | how often |
| "measurable progress reported at regular intervals" | numbers, every sprint | which numbers |
| "brief initial results at the December PMR" | a briefing in about twelve weeks | what "initial results" must show |
| "ISSM approval before use with program data" | the toolchain must be approved | how long that takes |
Table: The directive, read as a specification

The right-hand column is long. That is not a complaint about the memo. A directive from leadership is supposed to
say *what* and leave *how* to the people doing the work. But it meant that most of the important decisions were now
Jim's, and that the team would have to make them without experience. Jim wrote on the printout: "We decide the
'how'. Write every decision down, with the reason. If we're wrong, we'll at least know why."

That note became a habit, and it is why this book can explain the thought process behind each decision: the team
recorded them, first in the wiki and later in merge request descriptions.

=== The meeting with the program manager {#directive-pm}

Jim asked for thirty minutes with the program manager the next morning and brought two questions.

The first was: *what does success look like in December?* The program manager's answer was frank. "Leadership has
spent money on the platform and on licenses. They want to see that programs are using it and that it does
something. I need a story I can tell with numbers. I don't need perfection. I need progress I can measure, and I
need your team not to hate it."

The second question was: *what are we allowed to stop doing?* This mattered more than it sounded. A team of three
could not port a model, learn a new way of working and keep doing every document-era activity at the same time.
After some discussion, the program manager agreed to three things:

- the monthly screenshot-based model review package would be replaced by whatever the team could publish from the
  new system, starting when it was ready;
- the government's VCRM spreadsheet would be frozen at its current revision and replaced by the computed trace
  report once the trace report existed;
- the team would spend no time on Cameo beyond reading each drop, for the duration of the twelve weeks.

::: tip Ask what you can stop doing
Every change program adds work. Few of them say what to remove. Ask, early, in writing. The three things Jim
negotiated freed roughly a fifth of the team's time, and that time is what paid for the learning curve.
:::

=== The team meeting {#directive-team}

Jim scheduled the team meeting for Monday afternoon, 28 September, in the small conference room with the broken
projector. Jim printed the memo three times, handed out copies, and let Bob and Tom read it in silence.

Bob finished first and put the memo face down on the table.

> So we're doing it again. Three years ago it was "go MBSE." We spent two years clicking every box in the SSDD into
> Cameo. Now it's "go DevSecOps," and we get to do it all again in a different tool. What happens in three years?
> -- Bob

Tom read the memo twice, underlined "start small and iterate," and asked a question that sounded practical and was
actually the deepest objection in the room.

> I run CM. I know what a baseline is, I know what a CCB is, I know what an audit is. What I don't know is what any
> of these words mean. Pipeline. Merge request. I've never written code. Why can't we just do systems engineering
> the way we know how to do it, and put the documents in GitLab if they want them in GitLab?
> -- Tom

Jim did not have good answers yet, and said so. What Jim did instead turned out to be the most useful thing anyone
did in the first week: Jim went to the whiteboard and wrote down every objection, in the speaker's words, without
arguing with any of them. "If we can't answer these with evidence by December," Jim said, "then we'll put that in
the briefing too."

The whiteboard photo went into the team's notes that afternoon. The objections are reproduced here, each with a
pointer to the part of the book where it is answered. They are worth reading slowly; if you are in the team's
position, most of them are yours.

::: objection "We already did this once. Modeling took two years and gave us a spider web."
Bob.

Answer: The spider web is measured in [[cameo]] and untangled a strand at a time: one package loop broken in
[[sprint-4]], the Legacy Import clutter removed and the trace spaghetti pruned in [[sprint-5]]. The last loop, between
Stations and DataCenter, is still open after twelve weeks and stays on the scorecard ([[no-spider-web]]). The two years
went into porting documents by hand into a tool without a workflow; this time the content already exists, a
converter carries most of it, and the workflow is the point. [[scorecard]] compares the two efforts.
:::

::: objection "Why can't we just do systems engineering the old way?"
Tom.

Answer: You can keep doing systems engineering; nothing in this book changes what a requirement, a baseline or a
review is. What changes is how much of it is done by hand. [[devsecops]] translates every DevSecOps practice into
the systems engineering practice it implements, and [[minds]] revisits this objection against twelve weeks of
evidence.
:::

::: objection "We're systems engineers, not programmers. We don't know how to code."
Tom and Bob.

Answer: Nothing in the twelve weeks requires programming. The team edits text files in Vim (which they already
use), types a small number of commands (listed in full in [[survival]] and [[git]]) and reads reports. The pipeline
is a configuration file of about a hundred lines that calls tools other people wrote; [[pipeline]] explains every
line.
:::

::: objection "The contractor owns the model. Anything we do is a copy that will go stale."
Bob.

Answer: It would, if keeping it current were done by hand. [[operating-model]] sets up a vendor branch that holds
exactly what the prime delivered, converted, so each new drop is merged into the government's model like any other
change, with the conflicts shown precisely. [[maintenance]] shows six more drops absorbed this way over the
following fifteen months.
:::

::: objection "Nobody outside our team will be able to read SysML v2 text."
Jim, playing devil's advocate.

Answer: Fewer people could read the Cameo model, because only three people had licenses. The v2 model publishes
itself as a website with generated diagrams and reports on every baseline ([[tools]], [[sprint-4]]), readable in a
browser by anyone with access. The text is for the people who maintain the model; the site is for everybody else.
:::

::: objection "The ISSM will never approve this in time."
Tom.

Answer: The toolchain is small (Git for Windows, Perl and Vim, already approved on the laptops, plus the enterprise
GitLab, already approved for the enterprise) and [[sprint-2]] starts the ISSM conversation in week three with a
written system description. It does cause a three-day block in [[sprint-3]], and the plan absorbs it.
:::

::: objection "We'll spend twelve weeks learning tools and have nothing to show."
Bob.

Answer: The plan in [[operating-model]] delivers something measurable every sprint, starting in [[sprint-1]] with a
working project and a first pipeline, run locally on drop 1 until the runner arrives in [[sprint-2]]. The scorecard below is defined before the work starts, so progress is measured
against targets the team set, not against hope.
:::

::: objection "Management will just move the goalposts."
Tom, quietly, at the end of the meeting.

Answer: Possibly. The defense is the scorecard: agreed in writing in week one, measured automatically, reported
every sprint. [[briefing]] shows the December briefing built from it.
:::

::: objection "In three years there'll be another directive."
Bob.

Answer: Very likely. What survives a change of tools is the habit of keeping the system's description as reviewed,
versioned, checked text with a computed trace. That habit is tool-independent; [[pitfalls]] discusses how not to
lock it into GitLab either.
:::

=== The prime's view {#directive-prime}

Jim called the prime's modeler on Friday 2 October, the afternoon drop 1 arrived, to explain what the government was
about to do with it. Jim expected resistance. The prime's modeler was cautiously relieved.

> We've been getting comment matrices with screenshots for a year. Half the time we can't tell which diagram the
> screenshot came from. If you can tell us exactly which element is wrong, by its ID, we can fix it in the next drop.
> -- the prime's modeler, 2 October

The call settled three working agreements that the rest of the book relies on:

- **The prime keeps delivering what the CDRL requires**, unchanged: XMI, ReqIF, CSV and a manifest with checksums.
  The government will not ask the prime to change tools or formats.
- **The government reports problems as discrepancy reports**, one per problem, each naming the element by its v1
  identifier (the `xmi:id` that the converter keeps on every TODO line and that the drop-to-drop comparison
  reports), with the evidence and the drop by which the government would like it fixed.
- **The prime keeps element identifiers stable** between drops, which Cameo does by default as long as elements are
  edited rather than deleted and recreated. This one agreement is what makes the government's drop-to-drop
  comparison and merge possible ([[maintenance]]).

::: tip Talk to the contractor early
The government's derived model depends on the contractor's deliveries in ways the contract does not spell out:
stable identifiers, consistent export settings, a manifest with checksums. A friendly call before the first drop
is processed costs an hour and avoids months of friction. Write down what was agreed and send it to the
contracting officer's representative.
:::

=== Defining measurable progress {#directive-scorecard}

The most important thing the team produced in the week before sprint 1 was not a tool or a file. It was a single
page that defined what "measurable progress" would mean, agreed by Jim, the program manager and the deputy program
manager. The page has three columns that matter: the measure, how it is computed (automatically, from the system,
never by hand), and the target for week twelve. A fourth column holds the document-era baseline where one exists,
so the comparison in December is fair.

The rule Jim set for choosing measures was simple: *every measure must be computed by a program from the
repository or GitLab, and every measure must be something a skeptic in the room would accept as meaningful.* No
"percentage complete" judged by eye, no counts of meetings held.

|| # | Measure | How it is computed | Week-12 target | Document-era baseline (notional) ||
| 1 | Conversion debt: TODO lines left by the converter | counted by the pipeline's `todo` job on every change | all structure TODOs closed; behavior started | not applicable |
| 2 | Change lead time | median hours from merge request opened to merged, from GitLab | under 3 working days | 23 working days |
| 3 | Gated changes | share of changes to the integration branch that passed the pipeline and were approved by a code owner other than the author | 100% | not measured |
| 4 | Trace answer time | wall-clock time of the pipeline's `trace` job | under one minute | 2 working days |
| 5 | Trace measures | requirements linked (C), orphans (F), unverified (G), completeness (I), text drift | computed on every change; every gap has an owner | computed at reviews only |
| 6 | Discrepancies reported to the prime | discrepancy report issues opened, each with a due drop | every gap found is reported | ad hoc emails |
| 7 | Spider web in the v2 model | package dependency cycles, duplicate/stale blocks, max relationship ends per block | no cycles, no duplicates | see [[cameo]] |
| 8 | Pipeline health | share of pipelines passing on the integration branch; longest time from a failure to the next pass | above 90%; restore within one working day | not applicable |
| 9 | Reproducible baselines | baseline tags whose rebuild matches the recorded checksum | interim baseline tagged and reproducible | revision letters, not reproducible |
| 10 | Review turnaround | median hours from merge request opened to first approval | under 1 working day | 3 weeks per review package |
| 11 | Delivery against plan | story points done / committed per sprint, from the journal | 80% or better | not measured |
| 12 | Team morale | five-question pulse survey (1 to 5) at each retrospective | 3.5 or better, rising | not measured |
Table: The twelve-week scorecard, as agreed before sprint 1

A few choices on this page deserve explanation, because they reveal the team's thinking.

**Conversion debt is the primary progress measure.** The converter carries most of the v1 model into SysML v2
automatically, but leaves a `// TODO` comment wherever it meets something it cannot carry (see [[sysmlv2]]). Those
lines are a precise, countable, shrinking list of work. Unlike "percent complete," a TODO count cannot be argued
with: either the line is there or it is not. Jim chose it because it is the measure Bob, the strongest skeptic,
would accept.

**Lead time and review turnaround have document-era baselines.** These are the measures where the new way should
be dramatically better, and where management will look first. The team was careful to use the same definition on
both sides (from request to incorporation) and to label the old numbers as notional reconstructions.

**Gated changes must be 100%.** This is the one measure with no tolerance. The memo asks for review and approval
of *every* change. If even one change reaches the integration branch without a passing pipeline and an approval,
the audit record has a hole. GitLab's protected branches make this a setting rather than a promise, as [[gitlab-setup]]
shows.

**Morale is measured.** Including it was the program manager's idea, and it surprised the team. "If your team hates
this in December," the program manager said, "the numbers won't matter, because it won't last." The pulse survey
is five questions, answered anonymously at each retrospective on a scale of 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly
agree):

1. I understand why we are working this way.
2. I can do my work in the new tools without help.
3. The checks catch problems I would have missed.
4. I can see our progress.
5. I would choose to keep working this way.

**Nothing is measured by hand.** Every measure on the page comes from the repository, GitLab's records or the team's
journal, and the pipeline or a script computes it. This was a deliberate break from the document era, where every
status number was a one-off, like Bob's Wednesday list in [[documents]].

::: warning Agree on the measures before you start
If you define your measures after the work, you will be accused (correctly) of choosing the ones that look good.
Define them first, get them signed, and report all of them every sprint, including the ones that look bad. Several
of the team's measures looked bad in sprint 3. Reporting them anyway is what made the December briefing credible.
:::

=== Jim's plan in one page {#directive-plan}

With the scorecard agreed, Jim sketched the twelve weeks as six two-week sprints, each with a goal that produced
something measurable. The detailed plan, and the reasoning behind the sprint length and the size rules for work,
is in [[operating-model]]; this is the outline that went to the program manager on Friday 2 October, the same day
the prime delivered drop 1:

|| Sprint | Goal | Evidence at the end ||
| 1 | A GitLab project the team can work in; drop 1 received and assessed | project, protected branches, a first pipeline drafted and run locally on drop 1 (gating every change starts in sprint 2, when the runner is online), TODO inventory |
| 2 | Drop 1 on the integration branch through the vendor branch; every change gated | vendor branch, gated merges, TODO burn-down starts |
| 3 | Network and Types cleaned up; DOORS imported | trace report on every change; first drift found |
| 4 | Stations cleaned up; gaps reported to the prime | discrepancy reports; the model site |
| 5 | Data center and operations cleaned up; release rehearsed | structure TODOs closed; release procedure |
| 6 | Behavior work started; interim baseline; the briefing | baseline-0, release notes, the December briefing |
Table: The twelve-week plan, as sent to the program manager

At the bottom of the page Jim wrote one more line, which the program manager initialed: "We will report every
measure every sprint, including the bad ones."

=== The email Jim sent that night {#directive-email}

After the team meeting, Jim went home and wrote an email to Bob and Tom. Jim almost did not send it, and later said
it was the most important thing written in the twelve weeks. It is reproduced here because it set the tone for
everything that followed.

```text
From: Jim
To: Bob; Tom
Subject: About today

I don't have answers to most of what you said today, and I'm not going to pretend I do.

Here is what I can promise:

1. We will not port anything by hand that a program can port for us. If we find
   ourselves clicking boxes again, we stop and rethink.
2. Every decision we make about how to work goes in writing with the reason, so we can
   change it when we're wrong.
3. Every objection on the whiteboard gets an answer with evidence by December, or it goes
   in the briefing as an open issue. I'll brief it myself.
4. We measure everything the same way every sprint, including the bad numbers.
5. If after twelve weeks this is worse than what we had, I will say so to the PM.

What I'm asking:

1. Try it for real for twelve weeks. Not halfway.
2. Tell me every day what's in your way. That's what the stand-up is for.
3. Tom, take the pipeline. Bob, take the model. I'll take the plan, the prime and
   management, and I'll do reviews.

Neither of you has to become a programmer. I checked. The tools are written; we have to
learn to use them, the way we learned DOORS.

-- Jim
```

Bob replied the next morning with one line: "Twelve weeks. Then we talk." Tom did not reply, but on Wednesday Tom
had already requested a GitLab group from the enterprise administrators, three days before Jim had planned to.

::: tip What the email does
The email does four things a change leader has to do: it admits what is not known, commits to specific behaviors,
asks for specific behaviors in return, and sets an exit condition. It does not argue with the objections, and it
does not promise that the new way will be better. It promises that the team will find out honestly.
:::

=== Week zero {#directive-week0}

The week between the memo and sprint 1 (29 September to 2 October 2026) was not part of any sprint, and Jim used it
to prepare the ground. Nothing in it required DevSecOps knowledge; all of it was ordinary program work. The full
checklist is in [[checklists]]; the main items were:

- **Request access early.** Tom asked the enterprise GitLab administrators for a group (`tidewater`), a project
  (`tidewater/model`), three accounts with the right roles, and a runner, the machine that will execute the
  pipeline. The administrators' standard turnaround was five working days, so the request had to go in before
  sprint 1, not during it.
- **Book the ISSM.** Jim asked for an hour with the ISSM in the second week of sprint 2 to present the toolchain,
  knowing that approvals take time and that sprint 3 would need them.
- **Confirm the laptops.** Git for Windows (which includes Git Bash and Perl), Vim and the kit were already on the
  program's approved software list. Tom checked each laptop's versions so nobody would discover a missing tool on
  day one.
- **Agree the scorecard.** The scorecard above was agreed by Thursday 1 October, signed by the program manager and
  the deputy program manager.
- **Receive drop 1.** The prime delivered drop 1 on Friday 2 October. Jim logged the delivery against CDRL A003
  that afternoon and did nothing else with it until Monday. The receipt is the first story of [[sprint-1]].
- **Set the calendar.** Fifteen-minute stand-ups at 09:00 every working day; sprint planning on the first Monday of
  each sprint; review and retrospective on the last Friday. Holidays and leave were entered before planning, so
  sprint capacities would be honest.

::: warning Access requests are on the critical path
The longest lead time in the first two weeks was not learning git or writing a pipeline. It was waiting for
accounts, a project and a runner from the enterprise administrators. Submit those requests before you do anything
else, even before you are sure exactly what you need. You can adjust a project's settings later; you cannot get
back the week you spent waiting.
:::

=== What management needed to hear, and when {#directive-management}

The program manager's request ("a story I can tell with numbers") shaped the reporting rhythm for the twelve weeks.
Jim agreed to three kinds of report, each built from the scorecard:

|| Report | When | Content | Built from ||
| sprint review | every second Friday | what was finished, demonstrated live; scorecard for the sprint | GitLab, the journal, the pipeline |
| weekly note | every Friday in between | one paragraph: progress, risks, help needed | the journal's weekly quad |
| December PMR briefing | end of sprint 6 | the twelve-week scorecard, before and after, the live demo | everything above; see [[briefing]] |
Table: The twelve-week reporting rhythm

No report would contain a number that had been typed by hand. That rule was hard to keep in sprint 1, when there
was almost nothing to measure, and Jim kept it anyway: the first sprint review reported a scorecard with most cells
marked "not yet measurable" and an explanation of when each would become measurable. The program manager later
said that page was what convinced them the team was serious.

=== Exercises {#directive-exercises}

::: exercise Read your own directive
Take a directive, policy or tasking memo you have received. Build the three-column table from "The directive, read
as a specification": what it says, what it requires, what it leaves open. Who decides the open items?
:::

::: exercise Write down the objections
Hold a thirty-minute meeting with your team about a change you have been asked to make. Write every objection on
a whiteboard in the speaker's words, without arguing. Afterwards, for each, write where and how you will answer it,
and with what evidence.
:::

::: exercise Pick your measures
Choose five measures for your own first twelve weeks. For each, write how it will be computed without human
judgment, what the target is, and what the old-way baseline was. Which of your measures would your most skeptical
colleague reject, and why?
:::
