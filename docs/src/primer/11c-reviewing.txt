== Reviewing a model change {#reviewing}

::: bridge
You know: red-lining a document and filling in the comment matrix.

Here it is called: reviewing a merge request: line comments, threads, approve or request changes.

What is new: you review only what changed, with the generated views and the pipeline results beside it.
:::

::: survival
What you have: decades of document reviews -- comment matrices, redlines, the review board where forty people
looked at slide 37 -- and three years of Cameo reviews by screenshot, where nobody could tell exactly what had
changed.

What you need: to review a merge request in under half an hour, knowing exactly what changed; to read a `.sysml`
diff and the generated views beside it; to write findings that teach rather than blame; and to know when to approve,
when to ask for a change, and when to say "this is too big to review."
:::

::: note Core path
This chapter is CORE. Every model change on Tidewater is reviewed by a second person from the first week; the team
budgeted half a point each in sprint 4 to learn to do it well.
:::

=== Slide 37 {#reviewing-slide37}

Jim's last review before the directive was a Cameo model review for the prime's preliminary design. The model was
presented as sixty slides of diagram screenshots. On slide 37, an internal block diagram of the power system, Jim
noticed that the charge regulator now connected straight to the battery. Had that changed since the last review?
Nobody in the room could say. The prime's modeler thought so; the screenshot from three months earlier was in another
deck; the action item to find out took two weeks.

A merge request answers that question before anyone asks it. It shows exactly which lines changed, who changed them,
why, what the automated checks found, and what the change does to every view of the model. The reviewer's job is no
longer to discover what changed. It is to judge whether the change is right.

::: objection "We don't have time to review every change. We reviewed at milestones."
Answer: A milestone review of three months of changes takes the program days and finds problems weeks after they
were made. A review of one small change takes fifteen to thirty minutes and finds the problem the day it was made.
The total time is smaller, and the problems are cheaper. Tidewater's median review turnaround was measured every
sprint ([[analytics]]); it fell below a day by sprint 4.
:::

=== What the pipeline already checked {#reviewing-machine}

Before a reviewer opens a merge request, the pipeline has run on it ([[pipeline]]). By the time it is green, these
are already true, and the reviewer does not spend a minute on them:

- Every `.sysml` file parses against the official SysML v2 grammar.
- Lint reports 0 errors and 0 warnings; the outline and level rules hold.
- The trace has no *new* orphan, unverified or drifted requirement (from sprint 5).
- The generated documents in `docs/` match the model.
- No token, password or key is in the change.

That list is the reason a review can be short. **If the pipeline is red, do not review.** Say so in one line and
wait; reviewing a change that does not yet pass is reviewing the wrong thing.

=== The reviewer's question {#reviewing-question}

What is left is the part no machine can judge: *is the change right?* Tidewater's review checklist
(`docs/conventions/review-checklist.md` in every scaffolded repository) breaks that question into a handful of
things to look at. Here it is in full:

{{include-md: tools/gitlab/templates/docs/conventions/review-checklist.md}}

Two items on that list do most of the work in practice.

**"It does what the issue asked, and no more."** The most common problem in Tidewater's first sprints was not a wrong
change; it was a change that did two things. Bob's first Stations merge request tagged levels *and* reformatted the
indentation of the whole file, so the diff showed 180 changed lines for twelve real changes. Jim asked for it to be
split, and the second version was reviewed in ten minutes.

**"Converter files: lines that came from the prime are changed only to close a TODO line or by an accepted DR."**
This is the rule that keeps prime drops merging ([[repo-layout-rules]]). A reviewer who sees a change to a
prime-delivered line that is not a TODO closure asks: is this the prime's to fix? If yes, it becomes a discrepancy
report, not a merge request.

=== A review, start to finish {#reviewing-walk}

Practice lesson 11's sandbox has the Tidewater project in an offline GitLab, with a clone for Bob and the pipeline
the team uses. Play both sides: Bob makes a small change and opens a merge request; Jim reviews it.

==== Bob: the change {#reviewing-walk-change}

Bob tags the station segment with its level -- one TODO line closed, one import added. (The `perl -0pi` line is a
scripted stand-in for the edit you would make in Vim.)

```sh run sandbox=practice-11 expect="pipeline #\d+ success"
. ./env.sh > /dev/null
cd work
gl flow start feature 1-stationsegment-level
perl -0pi -e 's{// TODO stereotype Tidewater_Profile:L2Segment[^\n]*}{\@L2Segment;}; s{(package Stations \{\n)}{$1    private import Conventions::*;\n}' model/physical/stations/Stations.sysml
git commit -qam "Tag StationSegment with its level (L2Segment)"
gl flow finish --title "Tag StationSegment with its level" --description "Closes #1"
```

`flow finish` pushes the branch, opens the merge request against `develop`, and runs its pipeline. The last line
says which merge request it is and that its pipeline succeeded. Each block after this one starts with `. ../env.sh`:
the book's own test runs every block in a fresh shell, so it loads the lesson's environment again. In your terminal
you load it once, and it stays loaded until you close the window.

==== Jim: what changed, in text {#reviewing-walk-diff}

The reviewer starts with the merge request itself -- its description, its pipeline, its test widget -- and then the
diff. On the web these are the Overview and Changes tabs. From the clone:

```sh run cwd=work expect="@L2Segment;"
. ../env.sh > /dev/null
gl --as jim mr show 2
git diff develop...feature/1-stationsegment-level
```

The three dots in `develop...feature/...` mean "what the branch changed since it left `develop`," which is exactly
what the merge request will bring in. The diff shows one line removed (the TODO), one added (`@L2Segment;`) and the
import. That is a change you can review completely in two minutes.

How to read a `.sysml` diff:

- **Lines starting with `-` are gone, `+` are new.** A changed line shows as one of each.
- **The `@@ ... @@` lines say where.** The text after the second `@@` is often the enclosing package or definition,
  which tells you the context without opening the file.
- **Look at what each `+` line *means*,** not only what it says. `@L2Segment;` means "this part is a segment."
  Is StationSegment a segment? (Yes: it is one of the network's three segments.)
- **Look for what is missing.** A new `satisfy` without a `verify`; a new port with no connection; a requirement
  with no DOORS id. The pipeline catches some of these; the reviewer catches the rest.

==== Jim: what changed, in pictures {#reviewing-walk-views}

Text diffs show lines. Model changes are often easier to judge as pictures: what the change did to the part tree,
the trace, the package dependencies. The kit draws the views of both versions with the differences colored
([[diagrams-diff]]). Write them outside the clone, so they do not end up in a commit:

```sh run expect="0 new gap"
. ../env.sh > /dev/null
mkdir -p ../review
perl "$KIT/tools/sysml/model.pl" diff --git develop..feature/1-stationsegment-level -o ../review/stations-level
cat ../review/stations-level-changes.txt
```

The summary line is the most useful single line a reviewer reads: how many changes, how many *new trace gaps*, how
many new error findings. "0 new gaps" tells Jim the change cannot have broken traceability. The colored SVGs in
`../review/` show where the change sits in the part tree.

In the real GitLab the reviewer often does not need this command at all: from sprint 4 the pipeline publishes the
model site, and the merge request's pipeline has the drawn views as artifacts. Open the artifact, not the text.

==== Jim: the finding {#reviewing-walk-finding}

The change is right. But Jim wants one more thing: a line of documentation saying why StationSegment is the L2 level,
so a reader of the part tree does not have to guess. That is a finding, and it must be resolved before the merge:

```sh run expect="discussions not resolved"
. ../env.sh > /dev/null
gl --as jim mr note 2 "Line 12: the tag is right. Please add a doc line saying the station segment is the L2 level of the network, so a reader of the part tree knows why." --resolvable
gl --as jim mr approve 2
gl mr merge 2 || true
```

The merge is refused: "discussions not resolved." The project setting *all threads must be resolved* turns every
resolvable comment into a small gate. Bob answers the finding -- with a change, or with a reply that explains -- and
resolves the thread; then the merge goes through:

```sh run expect="merged as"
. ../env.sh > /dev/null
gl mr resolve 2
gl mr merge 2
```

Notice what Jim did *not* do: Jim did not withhold the approval while waiting for a one-line doc comment. Jim approved
the engineering and left a resolvable thread for the polish. The order matters to the author's day.

=== Suggestions: the redline you can apply {#reviewing-suggestions}

On the web, a reviewer who knows the exact replacement text can write it as a **suggestion**: a comment on a line
with a fenced block marked `suggestion`. The author applies it with one click, and the merge request gets a commit
with the reviewer's text. It is the redline, applied.

{{include-md: tools/gitlab/samples/08-review-thread.md}}

The first comment in that thread shows the form: three backticks, the word `suggestion`, an optional range (`:-0+1`
means "this line and the one after"), and the replacement lines. Use suggestions for exact wording, a missing unit,
a name that breaks the convention -- anything where you already know the answer. Do not use them for design
questions; ask those as questions.

=== Different changes, different questions {#reviewing-kinds}

Not every merge request is reviewed the same way. The kinds of task in [[stories-tasks]] produce different kinds of
change, and each has its own first question:

|| Change | Typical size | The reviewer's first question | Look at ||
| TODO closure (level tags, procured parts) | 5 to 40 lines | is each tag the right level for that part? | the diff; the part tree view |
| Hand-written construct (state machine, flows, constraint) | 20 to 80 lines | does it say what the v1 model meant? | the v1 element in the drop (its xmi:id is in the TODO line); the IDEF0 or state view |
| Requirement change | a few lines | is it one testable statement, by the recipe? | the recipe report; the trace view |
| Interface change | a few lines, wide effect | who uses this port or item, and do they agree? | the interconnection view; the second approver |
| Prime drop merge | hundreds of lines | what did the prime change, and what does it break of ours? | the delta and impact report, not the diff |
| Pipeline change | `.gitlab-ci.yml`, `ci/job.pl` | does every job still run, and does a red still block? | the pipeline of the MR itself, all jobs listed |
| Decision record or convention | one file | is the decision clear, and are its consequences named? | the text |
Table: What to ask first, by kind of change

=== Reviewing a prime drop merge {#reviewing-drops}

A prime drop merge request is the one review where reading the diff line by line is the wrong approach. A drop
changes hundreds of lines, most of them exactly as the prime delivered them; the government does not review the
prime's work line by line, it reviews *what the drop does to the government's model*.

So the drop's merge request carries the two reports from the converter, and the reviewer reads those first
([[prime-changes]]):

- **The delta**, `xmi2sysml.pl --delta OLD.xmi NEW.xmi`: every element the prime added, removed or changed, by its v1
  id, each marked *carried* (the converter brings it over) or *manual* (the converter cannot, and someone must rework
  it by hand). The manual list is the reviewer's checklist: each item is a task, or already done in this MR.
- **The impact**, `--impact model`: for each change, which of the government's requirements, verification cases,
  interfaces and allocations it touches, and which of the government's hand edits must be redone. A hand edit listed
  here and not redone in the merge request is a finding.

Then the conflicts: every file git could not merge on its own is listed in the merge request description, with how
it was resolved. The reviewer checks that each resolution took the prime's side for the prime's content and re-applied
the government's edits on top -- never the other way round ([[pitfall-take-ours]]).

=== Writing findings that teach {#reviewing-etiquette}

The team's first reviews were stiff. Bob wrote "This is wrong." Tom wrote nothing and approved. Jim wrote three
paragraphs. By sprint 4 they had settled on a few habits, which the review checklist's last table records:

- **Say what and why in one or two sentences, on the exact line.** "The battery `capacity` has no unit; `Energy`
  would let the energy balance check it" teaches; "units?" does not.
- **Ask, when you are not sure.** "Why does QualityControl depend on SensorSuite? It closes a cycle with Stations."
  The author may know something you do not.
- **Separate must from could.** Prefix optional ideas with "Optional:" or "Nit:", and do not hold the merge for them.
- **Praise specifically, once.** "Good split -- this is reviewable in ten minutes" tells the author what to repeat.
- **Never review the person.** The comment is about the line, not about who wrote it. On a three-person team, every
  reviewer is reviewed by the people they review.

::: sidebar Tom approved everything
In sprints 2 and 3, Tom approved every merge request Tom was asked to review, usually within minutes, without a
comment. At the sprint 3 retrospective Tom said why: "Who am I to tell Bob the model is wrong? Bob knows it
better than I do." Jim's answer became a team rule: the reviewer's job is not to know more than the author; it is to
be the second pair of eyes the change has never had. Tom's first real finding, in sprint 4, was a requirement
`satisfy` pointing at the whole Network instead of the Telemetry Unit -- exactly the "trace at the right level" item
on the checklist, and something Bob had not seen because Bob had written it.
:::

=== Approve, ask for changes, or ask to split {#reviewing-decide}

A reviewer ends with one of three outcomes:

|| Outcome | When | How ||
| Approve | You would sign for the change as it is, or with only optional comments | the Approve button, or the `/approve` quick action |
| Ask for changes | Something must change before merging | a resolvable thread on each finding (some GitLab versions also offer "Request changes" when you submit a review); do not approve yet |
| Ask to split | The change is too big to review in about half an hour, or does two unrelated things | one comment saying so and suggesting the split; do not review the rest |
Table: The three outcomes of a review

Approval is enforced, not ceremonial: `CODEOWNERS` names who must approve a change to each folder, and an interface
change needs two approvals, Jim's as systems lead and the other modeler's ([[gitlab-setup-codeowners]]). GitLab will
not let an author approve their own change.

=== Turnaround {#reviewing-turnaround}

A review that waits three days costs more than a review that finds a problem. The team agreed on three habits:

- **Review before you start new work in the morning.** The GitLab To-Do list shows every merge request waiting for
  you.
- **A first response within one working day**, even if it is "I'll look at this after the stand-up tomorrow."
- **Small changes get reviewed first.** They are quick, and they unblock someone.

Review turnaround is one of the analytics the team reported to management from sprint 4 ([[analytics]]): the time
from a merge request being opened to its first review comment or approval.

=== Applying this to your project {#reviewing-apply}

- **Copy:** the review checklist, the rule that a red pipeline is not reviewed, resolvable threads as gates, and the
  habit of reviewing the generated views, not only the text.
- **Adapt:** the checklist's engineering items to your model's conventions and your program's review criteria; the
  CODEOWNERS sections to your team's ownership.
- **Decide locally:** who reviews whom (on three people, everyone reviews everyone), whether a second approval is
  needed for interfaces, and the turnaround you will measure.
