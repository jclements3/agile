== Stand-ups, the journal and the board {#journal}

::: bridge
You know: the status spreadsheet and the action-item list.

Here it is called: the journal of stories, and the GitLab board for tasks.

What is new: status is recorded once, as it happens, and every report is derived from it.
:::

::: survival
What you have: a GitLab project where tasks live as issues ([[gitlab-setup]], [[tools]]), and an operating model that
says work comes in tomes, epics, stories and tasks ([[operating-model]]).

What you need: a record of the plan that management can trust: what each sprint committed, what it finished, what
slipped, how fast the team goes, and where each epic and tome stands. This chapter sets up the agile kit's
**journal**, a plain-text ledger of stories kept in Vim, explains how it divides the work with GitLab, and shows the
reports it produces. [[ceremonies]] then shows the daily and fortnightly rhythm that feeds it.
:::

=== Two records, one plan {#journal-two}

By the end of sprint 1 the team had two places that knew about the work, and Bob asked the obvious question: why two?

> If every task is an issue in GitLab, why do we also keep a journal? That's the double bookkeeping we were promised
> would go away.
> -- Bob, sprint 1 review

The answer is that the two records hold different things, for different readers, at different levels of the
hierarchy.

|| Level | Size rule | Where it lives | Who reads it ||
| Project | years; this one is 39 sprints | the journal: one journal per project | the program |
| Tome | under a year (two semesters) | the journal, `t:` on each story; a `tome::` label in GitLab | the program manager |
| Epic | under a semester (13 sprints) | the journal, `e:` on each story; an `epic::` label in GitLab | Jim, management |
| Story | under one sprint | **the journal** (id `TW-N`) and a GitLab issue labeled `type::story` (number N) | Jim, the team |
| Task | under a week (5 points or fewer) | **GitLab only**: an issue labeled `type::task`, closed by a merge request | the person doing it |
Table: The hierarchy, and where each level is recorded

The journal tracks **stories and above**: the commitments the team makes to management, sprint by sprint, and the
roll-up into epics and tomes. GitLab tracks **tasks and the stories they belong to**: the day-to-day work, the merge
requests that do it, the reviews and the pipelines.

The link between them is the number. A story is GitLab issue 12 and journal item `TW-12`. Anyone reading a stand-up
note that mentions `TW-12` opens issue 12 in GitLab to see its tasks, merge requests and discussion; anyone looking
at issue 12 knows the journal tracks it as `TW-12`.

This is a deliberate application of a rule the agile kit has always had: the architect's journal holds commitments
and their state, and task-level detail stays in the team's own tool. Tasks change daily; they are cut, merged,
re-cut. A journal that tracked them would be noisy and always out of date. Stories change state a few times each,
and every change is a fact management cares about: committed, done, carried, blocked.

::: note Why not GitLab for everything?
GitLab's Premium tier has epics and iterations with burndown charts, and with it a team could keep the whole hierarchy
in GitLab. The team had the Free tier, and more importantly a need GitLab does not meet: a ledger in which points
are never edited after the fact, only moved, so last month's numbers are exactly what they were last month. That
property is what lets [[scorecard]] compare sprint 1 with sprint 6 without anyone asking "has this been restated?"
:::

=== Everything is a ledger {#journal-ledger}

The journal is a text file, `scrum.txt`, in the format of **ledger-cli**, a decades-old plain-text accounting tool.
That sounds odd for a scrum record until you see what it buys.

Every story has **points** (the team's estimate of its size: one point is one ideal person-day). The points sit in an
**account**, and the account *is* the story's state:

```text
Backlog:Model  ->  Sprint:N:Model:Committed  ->  Done
                                             ->  Carryover  ->  next sprint's Committed
                                             ->  Removed
```

When the team commits a story to sprint 1, its points move from `Backlog:Model` to `Sprint:1:Model:Committed`. When
it is done, they move to `Sprint:1:Model:Done`. Nothing is ever edited; each event is a new entry that moves points.
Re-estimating a story posts the difference, not a new number. Like money in double-entry bookkeeping, points are
conserved: every entry balances, and the totals cannot drift.

Here is what the journal recorded on the first day of sprint 1, written by the kit from the planning notes (the
compile step is below):

```text
; ---- standup 2026-10-05 (standups/2026-10-05.txt)
~ Sprint 1
    Model   19 SP

2026-10-05 Intake TW-1 Create the GitLab group and model project
    Backlog:Model                     4 SP   ; id: TW-1, epic: E1 GitLab project and gitflow, owner: Tom, prio: 1, tome: T1 Stand up MBSE DevSecOps
    Equity:Intake

2026-10-05 Standup Model
    Backlog:Model                     -4 SP   ; id: TW-1
    Sprint:1:Model:Committed          4 SP   ; id: TW-1, owner: Tom
```

(an excerpt). The `~ Sprint 1` entry records the team's capacity for the sprint, 19 points. The `Intake` entry brings
a new story into the backlog with its metadata after the semicolon: its epic, owner, priority and tome. The `Standup`
entry moves its 4 points into the sprint's commitment.

You never write these entries by hand. You write terse notes, and the kit writes the ledger.

=== Setting up the journal {#journal-init}

The journal is its own git repository, separate from the model, because it has a different audience and different
rules (it is the team's management record; the model is a controlled item). The kit creates one:

```sh run sandbox=scratch expect="created scrum.conf"
mkdir -p ~/tidewater-journal && cd ~/tidewater-journal
perl $KIT/bin/daily.pl init .
```

```text
created .gitattributes
created .gitignore
created scrum.conf
created scrum.txt
git init

Next:
  1. edit scrum.conf (recipients, markings, townhall time, facilitator = your Teams display name)
  2. the people:  daily.pl roster add "Lee, Ann" ann.lee@example.com Alpha Lead ACME   (name exactly as Teams shows it)
  3. the backlog: daily.pl new, then 'new ID PTS title e:Epic t:Tome o:"Owner Name"' lines under '== Team', then compile
  4. add to ~/.vimrc:   source $KIT/vim/scrum.vim
     then in Vim:  :SNew  :SCompile  :SReport  :SDraft  :SAll  :SStatus
```

`scrum.conf` holds the settings, one `key = value` per line. For a single team the ones that matter on day one are
`default_team = Model` (the team's name in the journal; the new file leaves it empty) and `unit = SP` (story points,
already set). In Vim it is one edit; from the shell, one command. The people go in `roster.txt`, with their names
exactly as the meeting chat shows them, because the stand-up tools match chat names to the roster:

```sh run cwd=~/tidewater-journal expect="Tom  \| tom@example.invalid"
perl -pi -e 's/^default_team\s*=\s*/default_team  = Model   /' scrum.conf
perl $KIT/bin/daily.pl roster add "Jim" jim@example.invalid Model "MBSE lead" Gov
perl $KIT/bin/daily.pl roster add "Bob" bob@example.invalid Model "MBSE engineer" Gov
perl $KIT/bin/daily.pl roster add "Tom" tom@example.invalid Model "CM and DevSecOps engineer" Gov
cat roster.txt
```

```text
# name | email               | team  | role                      | org
  Bob  | bob@example.invalid | Model | MBSE engineer             | Gov
  Jim  | jim@example.invalid | Model | MBSE lead                 | Gov
  Tom  | tom@example.invalid | Model | CM and DevSecOps engineer | Gov
```

Jim keeps the journal repository on the team's share as well as in GitLab (a second project in the `tidewater`
group, private to the three of them), and commits it at the end of each day with `daily.pl commit`.

=== The stand-up file {#journal-file}

Each day's notes go in one small text file, `standups/YYYY-MM-DD.txt`, written in Vim (`:SNew` creates today's file
with the open stories listed as comments). The notes use a handful of four-letter verbs, each one line:

|| Verb | Meaning | Example ||
| `sprint N` | which sprint this file belongs to | `sprint 1` |
| `== Team` | the team the following lines are about | `== Model` |
| `cap N` | the team's capacity for the sprint, in points | `cap 19` |
| `new ID PTS title ...` | a new story into the backlog, with `p:` priority, `e:` epic, `t:` tome, `o:` owner | see below |
| `commit ID Owner` | commit a story to the sprint | `commit TW-1 Tom` |
| `done ID ...` | stories finished today | `done TW-3` |
| `block ID why` / `unblock ID` | a story is blocked, or no longer | `block TW-1 waiting on the enterprise GitLab admins` |
| `carry ID ...` | at the sprint's end, stories that go to the next sprint | `carry TW-11` |
| `est ID N` | a new estimate (posts the difference) | `est TW-7 5` |
| `note text` | a line for the record and the status mail | `note Demo went well` |
Table: The stand-up verbs used most

Sprint 1's planning file, written by Jim on Monday 5 October after the kickoff. In Vim, Jim typed the lines into the
file `:SNew` opened; to type along from the shell, write the same lines with a here-document (everything between the
`<<'PLAN'` line and the `PLAN` line goes into the file):

```sh run expect="^note Sprint 1 kickoff"
mkdir -p standups && cat > standups/2026-10-05.txt <<'PLAN'
2026-10-05
sprint 1
== Model
cap 19
new TW-1 4 Create the GitLab group and model project p:1 e:"E1 GitLab project and gitflow" t:"T1 Stand up MBSE DevSecOps" o:Tom
new TW-2 4 First pipeline gates every MR p:1 e:"E2 Pipeline and quality gates" t:"T1 Stand up MBSE DevSecOps" o:Tom
new TW-3 3 Receive drop 1 and record it p:1 e:"E5 Drop 1 intake and first import" t:"T2 Port Tidewater v1 to SysML v2" o:Jim
new TW-4 4 Convert drop 1 and assess it p:1 e:"E5 Drop 1 intake and first import" t:"T2 Port Tidewater v1 to SysML v2" o:Bob
new TW-5 2 Working agreements p:2 e:"E3 Team practices and onboarding" t:"T1 Stand up MBSE DevSecOps" o:Jim
commit TW-1 Tom
commit TW-2 Tom
commit TW-3 Jim
commit TW-4 Bob
commit TW-5 Jim
note Sprint 1 kickoff: goal "A GitLab project the team can work in; drop 1 received and assessed"
PLAN
tail -1 standups/2026-10-05.txt
```

(The story numbers in this chapter's examples come from a practice journal; Part III uses the numbers from the real
run.) Quoted values (`e:"E1 GitLab project and gitflow"`) may contain spaces; unquoted ones may not.

Then the **compile** step checks the file and, if every line is valid, appends the ledger entries to `scrum.txt`:

```sh run expect="applied standups/2026-10-05.txt \(6 transactions\)"
perl $KIT/bin/daily.pl compile
```

```text
applied standups/2026-10-05.txt (6 transactions)
```

A stand-up file with a single mistake (an unknown story id, a `done` for a story that was never committed, a verb
misspelled) is rejected whole, with every problem listed, and nothing is written. That is the journal's own gate: it
never contains half a day.

In Vim the same steps are `:SNew` (today's file), `:SCompile`, and `:SStatus`. [[vim]] lists the rest.

=== What the journal tells you {#journal-views}

Everything the journal reports is computed from the ledger when you ask; nothing is stored separately, so nothing can
disagree.

The sprint view, the morning after planning (`--today` sets the date the journal reads as "today", so this works on
any day you type it):

```sh run expect="Model   19      17     0    17"
perl $KIT/bin/daily.pl --today 2026-10-06 sprint
```

```text
Teams: sprint 1
Team   Cap  Commit  Done  Open  Carry  Removed  Done%  Load%
-----  ---  ------  ----  ----  -----  -------  -----  -----
Model   19      17     0    17      0        0     0%    89%
Total   19      17     0    17      0        0     0%    89%
```

`Load%` is commitment divided by capacity: 17 of 19 points, 89%. The kit's metrics treat a load above 110% as a
warning (the team is promising more than it can do) and below 70% as another (it is not using its capacity, or the
capacity is wrong). Jim aimed for 85 to 95% every sprint.

The epics view, at the end of sprint 1:

```text
Epics by tome
Tome                              Epic                                     Tasks  Total  Done  In sprint  Backlog  Done%
--------------------------------  ---------------------------------------  -----  -----  ----  ---------  -------  -----
T1 Stand up MBSE DevSecOps        E1 GitLab project and gitflow                1      4     4          0        0   100%
T1 Stand up MBSE DevSecOps        E2 Pipeline and quality gates                2      9     4          0        5    44%
T1 Stand up MBSE DevSecOps        E3 Team practices and onboarding             1      2     2          0        0   100%
T2 Port Tidewater v1 to SysML v2  E5 Drop 1 intake and first import            3     10     7          0        3    70%
T2 Port Tidewater v1 to SysML v2  E6 Structure: close the structure TODOs      1      3     0          0        3     0%
```

(The column is headed "Tasks" because the kit calls every journal item a task; in this project every journal item is a
story.)

Velocity, the points done per sprint, which becomes the basis for forecasts once there are three sprints of history:

```text
Model  (avg velocity 17.0 over the last 1 completed sprint)
Sprint  Cap  Commit  Done  Done%
------  ---  ------  ----  -----
     1   19      17    17   100%
```

And the ledger itself, through the kit's ledger tool, balances to zero, which is the proof that no points were
created or lost. Right after planning, in your journal:

```sh run expect="17 SP  Sprint:1:Model:Committed"
perl $KIT/bin/ledger.pl -f scrum.txt bal
```

```text
              -17 SP  Equity:Intake
               17 SP  Sprint:1:Model:Committed
--------------------
                   0
```

and in the practice journal at the end of sprint 1, after the stories were done and the next ones were filed:

```text
               11 SP  Backlog:Model
              -28 SP  Equity:Intake
               17 SP  Sprint:1:Model
               17 SP    Done
--------------------
                   0
```

Twenty-eight points came in (`Equity:Intake` is the counterweight of every intake); seventeen are done in sprint 1;
eleven wait in the backlog for sprint 2.

=== Estimates that change {#journal-est}

Estimates change, especially in the first weeks, when the team does not yet know how long anything takes. In the
journal a new estimate is a posting of the difference. When the team re-estimated story `TW-7` from 4 to 5 points in
refinement ([[ceremonies]] shows the poker round), the journal recorded:

```text
2026-10-14 Intake TW-7 Docs, outline and TODO jobs
    Backlog:Model                     4 SP   ; id: TW-7, epic: E2 Pipeline and quality gates, owner: Tom, prio: 1, tome: T1 Stand up MBSE DevSecOps
...
    Backlog:Model                     1 SP   ; id: TW-7
```

The original 4 points and the added 1 are both visible forever. A forecast made on the 13th and one made on the 15th
can each be reproduced from the journal as it stood that day; the kit can load the journal "as of" any date. In the
document era, the action item spreadsheet had one estimate column, overwritten.

=== A story from end to end {#journal-story}

It helps to follow one story through both records. Take the story Bob owned in sprint 1, "Convert drop 1 and assess
it", four points.

**Monday, planning.** Jim creates the story as a GitLab issue with the Story template (its number becomes the id: in
the practice project it was issue 1) and the journal line `new TW-4 4 Convert drop 1 and assess it ...` with
`commit TW-4 Bob`. In GitLab the issue gets the labels `type::story`, `tome::port`, `epic::drop-intake` and the
milestone "Sprint 1". Bob cuts it into tasks during the kickoff, each its own issue with the Task template and "Part
of" the story:

|| Task | Points | What it produces ||
| Run both converters with --report | 1 | the two converter reports, attached to the story |
| TODO inventory by package | 2 | a table of the 52 TODO lines by package and kind, in the story |
| Estimate the port backlog with the team | 1 | candidate stories for the structure epic, poker-estimated |
Table: The tasks of one story

**Tuesday to Thursday, the work.** Each task moves across the board: `workflow::in-progress` when Bob starts it, a
comment with the result and `/spend` when it is finished, `/close`. [[tools]] shows the first task's history, driven
entirely by quick actions. Bob's stand-up lines name the story, not the tasks (`Y TW-4 read the converter report`),
because the journal knows stories.

**Friday of week 2, done.** When the last task closes, Bob says `done` in the stand-up; the answers step suggests the
`done TW-4` line; Jim confirms and compiles. In GitLab, Jim checks the story's acceptance criteria and closes it with a
comment. The journal moved 4 points from `Sprint:1:Model:Committed` to `Sprint:1:Model:Done`, dated that day; GitLab
recorded three closed tasks with their time and their outputs.

Neither record holds the other's detail, and neither needs to. Management sees the story in the quad; anyone who
wants the details follows the number.

::: tip When a story will not fit
If, during the sprint, a story turns out bigger than the sprint, do not stretch it. Split it: the finished part is a
story that is done; the rest is a new story in the backlog, estimated at the next refinement. The journal's `carry`
exists for the story that is nearly done on the last day, not for the one that was never going to fit.
:::

=== The board and the journal, day to day {#journal-board}

The team kept the two in step with three habits:

- **The board is for tasks; the journal is for stories.** The GitLab board, scoped to the current sprint's milestone,
  is what the team looks at during the day. The journal is updated once a day, after the stand-up.
- **A story is done when its last task closes.** GitLab closes a task when its merge request merges; when a story's
  last task is closed, the owner says so in the stand-up (`done TW-12`), and Jim's journal records it.
- **Blocked is said out loud.** A blocked task gets the `workflow::blocked` label in GitLab and a comment saying why; the
  story gets a `block` line in the journal the same day, so the weekly report to management shows it ([[ceremonies]],
  [[communicating]]).

=== The reports {#journal-reports}

The journal feeds every report the team sends upward. Each one is a command, and each was produced every sprint
without anyone assembling a slide:

- `daily.pl review N`: the sprint report at the review: committed, done, carried, velocity, predictability, and each
  epic's remaining points and forecast completion sprint. Sprint 1's began:

```text
Sprint 1 report -- as of 2026-10-16: 17/17 SP done (100%), 0 carried over, 0 removed.

Team           Commit  Done  Done%  Carry Carry%   Load Velocity  Predict%  Punted Interrupt    Cap
Model              17    17   100%      0     0%    89%     17.0      100%       0         0     19
```

- `daily.pl quad`: the weekly one-page status in four quadrants (technical priorities, watch items, schedule
  milestones, accomplishments), shown in [[ceremonies]] and [[communicating]].
- `daily.pl health`: every metric against its threshold, red, amber or fine.
- `daily.pl report`: all of the above as HTML pages, plus the cockpit dashboard.

::: warning One hundred percent is not the goal
Sprint 1 in the practice journal finished 17 of 17, and so did the real sprint 1 ({{value: docs/tidewater/sprints/01/scorecard.json 11_delivery.done}} of {{value: docs/tidewater/sprints/01/scorecard.json 11_delivery.committed}}). Sprint 4 did not:
it finished {{value: docs/tidewater/sprints/04/scorecard.json 11_delivery.done}} of {{value: docs/tidewater/sprints/04/scorecard.json 11_delivery.committed}} story points ([[om-done]]) and carried a walk-through into sprint 5, and that is fine ([[sprint-4]]). A team
that finishes everything every sprint is committing too little. The kit's metrics look for predictability (done over
committed) of 80% or better, not 100%.
:::

=== Exercises {#journal-exercises}

::: exercise {#ex-journal-init} Make a journal
Create a journal with `daily.pl init`, add yourself and two colleagues to the roster, and write a planning file for
a sprint of your own work with three stories. Compile it, then run `daily.pl sprint` and `daily.pl epics`.
:::

::: exercise {#ex-journal-break} Break the compile
Add a line `done XX-99` to a stand-up file and compile. What does the kit say, and what did it write to `scrum.txt`?
:::

::: exercise {#ex-journal-split} Where does it go?
For each item, decide whether it belongs in the journal, in GitLab, or both, and why: (a) "write the state definition
for BuoyModes, 3 points"; (b) "close the Stations TODO lines, 8 points"; (c) "the structure port"; (d) "Bob spent 4
hours on the review"; (e) "blocked: waiting for the ISSM".
:::

::: exercise {#ex-journal-est} Re-estimate
Re-estimate one of your stories with an `est` line. Look at the ledger entries it produced. Why does the kit post the
difference instead of replacing the number?
:::

=== Applying this to your project {#journal-apply}

- **Copy**: two records with a clear split (commitments and roll-ups in the journal, tasks in the team's tool); one
  number for a story in both; points that move and are never edited; a compile step that rejects a bad day whole;
  reports computed from the record, never assembled by hand.
- **Adapt**: the hierarchy's names and size rules to your program; the capacity per person; the load band.
- **Decide locally**: who keeps the journal (one person, usually the lead); where it lives; who may read it; which
  reports go to whom ([[communicating]]).
