== The operating model {#operating-model}

::: bridge
You know: the integrated master schedule, phases, milestones and the weekly status meeting.

Here it is called: the operating model: project, tomes, epics, stories and tasks, in two-week sprints.

What is new: work is cut into pieces small enough to finish in days, and progress is counted, not reported.
:::

::: survival
What you have: a directive, a signed scorecard, a twelve-week outline, three people and a contractor who delivers a
model every quarter.

What you need: a way to cut the work into pieces of a known size, a calendar the team keeps, a clear answer to
"who decides?", and a written list of the decisions that shape everything else. That is the operating model.
:::

=== Sunday evening, the whiteboard {#om-whiteboard}

On the Sunday before sprint 1, Jim stood in front of the whiteboard in the team room with a marker and a question
the program manager had asked on Thursday: "When you say twelve weeks, twelve weeks of *what*?"

The honest answer, at that moment, was "twelve weeks of figuring it out." Jim knew that would not survive the first
review. In the document era the answer would have been a work breakdown structure, an integrated master schedule
and a systems engineering management plan: three documents, a month to write, approved by people who would never
read them again. Jim did not have a month, and did not want three more documents. What Jim wanted was something
the team could carry in their heads, check every morning, and change on a Wednesday without a CCB.

By the time Bob and Tom arrived on Monday morning, the board held five boxes stacked like a parts tree, each with a
size limit written next to it, a two-week calendar with the meetings marked, and three names with arrows to the
things each would own. Bob looked at it for a while and said, "So it's a WBS and a schedule."

"It's a WBS and a schedule," Jim agreed. "With size limits that we actually enforce, and a schedule that repeats
every two weeks instead of running for three years."

That whiteboard is this chapter.

=== Why an operating model, and why so small {#om-why}

An **operating model** is the answer to four questions: how is the work divided, when does the team meet and
plan, who decides what, and which decisions have already been made. Every systems engineering organization has one,
whether it writes it down or not. In the document era the written version was the SEMP, and the real version was
the set of habits the team had built over twenty years: who you called before you changed an ICD, which
spreadsheet held the truth this month, which review package was actually read.

DevSecOps does not abolish any of that. It changes three things about it.

First, **the pieces of work are small and of a known size**. In the document era a work package was sized to the
contract: "System Specification, revision C" might be six months of effort with one milestone at the end. Nobody
knew whether it was on track until the review. In the new way, no piece of work the team commits to is larger than
two weeks, and most are a few days. Progress becomes visible because things finish.

Second, **the calendar is a heartbeat, not a timeline**. Instead of an integrated master schedule that runs for
years with a few dozen milestones, the team runs the same two-week cycle again and again: plan, work, show, reflect.
The long-range plan still exists (Tidewater's runs for thirty-nine sprints), but it is built out of these
repeating cycles, and it is re-planned at every one.

Third, **the process is enforced by the platform, not by memory**. "Every change is reviewed" stops being a sentence
in a SEMP and becomes a setting on a protected branch in GitLab ([[gitlab-setup]]). "Nobody changes a baseline"
becomes a protected tag. The operating model says what the rule is; the tools make it true.

::: sidebar The SEMP the team did not write
Tom asked, in the first week, whether the team should update the SEMP to describe the new way of working. Jim's
answer was to write a two-page "operating model" page in the GitLab wiki instead, and to put a one-paragraph pointer
to it in the next SEMP revision. The wiki page is under version control like everything else, and it changed eleven
times in the first twelve weeks. A SEMP revision takes a CCB. The team would have spent the twelve weeks either
violating its own SEMP or waiting for approval to change it.
:::

=== The five levels {#om-levels}

Tidewater's work is divided into five levels. Each has a size limit, and the limit is the point: a level is defined
by how long its pieces are allowed to take, not by what kind of work they hold.

|| Level | Size limit | Tidewater example | Where it lives | Who owns it ||
| Project | years | "Tidewater SysML v2 port", 18 months (39 sprints) | the journal (one per project) | Jim, with the program manager |
| Tome | under 1 year (2 semesters, 26 sprints) | T2 "Port Tidewater v1 to SysML v2", sprints 1 to 20 | the journal (`t:` tag); GitLab label `tome::port` | Jim |
| Epic | under 1 semester (13 sprints) | E6 "Structure: close the structure TODOs", sprints 2 to 8 | the journal (`e:` tag); GitLab label `epic::structure` | Jim |
| Story | under 1 sprint (2 weeks) | "Close the Network TODOs", sprint 3 | the journal (an item); a GitLab issue labeled `type::story` | the person assigned |
| Task | under 1 week (5 points or fewer) | "Replace the level stereotype TODOs in Network.sysml with @L tags", 1 point | GitLab only: an issue labeled `type::task`, closed by a merge request | the person assigned |
Table: The five levels of work, with their size limits

A **project** is the whole effort, with a start, an end and a purpose a manager can state in a sentence. Tidewater's
is "port the Tidewater system model from SysML v1 to SysML v2 in GitLab, then keep it current with the prime's
deliveries." A project lasts years. It is the level the program manager funds and the level the program's
leadership hears about.

A **tome** is a major body of work inside the project, the size of a large contract line item or a major phase. The
word is unusual on purpose (it is the agile kit's word, and it means "a big book"): it does not collide with any
term the team already uses, so nobody confuses a tome with a contractual "phase" or "increment." A tome must finish
within a year. Tidewater has three.

An **epic** is a body of work inside a tome that delivers one recognizable capability or outcome, the size of a work
package in an old WBS. An epic must finish within a semester, thirteen sprints. Tidewater has fourteen.

A **story** is a slice of work that produces something the team can show at the end of a sprint: a package with its
TODO lines closed, a pipeline job that blocks a bad change, a set of discrepancy reports sent to the prime. A story
must finish within one sprint. Stories are what the team plans with, commits to, and demonstrates.

A **task** is one step of a story that one person can finish in a week or less, usually a day or two: convert this
file, write that job, review the other merge request. Tasks are what the team does each day. They are tracked in
GitLab, where the work happens, and each model-changing task ends in exactly one merge request.

==== What a point is {#om-points}

Size is measured in **points**. On Tidewater one point is one **ideal person-day**: a day of focused work on the
task by the person who will do it, with no meetings, no interruptions and no waiting. Nobody has ideal days, and
that is fine. Points are not hours on a timecard. They are a unit for comparing pieces of work with each other and
with what the team has finished before.

Why ideal days rather than hours? Three reasons the team found persuasive:

- An estimate in hours invites false precision. Nobody knows whether converting a package will take 6 or 9 hours.
  Most people can tell whether it is a one-day job or a three-day job.
- Hours get compared with timecards, and then the conversation becomes about the timecard, not the work.
- Ideal days are easy to sanity-check against the calendar. A person has about eight points of capacity in a
  ten-day sprint once meetings, email and interruptions are taken out (see [[om-capacity]]).

The size limits translate directly: a task is at most 5 points (a person's working week), and a story is at most
what fits in one sprint for the people working on it, which on Tidewater rarely exceeds 6 points.

::: note Why the agile kit says "task" for what Tidewater calls a story
The kit's own documentation calls a journal item a *task* and gives it the limit "under one sprint." That is the
same thing Tidewater calls a *story*. Tidewater added a smaller level underneath, the one-week task, and keeps it
only in GitLab. If you read the kit's help (`:help agile`) alongside this book, translate the kit's "task" as
Tidewater's "story." The reason for the split is in [[om-where]].
:::

==== Why each limit is where it is {#om-limits}

The limits are not arbitrary. Each one is the longest a piece of work can run before the team loses the ability to
see whether it is going well.

**A task under one week** keeps every merge request small enough to review in under an hour. A merge request that
changes forty lines can be read line by line; one that changes four thousand gets a glance and an approval, which
is the review-by-screenshot habit of the Cameo years ([[cameo-review]]) in a new form. It also means the pipeline
gives feedback within days of the work starting, not at the end of a month.

**A story under one sprint** means every story either finishes and is demonstrated at the sprint review, or visibly
does not. There is no "eighty percent done" in a sprint review; there is done, and there is carried over. A story
that is carried over is a signal, and the team looks at why (see [[om-splitting]]).

**An epic under one semester** keeps the medium-range plan honest. A semester is about the horizon over which a
team can predict its own work with any confidence. An epic that would run longer is really two outcomes, and
splitting it gives the program manager two points at which to say "that one is done."

**A tome under one year** matches the program's planning and funding cycle. The program plans and budgets by fiscal
year; a tome that crosses two fiscal years becomes hard to fund and harder to report.

**A project of years** is whatever the mission needs. The limits below it are what make a long project tractable:
a project of thirty-nine sprints is thirty-nine short, visible, re-planned cycles, not one long one.

::: objection "Real systems engineering work doesn't come in one-week pieces. An ICD takes months."
Answer: An ICD took months because it was one deliverable reviewed at the end. The work inside it was always made
of smaller pieces: agree the message list, define each message, reconcile the units, review with the other
segment. Each of those is a task or a story. What changes is that each piece is finished, reviewed and merged on its
own, so the ICD is always in a known state instead of being "in work" for months. In Tidewater the equivalent of the
ICD is the `Interfaces` package; it was ported, cleaned up and reviewed in five stories over three sprints, and was
never in an unknown state. See [[sprint-3]].
:::

==== Splitting work that is too big {#om-splitting}

The skill the team practiced most in the first twelve weeks was not git or SysML. It was splitting. Every planning
session found at least one story that was too big, and the team learned a handful of ways to cut it.

|| Too big | Why it is too big | Split it by | Resulting stories ||
| Port the Stations package | 25 members, 18 TODO lines, two reviewers | sub-area | Stations level tags; Stations procured parts; Power system and parametric |
| Set up the pipeline | unknown amount of learning | first useful slice | Pipeline runs grammar and lint on every MR; then outline and TODO jobs; then trace |
| Import the requirements | three DOORS modules, drift to investigate | output vs investigation | Import the DOORS modules; Report drift and gaps to the prime |
| Write the security plan | the ISSM's review cycle is outside the team | what the team controls | Draft the toolchain description; ISSM review meeting; Answer the ISSM's comments |
| Behavior TODOs | four different constructs | construct | State machine; fork, pin and object flow; constraint; interaction |
Table: Splitting stories that would not fit in a sprint

Three rules of thumb came out of those sessions:

1. **Split by result, not by activity.** "Analyze the Stations package" and "Fix the Stations package" are not a
   good split, because the first produces nothing anyone can see. "Stations level tags" and "Stations procured
   parts" each end in a merged change.
2. **Pull out the waiting.** Anything that depends on someone outside the team (the ISSM, the enterprise GitLab
   administrators, the prime) becomes its own story or task, so the rest of the work does not wait with it.
3. **When in doubt, do the smallest useful thing first.** The first pipeline story was two jobs. It was the most
   valuable story in sprint 1, because it proved the whole chain (branch, merge request, pipeline, approval, merge)
   worked end to end. Everything after it was adding jobs to a chain that already worked.

=== Where each level lives {#om-where}

Each level lives in the place where the people who use it already look.

**Stories, epics and tomes live in the team's journal**, the plain-text ledger the agile kit keeps for each project
([[journal]]). Each story is one journal item, with an id like `TW-12` (the number is the story's GitLab issue
number, so the two always match), a size in points, an epic (`e:`) and a tome (`t:`). The journal is where the
sprint's commitment, capacity, done and carried-over work are recorded, and where velocity, burn-down and the weekly
report come from. It is the part of the operating model Jim owns and reports from.

**Tasks live only in GitLab.** Each task is a GitLab issue, labeled `type::task`, linked to its story's issue
("Part of #12"), and, if it changes the model, closed automatically by the merge request that does the work. That
is where Bob and Tom spend their day.

Why not put tasks in the journal too? Because the kit follows a rule that turned out to suit the team well: the
architect's record holds what the architect is accountable for, and the team's day-to-day breakdown stays in the
team's own tool. Jim is accountable for stories finishing in the sprint they were committed to; Jim does not need,
and should not manage, which of Bob's three tasks is done by Wednesday. GitLab already records that, in detail, for
anyone who wants to look.

The mapping to GitLab objects depends on which tier of GitLab the organization licenses. Tidewater's enterprise
GitLab is the Free tier (GitLab's name for its unpaid edition, which in an enterprise installation is still
supported by the administrators), so the team used labels and milestones for the upper levels:

|| Level | GitLab Free (what Tidewater used) | GitLab Premium or Ultimate (if you have it) ||
| Project | a group (`tidewater`) holding the model project | the same |
| Tome | a scoped label `tome::port` | a parent epic (Ultimate supports multi-level epics) |
| Epic | a scoped label `epic::structure` | a group epic, with child issues and a roadmap |
| Sprint | a milestone "Sprint 3" with start and due dates | an iteration in an iteration cadence |
| Story | an issue labeled `type::story` | the same, attached to its epic |
| Task | an issue labeled `type::task`, related to its story | a child task (a GitLab "task" work item) or the same issue |
Table: The levels in GitLab, by tier

A **scoped label** is a GitLab label whose name contains `::`. An issue can carry only one label from each scope, so
adding `workflow::review` to an issue automatically removes `workflow::in-progress`. That one feature is what makes
labels usable as fields: an issue's type, epic, tome and workflow state are each exactly one value. [[gitlab-setup]]
creates them from a file.

=== The cadence {#om-cadence}

Every sprint on Tidewater is ten working days, Monday of week one to Friday of week two, and every sprint has the
same shape. The team's calendar invitations were created once, in week zero, and never changed.

|| Day | When | Event | Length | Who | Produces ||
| Day 1 (Monday) | 09:00 | Sprint kickoff and planning | 90 minutes | team; program manager invited for the first 15 | the sprint goal, capacity, committed stories, task breakdown, poker estimates |
| Every day | 09:00 | Stand-up | 15 minutes | team | three terse lines per person (yesterday, today, blockers); blockers become actions |
| Every Friday | 15:00 | Weekly note | 20 minutes (Jim alone) | Jim | the weekly quad page to the program manager |
| Wednesday of week 2 | 10:00 | Backlog refinement | 60 minutes | team | next sprint's stories meet the Definition of Ready; poker estimates |
| Day 10 (Friday) | 10:00 | Sprint review (demo) | 60 minutes | team, program manager, deputy program manager; prime's modeler when a drop is involved | live demonstration from GitLab; scorecard; feedback |
| Day 10 (Friday) | 11:00 | Retrospective | 60 minutes | team only | start, stop, continue; the pulse survey; one improvement for next sprint |
Table: The two-week cadence

[[ceremonies]] describes each event in detail, with the real chat pastes, poker rounds and retrospective notes from
the twelve weeks. Here the point is the shape: about {{value: docs/tidewater/work-split.json ceremony_hours.total}} hours of meetings per person per sprint (averaged over the
first six sprints, from the meeting records), out of eighty
hours. In the document era, Bob counted the team's recurring meetings in a typical fortnight (status meetings,
working groups, review dry-runs, the CCB) and got eleven hours, not counting the review packages themselves.

**Why two weeks?** The team considered one week and three. One week was too short to finish anything that involved
the ISSM or the prime, and planning every week would have eaten a day a month. Three weeks was too long to wait for
feedback in the first quarter, when everything was new. Two weeks gave six sprints in the twelve weeks the directive
allowed, which meant six reviews at which the program manager could see progress, and six retrospectives at which
the team could fix what was not working.

**Holidays do not move the cadence.** When a holiday falls in a sprint (12 October in sprint 1, 11 November in sprint
3, 26 November in sprint 4, 25 December in sprint 6), the sprint keeps its dates and loses capacity. The events on
the holiday move to the nearest working day. A cadence that shifts with every holiday stops being a cadence.

=== Capacity {#om-capacity}

**Capacity** is how many points the team can expect to finish in a sprint, worked out before planning from the
calendar, not from hope.

Each person has a standing capacity per full sprint, agreed in week zero:

- **Jim: 5 points.** Jim is the MBSE lead and spends roughly half of every sprint on things that are not stories:
  the program manager, the prime, the CCB, reviews of Bob's and Tom's merge requests, the weekly note. Five points
  is Jim's honest remainder.
- **Bob: 8 points.** Ten working days, minus about two days for meetings, reviews of others' work, email and the
  unexpected.
- **Tom: 8 points.** The same.

Then, before each planning session, the calendar is applied. A holiday removes a day for everyone; leave removes it
for one person. Capacity is prorated by working days available out of ten. Sprint 1 had one holiday (Monday 12
October), so everyone had nine days:

|| Person | Standing capacity | Days available | Sprint 1 capacity ||
| Jim | 5 | 9 of 10 | 4.5 |
| Bob | 8 | 9 of 10 | 7.2 |
| Tom | 8 | 9 of 10 | 7.2 |
| Team | 21 | | the sum |
Table: Sprint 1 capacity

The kickoff record shows the result, and the commitment the team made against it:

{{include: docs/tidewater/sprints/01/kickoff.txt | lines 4-5}}

The team then commits to between 85 and 95 percent of capacity. The margin is not padding; it is room for the
unplanned work that every sprint contains (a pipeline that breaks, a question from the prime, a review that takes
longer than it should). Sprint 1's commitment, in the record above, is inside that band.

Capacity is recorded in the journal with the stand-up verb `cap` on the first day of the sprint. The kit's metrics
compare each sprint's load (committed divided by capacity) against two thresholds: above 110 percent is overload,
below 70 percent is under-use. Both raise a signal in the weekly report ([[journal]]).

::: warning The temptation in sprint 1
On the Friday before sprint 1, Jim's first draft plan held 26 points: everything the team wanted done by the first
review. Tom did the capacity arithmetic on the whiteboard and the draft went to {{value: docs/tidewater/sprints/01/scorecard.json 11_delivery.committed}}, with a learning story and a laptop story
that the first draft had left out. Jim later said that cutting those points was the single most useful thing done in week zero, because it meant sprint 1 finished. A first
sprint that finishes everything it committed to is worth far more to a skeptical team than one that attempts
everything and finishes half.
:::

=== Two ways to count "done" {#om-done}

The book uses two measures of finished work, and says which one every time:

- **Story points done** is the journal's measure. A story counts only when all of its tasks are done; a story with
  one open task counts zero, and its remaining points carry over. This is the number behind velocity,
  predictability and the sprint report: sprint 4 finished
  {{value: docs/tidewater/sprints/04/scorecard.json 11_delivery.done}} of
  {{value: docs/tidewater/sprints/04/scorecard.json 11_delivery.committed}} story points, because the Stations story
  carried over.
- **Task points done** is GitLab's measure: every task closed in the sprint counts, including the finished tasks of
  a story that carried over. It is the number behind the work mix (engineering, platform, learning) in the [[bluf]]
  and the scorecard: sprint 4 closed {{value: docs/tidewater/work-split.json sprints.4.points}} task points.

Neither is wrong. Story points done tells management what was *delivered*; task points done tells the team where the
*effort* went. Mixing them in one sentence is the mistake.

=== Roles and decisions {#om-roles}

Three people do the work. Their roles were assigned in Jim's email ([[directive-email]]) and written into the
project's settings, so that GitLab enforces them.

**Jim, MBSE lead.** Owns the model's content and direction: what must be in it, which work comes first (the order
of the backlog), what "done" means. Owns tomes and epics, plans each sprint with the team, approves merge requests
as a code owner, creates baselines, reports to the program manager, and talks to the prime about the content of
each drop. In GitLab, a maintainer.

**Bob, MBSE engineer.** Does most of the model work: converts, cleans up, writes what the converter cannot,
reviews Tom's changes to the model. Owns the model's structure in day-to-day terms. In GitLab, a developer: Bob can
push feature branches and open merge requests but cannot push to protected branches or create baseline tags.

**Tom, configuration management and DevSecOps engineer.** Owns the pipeline, the runner, the project settings, the
vendor branch where prime drops land, and the mechanics of releases and baselines. Reviews Bob's changes to the
pipeline and project files. In GitLab, a maintainer.

Around them:

|| Activity | Jim | Bob | Tom | Program manager | Deputy PM (CCB chair) | ISSM | Prime | GitLab admins ||
| Set project priorities and the backlog order | A/R | C | C | C | I | | | |
| Plan and commit a sprint | A | R | R | I | | | | |
| Change the model (a feature branch and MR) | C | R | R | | | | | |
| Approve a model change (code owner) | R | R | C | | | | | |
| Change the pipeline or project settings | C | C | A/R | | | C | | C |
| Create a baseline (tag and release) | A | C | R | I | C | | I | |
| Accept a prime drop | A | R | R | I | I | | C | |
| Raise a discrepancy report to the prime | A/R | R | C | I | | | I | |
| Approve the toolchain for use | C | | R | I | | A | | C |
| Provide accounts, runner registration | | | R | | | | | A |
| Report progress | A/R | C | C | I | I | | | |
Table: Who does what (R responsible, A accountable, C consulted, I informed)

A **RACI** table like this one is familiar from the document era; what is new is that most of its cells are enforced.
Bob cannot create a baseline because the protected-tag setting allows only maintainers. A change cannot reach the
integration branch without a code owner's approval because the protected-branch setting requires it. The ISSM's
"A" on toolchain approval is not enforced by GitLab, which is why it is a story with its own tasks ([[sprint-2]]).

==== What happened to the CCB {#om-ccb}

The deputy program manager's first question, when Jim briefed the operating model, was: "Where does my CCB fit?"

The answer the team worked out, and the deputy accepted after the sprint 2 review, has three parts:

1. **Day-to-day model changes do not go to the CCB.** They are made on feature branches, reviewed in merge
   requests, checked by the pipeline and approved by a code owner. The approval, the reviewer's comments, the
   pipeline results and the change itself are recorded together in GitLab, permanently. That record is more
   complete than any CCB minutes the program ever kept.
2. **Baselines go to the CCB.** A baseline (a protected tag such as `baseline-1`, built from a release branch merged
   into `main`) is what analyses, contracts and other programs cite. Creating one requires Jim's approval in GitLab
   and the deputy's concurrence, recorded as a comment on the release merge request. The CCB reviews the release
   notes, which are generated from the merge requests since the last baseline, not written by hand.
3. **Disagreements go to the CCB.** If a reviewer and an author cannot agree in a merge request, or a change would
   affect another program's interfaces, the merge request is labeled for the CCB and waits. In twelve weeks this
   happened once.

The deputy's question ("can a change be traced from request to approval to implementation?") is answered by a chain
of links that GitLab maintains automatically: the issue that asked for the change, the merge request that made it
(which says "Closes #N"), the pipeline that checked it, the approval, and the merge commit on `develop`.
[[devsecops-change]] follows one change along that chain.

=== Definitions of Ready and Done {#om-dor-dod}

Two short checklists govern the edges of every sprint. The **Definition of Ready** says when a story may be pulled
into a sprint; the **Definition of Done** says when a story may be called finished. Both live in the project's
wiki, under version control, and both changed several times in the twelve weeks as the team learned what mattered.

The Definition of Ready exists because the most common reason a story was carried over in the document era was
that nobody knew what it meant when work started. The Definition of Done exists because "done" meant something
different to each of the three people until it was written down. Jim's first draft of each was a page long. The
versions that worked are below; the cut-down versions are what the team actually checked at refinement and review.

=== The decisions the team wrote down {#om-decisions}

The last part of the operating model is a short list of decisions that shape everything else, each with its reason
and the condition under which the team would revisit it. Jim kept them as a numbered list on the wiki's Home page
and added to it whenever the team argued about something for more than ten minutes. These ten were made in week
zero and sprint 1; each is explained fully in the chapter shown.

|| # | Decision | Why | Revisit if ||
| D-1 | The v2 model is SysML v2 textual notation in a git repository | text can be compared, merged, checked and reviewed line by line; see [[sysmlv2]] | the program mandates a SysML v2 tool with its own repository |
| D-2 | Gitflow branching: `develop` is the default (integration) branch; `main` holds baselines only | baselines are released to other programs; day-to-day work must never wait on a release; see [[git-strategies]] | releases stop mattering (then switch to GitLab flow) |
| D-3 | Every merge is a merge commit; no squash, no fast-forward | the history of who changed what feeds the metrics and the audit trail | never |
| D-4 | DOORS stays authoritative for requirement text; the model holds a converted copy | the prime and the program manage requirements in DOORS; the trace job reports drift | the program moves requirements into the model |
| D-5 | Prime drops land on a vendor branch, converted and never hand-edited, and are merged into `develop` | git can then merge the prime's changes with the team's own edits; see [[git]] and [[maintenance]] | the prime delivers SysML v2 directly |
| D-6 | The team never edits the v1 model; problems in it go to the prime as discrepancy reports | the prime owns the v1 model under the contract | the contract changes ownership |
| D-7 | Conversion debt (TODO lines left by the converter) is the primary port progress measure | it is computed, countable and indisputable; see [[directive-scorecard]] | the TODO count reaches zero |
| D-8 | Tasks are at most 5 points; stories fit one sprint; tasks live in GitLab, stories in the journal | small merge requests are reviewed properly; see [[om-limits]] | the team grows past about eight people |
| D-9 | The pipeline runs on a Windows shell runner with Git for Windows' Perl | the same tools as the laptops; nothing new to approve; see [[tools]] | the enterprise offers an approved Linux runner with Perl |
| D-10 | Everything that can be a file is a file under version control: settings, templates, pipeline, wiki, decisions | it can be reviewed, rebuilt and audited | never |
Table: The decisions behind the operating model

A **decision record** need not be long. The team used this template in the wiki, one per decision, and the table
above is their index:

```md
## D-5 Prime drops land on a vendor branch

**Decided:** 2026-10-07, Jim (with Bob and Tom)
**Context:** The prime keeps changing the v1 model and delivers it every quarter. We
will edit the converted v2 text (close TODOs, remove clutter). We need each new
drop's changes without losing ours.
**Decision:** Convert each drop as delivered onto `vendor/prime` (never hand-edited)
and merge that branch into `develop` through a merge request.
**Consequences:** Git does a three-way merge; conflicts appear only where both
sides changed the same lines. Converter output paths must stay stable.
**Revisit if:** the prime delivers SysML v2 directly.
```

::: sidebar Writing it down is the point
Bob, who argued hardest against several of these decisions, said at the sprint 3 retrospective that the decision
list was the thing that made the arguments bearable. "When I lost an argument, it was written down with the reason
and the condition for changing it. I didn't have to keep re-fighting it; I had to watch for the condition." Two of
the ten were revisited in the eighteen months. Neither was reversed.
:::

=== What the operating model is not {#om-not}

It is not Scrum, SAFe or any other branded method, although it borrows freely from them, and the team did not
attend a certification course. Jim's view was that the program needed a way of working that three systems engineers
could explain to a program manager in five minutes, not a method that needed a coach.

It is not a replacement for the program's systems engineering processes. Requirements are still managed in DOORS.
The CCB still meets. The SEMP still exists, with a pointer to the wiki. What changes is the unit of work (small),
the rhythm (two weeks, repeated), and the enforcement (by the platform).

It is not fixed. The operating model page changed eleven times in twelve weeks, every change through a merge
request like any other file. The retrospective at the end of each sprint is where changes to it are proposed.

=== Applying this to your project {#om-apply}

Copy these as they are:

- **The size limits.** Task under a week, story under a sprint, epic under a semester, tome under a year. They work
  for any team of two to eight people doing model work.
- **Points as ideal person-days**, with standing capacity per person and prorating for holidays and leave.
- **The cadence**: one planning day, a daily fifteen-minute stand-up, a mid-sprint refinement, a review and a
  retrospective at the end. Book it all in the calendar once.
- **Writing decisions down** with reason and revisit condition.

Adapt these to your situation:

- **The number of tomes and epics.** Draw your own tree. Start from the outcomes your program manager will
  recognize, then check each against the size limits and split what does not fit.
- **The RACI around the team.** Your CCB, security approver and contractor relationships will differ. The question
  to answer for each is: which of their decisions can be enforced by a GitLab setting, and which must be a story?
- **The GitLab mapping.** If your organization licenses Premium or Ultimate, use epics and iterations; the
  operating model does not change.

Decide these locally, before sprint 1:

- Each person's standing capacity. Be honest about how much of the lead's time goes to things that are not stories.
- Who approves what, and who may create a baseline.
- Your decision list. Ten decisions is a good start; do not try to decide everything in advance.

=== Exercises {#om-exercises}

::: exercise {#ex-om-size} Size the tasks
Take a work package from your own program (a document section, an interface definition, a test procedure) and
split it into stories that each fit in one sprint and tasks that each fit in one week. Give each task a size in
ideal person-days. Which split did you find hardest, and which of the three rules of thumb in [[om-splitting]]
helped?
:::

::: exercise {#ex-om-split} Split a story
The story "Close the Behavior TODOs" covers a state machine, an interaction, a fork with pins and object flows, and a
constraint expression. Bob estimated it at 11 points. Split it into stories of at most 6 points each, by the rule
"split by result." Name each resulting story so that its end state is something that can be demonstrated.
:::

::: exercise {#ex-om-capacity} Compute a sprint's capacity
Sprint 4 runs 16 to 27 November 2026. Thanksgiving (26 November) is a holiday, and Jim takes leave on Friday 27
November. Using the standing capacities above, compute each person's capacity and the team's, and the range of
points the team should commit to.
:::

::: exercise {#ex-om-decision} Write a decision record
Write a decision record, in the template's format, for one decision your own team has argued about more than once
(a naming convention, a review rule, a tool choice). Include the condition under which you would revisit it.
:::

::: exercise {#ex-om-raci} Enforce a RACI cell
Pick three cells of your own team's RACI. For each, say whether a GitLab setting could enforce it (which one?), or
whether it must be handled as a story with tasks. Use [[gitlab-setup]] once you have read it to check your answers.
:::
