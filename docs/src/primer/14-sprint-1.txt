== Sprint 1: A GitLab project the team can work in {#sprint-1}

::: bridge
You know: the first weeks of a new program: kickoffs, accounts, and a plan nobody believes yet.

Here it is called: sprint 1: planning, stand-ups, the first drop and the first pipeline.

What is new: by day ten, something real is finished and measured.
:::

::: survival
What you have: three laptops that have never run git, a prime drop that arrived the Friday before, a directive, and a
team that would rather be writing documents.

What you need from this sprint: a GitLab project with its rules switched on, laptops that pass the kit's doctor, the
drop converted and measured, and an honest estimate of what the port will cost. Nothing in the model changes yet.
:::

=== The kickoff: Monday 5 October, 09:00 {#sprint-1-kickoff}

The kickoff ran ninety minutes and replaced the day's stand-up. Jim opened with the goal Jim had written on the
whiteboard the night before:

> A GitLab project the team can work in; drop 1 received and assessed.

Then capacity. The standing numbers ([[operating-model]]) are five points a sprint for Jim, whose other duties do not
stop for a port, and eight each for Bob and Tom. Monday 12 October was a federal holiday, so each person's capacity
was prorated by working days. Then the commitment, kept deliberately below capacity. The kit printed the result as
the kickoff record, and this is the plan the team worked to for the next two weeks, exactly as it was recorded:

{{include: docs/tidewater/sprints/01/kickoff.txt}}

Read the plan from the top down and three decisions stand out.

**The learning story comes first.** TW-1, "Learn the tools," is a story with points, an owner per task and a done date,
like any other. It is {{value: docs/tidewater/work-split.json sprints.1.learning}}% of the sprint's task points done ([[om-done]]). Jim's
argument, made to the program manager in week zero and repeated to the team at the kickoff, was that the
learning would happen either way: on the board, where it is visible and can be protected, or on nights and weekends,
where it becomes resentment. Putting it on the board also made it measurable, which mattered later when management
asked what the overhead had cost ([[bluf]]).

**Most of the sprint is platform work.** Laptops, the GitLab project, the first pipeline, the working agreements: none
of it changes the model. That is the honest shape of a first sprint, and the team said so in the plan rather than
dressing it up. Only TW-5 and TW-6, receiving and assessing the drop, are engineering.

**The commitment is below capacity.** The kickoff line says how far below. Jim's first draft had been much larger
([[operating-model]]); cutting it was, by Jim's own account later, the most useful thing Jim did in week zero, because it
meant sprint 1 finished.

The journal holds the same numbers. This is the sprint report the kit prints from the ledger, run against the
simulation's journal:

```sh run sandbox=tidewater expect="Model\s+18\.9\s+16\.5\s+16\.5"
perl "$KIT/bin/daily.pl" --conf journal/scrum.conf sprint 1
```

==== Planning poker, the first time {#sprint-1-poker}

Each story was estimated in the meeting chat with a `#est` round: Jim posted the story, each person posted a number,
and the kit's tally ([[ceremonies]]) read the chat afterwards. When the votes spread, Jim did what the method asks:
the highest and lowest voters said what they knew, and the team voted again. The round that taught the team the most
was the one for converting and assessing the drop:

{{include: docs/tidewater/sprints/01/poker-2026-10-05.txt | lines 176-187}}

The spread was wide. The high vote came from Bob, who had spent two years of evenings fighting Cameo's exports and
expected the conversion to be a week of hand work. The low vote came from Jim, who had run the converter once in week
zero and knew it does the bulk mechanically. The conversation in between was the useful part: the story was not
"convert the model" but "convert it and *measure* what is left," and the measuring is a report, not hand work. The
second round converged. Bob's worry did not disappear; it moved, correctly, into the later stories that close the TODO
lines the converter leaves behind.

::: tip What poker is for
A spread is information, not a failure. If everyone votes the same number at once, either the story is well
understood or nobody has thought about it. The second round matters less than the sentence each outlier says
between the rounds. In the chat record the sentences are not there, only the numbers; write them into the story's
notes when they change the plan.
:::

==== Thursday 8: drift in two minutes {#sprint-1-drift}

The best moment of sprint 1 happened on its fourth day, and it happened to the team's biggest skeptic. Bob ran both
converters on drop 1 for TW-6, the first time anyone on the team had used them on the real drop. The XMI converter
reported what it carried and what it could not:

{{include: docs/tidewater/sprints/01/drop1-xmi2sysml-report.txt | lines 54-56}}

Then the requirements converter compared the prime's DOORS text with the requirement text inside the prime's own
model, and printed this at the bottom:

{{include: docs/tidewater/sprints/01/drop1-trace.txt | lines 21-32}}

The last three lines are the drift: the same requirement, TW-SYS-006, says "twice a day" in DOORS and does not in the
model. In the document era nobody would have found it until a review, if then, because finding it meant reading two
documents side by side. Bob posted in the chat:

{{include: docs/tidewater/sprints/01/quotes.txt | lines 3-3}}

You can do what Bob did. These two commands convert the drop into a scratch folder with the team's layout map and run
the trace against it:

```sh run sandbox=tidewater expect="drift: TW-SYS-006"
perl "$KIT/bin/xmi2sysml.pl" --out s1/model --layout-map "$KIT/examples/tidewater-v1/layout.map" drops/v1.0/tidewater.xmi
perl "$KIT/bin/reqif2sysml.pl" --report --model s1/model drops/v1.0/tidewater.reqif
```

and this counts the TODO lines the converter left, which is the port's starting debt:

```sh run expect="^52$"
grep -r "// TODO" s1/model | wc -l
```

::: sidebar Why the drift mattered more than the number
The converter's numbers were reassuring: everything in scope ported, and a manageable set of TODO lines. But numbers
like that do not change minds; they confirm what the person who ran the tool already believed. The drift changed a
mind, because it was a real defect in a real deliverable, found in two minutes by the person who least wanted to be
there. If you are leading a team like this one, look for that moment early and let the skeptic have it.
:::

The drift became the team's first discrepancy report to the prime later, in sprint 4 ([[sprint-4]]). In sprint 1 it
stayed a note on TW-6, because the team had not yet agreed how to report discrepancies, and Jim did not want the first
thing the prime received from the new process to be improvised.

=== Week two {#sprint-1-week-2}

Monday 12 October was a holiday. The second week was four days.

==== Tuesday 13: proving a refusal {#sprint-1-refusal}

Tom's task #15 sounds odd for a systems engineer: prove that a push is *refused*. But a rule you have never seen fire
is a rule you do not know works. Tom pushed a commit straight to the protected integration branch, and GitLab
answered:

{{include: docs/tidewater/sprints/01/protect-proof.txt}}

That rejection is the configuration management board's rule, enforced by software: nothing reaches the integration
branch except through a reviewed merge request. Tom saved the output to the issue as evidence and closed it.

The same day Jim checked the drop against its manifest. Every file the prime delivered is listed in the manifest with
a SHA-256 checksum, and checking them takes one line. The `tr` at the front removes Windows line endings from the
manifest, which the prime's build writes:

```sh run expect="tidewater\.xmi: OK"
cd drops/v1.0 && tr -d '\r' < MANIFEST.txt | sed -n 's/^  \([0-9a-f]\{64\}\)  /\1  /p' | sha256sum -c
```

The first time Jim ran it without the `tr`, every file "failed," which is the kind of thing that ruins a morning if
you do not know about line endings. It is in the rescue appendix now ([[rescue]]).

==== Friday 16: a red pipeline on a laptop {#sprint-1-local-ci}

Tom's last task was to run the draft pipeline on Tom's laptop against drop 1, with the kit's local runner ([[cicd]]).
There was still no GitLab runner, so this was the only way to see the pipeline work. It did not pass:

{{include: docs/tidewater/sprints/01/local-ci-draft.txt}}

Grammar and lint passed: the converted model is valid SysML v2 and structurally sound. The outline check failed,
because the converted packages and folders do not meet the rule that every parent has between two and nine children.
That is not a defect Tom introduced; it is what the prime's model looks like.

This is the first real decision of the port, and it is worth slowing down for, because every team porting an
inherited model will face it.

::: objection "Just turn the outline check off until the port is done"
That was Tom's first suggestion, and it is the most common one. The trouble is that a check you have turned off does
not come back on by itself; the team would have shipped baseline-0 with the check still off.

Answer: The team kept the check and gave it an *allowance*: the number of violations the drop arrived with is
recorded in a file in the repository, the pipeline fails only if a change makes the number bigger, and every change
that makes it smaller lowers the allowance in the same merge request. The inherited debt is visible, counted and
paid down; new debt is refused. This is a *ratchet*, and the team used the same idea later for the trace and
complexity checks ([[pipeline]]).
:::

{{include: docs/tidewater/sprints/01/review.txt}}

=== What went wrong, and why it was fine {#sprint-1-wrong}

- **The first stand-ups failed the lint.** Expected, cheap to fix, and fixed by the habit rather than by a rule.
- **The first merge request had no pipeline.** Unavoidable without a runner; the description said so, which is what
  made it acceptable.
- **Checklist boxes ticked by habit.** Not caught in review. A template checklist is only worth what the person
  ticking it actually did.
- **The first pipeline was red.** On a laptop, before anything depended on it, which is the cheapest place for a
  pipeline to be red. The red outline check produced the ratchet, one of the most useful ideas of the twelve weeks.
- **The drift was found and then parked.** Deliberately: the team wanted an agreed discrepancy-report route before
  sending the prime anything.

=== You can now... {#sprint-1-can-now}

- run `kickoff.pl doctor` and act on its output;
- read a stand-up lint and fix a status that fails it;
- run planning poker and use the spread, not just the result;
- convert a prime drop, read the converter's report, and count the conversion debt;
- run the trace and find text drift between DOORS and the model;
- verify a delivery against its manifest;
- explain why a gate is switched on only when the thing it checks exists;
- explain a ratchet, and why it beats turning a check off.

=== Applying this to your project {#sprint-1-applying}

- **Make the first sprint about the shop and say so.** Laptops, the project, the rules, the first pipeline on a laptop.
  Do not promise model progress in sprint 1; promise a measured starting point.
- **Put learning on the board** with points and owners. It is the first thing management will ask about later.
- **Measure the inherited work before you estimate it.** Run the converters, count the debt, run the trace. The first
  real finding is your best argument; let the person who doubts the most find it.
- **Switch gates on in the order the things they check come into being,** and prove each one refuses what it should.
- **Ratchet inherited debt** instead of switching checks off.
- **Ask the prime for stable ids and a manifest** before the first drop, not after.
