== Sprint 3: the first red X, DOORS in the model, and a wait nobody named {#sprint-3}

::: survival
What you have: a pipeline that gates every change ([[sprint-2]]), drop 1 on `develop` through the vendor branch, the
level conventions, and a team that has merged its first model changes but is still unsure it likes any of this.

What you need: the DOORS requirements inside the model next to the prime's copy, a report on every change that says
which requirements have no design and no test, and a way of working with the pipeline when it says no. You also need
to learn that a wait you do not say out loud is a blocker nobody can help with.
:::

Sprint 3 ran from Monday 2 November to Friday 13 November 2026. It is the sprint the team remembers as the low point,
and the survey agrees: the pulse fell from {{value: docs/tidewater/sprints/02/scorecard.json 12_morale}} at the end of
sprint 2 to {{value: docs/tidewater/sprints/03/scorecard.json 12_morale}}. Nothing went badly wrong. The pipeline
stopped one of Bob's changes, the security office took three days to sign a form, and the burn-down chart barely moved.
Those three things, each small, landed on people who had not yet seen the new way of working pay them back.

This chapter is worth reading slowly for that reason. Every team that makes this change has a sprint 3. The work gets
done; the feeling lags behind it. What the team did about the feeling matters as much as what it did about the model.

=== The question this sprint answered {#sprint-3-question}

Sprint 2 put the prime's model into GitLab. Sprint 3 asked the first question a systems engineer actually cares about:

> Do the requirements in our model match the requirements in DOORS, and which of them have nothing designed to meet
> them or nothing planned to verify them?

For thirty years the team answered that question with a spreadsheet, two days before a milestone review. Jim's
scorecard records the document-era answer time as {{value: docs/tidewater/sprints/03/scorecard.json document_era_baseline_notional.unverified_question_days}}
working days of spreadsheet work (a notional figure, from the program's last document-era review cycle). At the end of
this sprint the pipeline answers it on every change, in about
{{value: docs/tidewater/sprints/03/scorecard.json 4_trace_answer_seconds}} seconds of job time. That is the sprint's
real result. The rest of this chapter is how they got there and what it felt like.

Three things in that record are worth pausing on.

**The commitment is lower than last sprint's.** The team committed
{{value: docs/tidewater/sprints/03/scorecard.json 11_delivery.committed}} points against the capacity line above,
where in sprint 2 it had committed {{value: docs/tidewater/sprints/02/scorecard.json 11_delivery.committed}}. Sprint 2

::: note The thought process: commit to what you can finish, not to what you hope
A team that commits to everything it can imagine and finishes three quarters of it has told management nothing. A
team that commits to less and finishes all of it has told management exactly what it can do. The second team gets
trusted with more. Jim's rule, borrowed from the operating model ([[operating-model]]), is to commit no more than about
nine tenths of capacity and less when the sprint has a new kind of work in it.
:::

**Learning is still on the board.** The story TW-50, "Learn: the workbook second half; talking about progress", carries
{{value: docs/tidewater/sprints/03/scorecard.json work_mix.learning}} points: the second half of the SysML v2 workbook
([[workbook]]) for Bob, and for everyone the stand-up drafting tool `talk.pl` ([[talking]]). In sprint 1 learning was a
quarter of the board; here it is {{value: docs/tidewater/work-split.json sprints.3.learning}}% of the task points done ([[om-done]]),
and it keeps falling. That is the curve the BLUF promised management ([[bluf]]), and it only happens if the learning is
on the board where everyone can see it being spent.

**Platform work has shrunk.** In sprint 2, building the shop took more than half the board. This sprint the platform
share is {{value: docs/tidewater/work-split.json sprints.3.platform}}% and engineering is
{{value: docs/tidewater/work-split.json sprints.3.engineering}}% for the first time. Tom has two platform stories, the
trace and secrets jobs and the runner hardening, and both exist because a real need showed up, not because a list of
DevSecOps features said so.

Each story on that list was estimated with planning poker at the sprint 2 refinement and checked against the Definition
of Ready. The kickoff did not re-estimate. It cut each story into tasks of at most a few days, assigned them, and
committed. Ninety minutes, including the stand-up.

=== Week 1 {#sprint-3-week-1}

==== Monday 2 November: drafting the stand-up

The sprint 2 retrospective had produced one change to how the team talks: statuses should lead with an outcome and
carry one number. In sprint 2, not one stand-up line did; the scorecard's measure for it is
{{value: docs/tidewater/sprints/02/scorecard.json standup_outcome_share}}. People reported motion ("worked on the
pipeline") because that is what thirty years of status meetings had taught them to report.

So from this sprint the team drafts its statuses with `talk.pl`, which reads yesterday's GitLab activity and writes a
line in the kit's terse format, outcome first. Each person then edits the draft before posting it. The kit keeps both:

{{include: docs/tidewater/sprints/03/standups/2026-11-05-talk-drafts.txt}}

Look at what changed between draft and posted line: almost nothing. The drafts end with an ellipsis where a long task
title was cut; the posted lines drop it. That is the honest finding from the evidence. Once the board is kept up to
date, the draft is usually right, and editing it takes seconds. The value is not in the editing. It is that the draft
starts from what actually happened, so nobody has to remember yesterday at 09:00 or invent a number.

By the end of the sprint, {{value: docs/tidewater/sprints/03/scorecard.json standup_outcome_share}} of the stand-up
lines that reported work carried an outcome and a number.
Chapter [[talking]] explains why the draft helps engineers who would otherwise say nothing, and its limits.

==== Tuesday 3 November: the DOORS modules go onto the vendor branch

Jim's first task of the sprint was to put the DOORS export into the model. The prime's drop 1 included a ReqIF file
alongside the Cameo XMI. In sprint 2 the team converted only the XMI. Now Jim re-ran the import with both converters,
so the DOORS modules become SysML v2 packages under `model/requirements/` next to the Cameo copy of the requirements.

Jim did it on the vendor branch, not on `develop`, and that choice deserves a sentence because it was argued. Bob's
first instinct was to convert the ReqIF on a feature branch, since "it's just more files." Jim's answer was the rule
the team wrote down in sprint 2 (ADR 0002, the prime boundary): anything that comes from the prime enters through
`vendor/prime` and nowhere else. The DOORS export is the prime's. If it enters through a feature branch, the next drop
cannot merge cleanly against it, because git has no record of the prime's version to merge from. Chapter
[[prime-changes]] works through why the vendor branch is the only way the drops stay mergeable.

==== Friday 6 November: the red X {#sprint-3-red-x}

Bob opened merge request !9 at ten in the morning. The story was the last Network TODO line, a Cameo "include" between two use
cases. The converter had left it as a comment, because it does not map UML includes:

```text
// TODO uml:Include (xmi:id _22_1_7f3a0c1_08793d6918c3): no SysML v2 mapping in this converter
```

Bob replaced the TODO with what Bob believed was the SysML v2 form. You can see the edit in Bob's clone:

```sh run cwd=work/bob expect="include use case : QueryArchive queryArchive"
git show --format='%h %an %s' 62a57b0 -- model/physical/network/Network.sysml | grep '^[-+ ]  *include\|^[0-9a-f]'
```

The pipeline for !9 failed. Two jobs went red, grammar and lint, and the merge request showed a red X. This is the
first time the team saw the gate stop a change, and here is the part of the log that matters:

{{include: docs/tidewater/sprints/03/pipelines/mr9-first-23.log | lines 1-9}}

The grammar job ran the kit's checker against the official SysML v2 grammar and found the problem on line 39,
column 41: after `include use case :` it expected a name, a `:>` or another of the listed tokens, and it found
`QueryArchive queryArchive` instead. Bob had written the type before the name, the way Cameo shows a property in a
diagram compartment and the way many programming languages declare a variable. SysML v2 text puts the name first and
the type after a colon: `queryArchive : QueryArchive`.

You can reproduce the error yourself from the commit, which is still in the repository:

```sh run cwd=. expect="unexpected 'queryArchive'"
git -C work/bob show 62a57b0:model/physical/network/Network.sysml > network-before.sysml
perl "$KIT/tools/sysml/sysml.pl" check network-before.sysml || echo "exit status $?"
```

And the fixed file, from the commit Bob pushed to the same branch:

```sh run expect="0 error"
git -C work/bob show 472756e:model/physical/network/Network.sysml > network-after.sysml
perl "$KIT/tools/sysml/sysml.pl" check network-after.sysml
```

==== Reading the log, line by line {#sprint-3-log}

Nobody on the team had read a job log before October. It is worth taking one apart once, slowly, because every red
X from now on is read the same way.

|| Line in the log | What it tells you ||
| `pipeline #23 (merge_request_event, refs/merge-requests/9/merge) failed, 5 jobs` | which pipeline, why it ran (a merge request), what it tested (the merge of !9 into develop, not just Bob's branch), and the result |
| `==== grammar   check   failed` | one job: its name, its stage, its result, how long it took |
| `$ perl ci/job.pl grammar` | the command the job ran, exactly as you can run it yourself |
| `$ /usr/bin/perl $KIT/tools/sysml/sysml.pl check model/...` | what that command ran in turn: the grammar check on every model file |
| `model/physical/network/Network.sysml:39:41: error: unexpected 'queryArchive'; expected ...` | the finding: file, line, column, what was found and what the grammar allowed there |
| `sysml: 14 file(s), 1 error(s)` | the checker's summary |
| `ci-reports grammar: 14 check(s), 1 failed, 1 finding(s)` | the same result turned into the test and code-quality reports GitLab shows on the merge request |
Table: Reading a failing job log

Three habits follow from that table. Read the first line, so you know whether you are looking at your branch alone or
at the merge result. Scroll to the first line with `error:` in it, not the last line of the log, because later jobs often
fail *because of* the first error (here the lint job failed on the same syntax error). And copy the `$` command: it
runs the same way on your laptop, which is the fastest way to try a fix. In Vim, `:make` on the file puts the same error
in the quickfix list and jumps to line 39, column 41 ([[vim]]).

::: sidebar What "include use case" means
In the Cameo model, the use case ReviewObservations had a UML *include* relationship to QueryArchive: reviewing
observations always involves querying the archive. The converter has no mapping for UML includes, so it left a TODO
line naming the include and its xmi:id.

In SysML v2 text an include is a usage inside the including use case: `include use case queryArchive :
QueryArchive;` says that every performance of ReviewObservations includes a performance of QueryArchive, and gives
that included performance a name, `queryArchive`, that other parts of the model can refer to. The grammar check
confirms the line is well formed; the lint step confirms that `QueryArchive` resolves to the use case defined a few
lines below. Both are needed. The grammar check alone would accept a misspelled type name; [[workbook]] shows the
difference with a worked example.
:::

The second pipeline, #24, was green, Jim approved, and Bob merged !9 at 15:00 the same day. The whole episode, from
red X to merged, took one working morning.

Bob's reaction, recorded in the sprint's quotes, is the honest one:

> A red X on my own MR. One missing word, and the machine found it before Jim did.
> -- Bob, sprint 3

There are two ways to hear that sentence, and the team heard both. One is
embarrassment: a machine graded my work and I failed in front of my colleagues. The other is relief: the mistake never
reached the model, never reached Jim's review, and never reached the prime or the program office. In the Cameo years,
an error like this sat in the model until somebody tried to use the element, sometimes months later.

::: objection The machine is grading me
The objection, as Bob put it to Jim: "I have been doing this for twenty-six years. I do not need a script telling me
my syntax is wrong in front of everybody."

Answer: the pipeline is not a judge, and nobody sees it but the team. A red X on a merge request is the cheapest
possible place for a mistake to be found: before review, before merge, before anyone downstream relies on it. The
retrospective turned the complaint into a
practice the team kept: fix a red pipeline on the branch, not around it, and do not take it personally. The numbers
back the habit: in this sprint {{value: docs/tidewater/sprints/03/scorecard.json first_pipeline_pass.passed}} of
{{value: docs/tidewater/sprints/03/scorecard.json first_pipeline_pass.mrs}} merge requests passed on their first
pipeline, and the one that did not was green by lunch.
:::

Tom's !11 added the trace report to the pipeline, and a secrets check. The trace job runs the DOORS-to-model trace on
every pipeline. Here is what it printed on the first run, from the log of pipeline #29:

{{include: docs/tidewater/sprints/03/pipelines/mr11-first-29.log | lines 80-95}}

Read it slowly; this is the most important output of the sprint.

- **C, linked:** {{value: docs/tidewater/sprints/03/scorecard.json 5_trace.C}} DOORS requirements have a counterpart
  in the model with the same DOORS id. One does not: TW-SYS-016 is in DOORS and not in the prime's model at all.
- **F, orphans:** {{value: docs/tidewater/sprints/03/scorecard.json 5_trace.F}} requirements have nothing in the model

- **G, unverified:** {{value: docs/tidewater/sprints/03/scorecard.json 5_trace.G}} requirements have no verification
  case.
- **I, completeness:** {{value: docs/tidewater/sprints/03/scorecard.json 5_trace.I}} of requirements are both
  satisfied and verified.
- **Drift:** TW-SYS-006 says something different in DOORS than in the model's copy.

The drift line is the easiest to see with your own eyes. Both copies of TW-SYS-006 are in the model now, one from the
prime's Cameo export and one from the DOORS export:

```sh run cwd=work/bob expect="twice a day"
git grep -h 'doorsId = "TW-SYS-006"' origin/develop -- model/requirements | sed 's/ attribute .*//'
```

The Cameo copy says the data center shall publish a marine bulletin on a fixed schedule. DOORS says twice a day, on
a fixed schedule. Which wording is right is not the government team's call to make by editing a file: DOORS is the
requirements baseline the prime is contracted to, and the model is supposed to match it. The gap goes back to the
prime as a discrepancy report, DR-1, in [[sprint-4]].

How does the trace job know which copy is which? Every file the DOORS converter writes starts with a line that says
so:

```sh run expect="generated by reqif2sysml"
git show origin/develop:model/requirements/system/System_Requirements.sysml | head -2
```

The trace job compares DOORS, as exported, against the model *without* those generated packages; otherwise it would
compare DOORS with its own copy and find nothing. Chapter [[pipeline]] shows the rule in the job.

None of these gaps were made by the government team. They are in the prime's model and the prime's DOORS module, and
they had been there, unseen, since drop 1 arrived. The trace job made them visible. That is why Tom added it as
**report only**: the job prints the gaps and never fails a pipeline because of them. If it failed, every merge request
on the team would go red for problems none of them could fix, and the team would learn to ignore red. Chapter
[[pipeline]] shows the job; [[sprint-5]] shows the day it becomes a gate, and why only then.

::: note The thought process: a check you cannot pass teaches people to ignore checks
Tom's rule for adding a job: if the team cannot make it green by its own work, the job reports; it does not fail. The
gaps here belong to the prime. The right response is a discrepancy report to the prime ([[sprint-4]]), not a red
pipeline for Bob. A gate is for problems the person merging can fix.
:::

The secrets job is the "Sec" in DevSecOps, at its smallest useful size. It scans every tracked file for things that
look like keys, tokens or passwords and fails if it finds one. On the first run it found none in the repository's
tracked files. It took Tom less than an hour, it runs in seconds, and it closes the most likely security incident for a
modeling team: someone pastes a credential into a file and pushes it. Chapter [[security]] puts it in context.

{{include: docs/tidewater/sprints/03/standups/2026-11-10-chat.txt | lines 8-9}}

Read the last field. **B none.** Tom was waiting for three days on another office, and the stand-up said nothing
blocked the work. The kit's lint could not catch it, because a status that says "none" is a valid status. The journal
recorded no block. The weekly quad showed no watch items. Nobody asked Jim to call the ISSM.

At the retrospective, Tom said what had happened:

> Three days waiting on a signature. The pipeline was the fast part.
> -- Tom, 10 November

and the team wrote it down as the sprint's one thing to stop:

{{include: docs/tidewater/sprints/03/retro.txt | lines 6-7}}

This is the most useful failure in the twelve weeks, because it is the one every engineer recognizes. Engineers do
not like to say they are blocked. It sounds like an excuse, or like asking for help, or like criticizing another
office. So they say "none" and wait. The stand-up exists to catch exactly this, and it only works if waiting is said
out loud, with a date and an ask:

```text
Y TW-54 checklist in the wiki. T TW-54 runner service account, by Tue. B TW-54 waiting on the ISSM's approval since Fri -- need: Jim to ask for a date today
```

That line would have put the wait in the journal as a block, on the quad as a watch item, and in Jim's hands the
same morning. Chapter [[talking]] has the templates; the change the team made was simply to agree that a wait on
anyone outside the team for more than one day is a blocker and gets said.

==== Tuesday 10 November: the DOORS modules merge

Bob merged !10 on Tuesday, after reading the delta. The review note mentions the outline allowance. That is the file
`.ci/outline-max`, which records how many outline violations (packages with too few or too many members, folders with
a single file) the model is allowed to have. The prime's drop brings its own: the converted Requirements package has
more members than the 2-to-9 rule allows, because Cameo put every requirement in one package. The outline job does not
fail the drop for that. It records the new count as the allowance, and from then on any change that *adds* a violation
fails, while any change that removes one must lower the number. You can see the allowance move in the history:

```sh run cwd=work/bob expect="allowance 19 after drop v1.0"
git log --format='%ad %s' --date=short origin/develop -- .ci/outline-max | sed -n '2,3p'
```

That pattern, a measure that may only get better, is called a ratchet. The team uses it for every measure it cannot
make perfect on day one: outline violations here, trace gaps from [[sprint-5]], package complexity later
([[pipeline]]).

From this point the DOORS requirements live in the model, under
`model/requirements/stakeholder/`, `system/` and `segment/`, and the Cameo copy of the requirements lives beside them
under `model/requirements/model/`. Two copies of the same requirements may look wasteful. It is the opposite: DOORS is
the authoritative wording, the Cameo copy is what the prime's model actually links to, and keeping both is what lets
the trace job see where they disagree.

=== You can now {#sprint-3-you-can-now}

- Read a failing pipeline log, find the file, line and column, and reproduce the error locally with the grammar check.
- Explain the SysML v2 naming order (`name : Type`) and spot the v1 habit that writes it backwards.
- Read the trace report's C, F, G, I and drift lines and say which gaps are yours and which are the prime's.
- Explain why a new check starts as report only and becomes a gate only when the team can pass it.
- Say a wait out loud as a blocker, with a date and an ask.
- Use planning poker's outlier question to surface what one person knows.

Do practice lesson 11 ([[sprint-2]] shows the setup). It plants exactly the kind of mistake Bob made: a model change
with a syntax error. Watch the pipeline fail, read the job log, find the line, fix it on the branch, and push again.
Then write the stand-up line you would post the next morning, outcome first, with one number.
:::

=== Applying this to your project {#sprint-3-applying}

- **Expect a sprint 3.** Somewhere around week five or six, the novelty is gone and the payoff has not arrived. Measure
  it (a five-question pulse costs a minute) so you can see it, and do not argue with it.
- **Put your requirements source into the model next to the model's copy,** and run a trace on every change. The first
  report will show gaps that were always there. Decide in advance whose they are and how they will be reported.
- **Add every new check as report-only first.** Make it a gate only when the people merging can make it green.
- **Agree that waiting on anyone outside the team for more than a day is a blocker,** and that it is said at the
  stand-up with a date and an ask.
- **Close the issues you finish.** Every tool that reads the board, from the stand-up draft to the analytics, repeats
  your board's mistakes back to you.
