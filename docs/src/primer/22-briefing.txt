== The briefing {#briefing}

::: bridge
You know: the program review briefing.

Here it is called: the twelve-week briefing.

What is new: every number on it traces to a file in the repository, and the demo is live.
:::

::: survival Before you start
What you have: six sprints of evidence, the scorecard ([[scorecard]]), and twenty minutes on the program management
review agenda on 24 December.

What you need: a briefing that a program manager who has never opened GitLab can follow, that leads with conclusions,
shows the real thing working, admits what did not work, and ends with an ask.
:::

The directive of September promised management a briefing "at the December PMR." For twelve weeks that line hung
over the team. Bob's fear, said out loud in week one, was that the briefing would be judged on how polished the
slides were, the way briefings always had been. Jim's answer was to build the briefing the same way the team now
built everything else: from the evidence, rehearsed, reviewed, with as few slides as possible and one live
demonstration. This chapter is that briefing, slide by slide, with the reasoning behind each choice.

=== Preparing it {#briefing-prep}

The preparation took one story in sprint 6 ("Retrospective, the management briefing and the plan for next
semester," two points, Jim) and used the same rhythm as every sprint review since sprint 4 ([[talking]]):

1. **Draft from the system.** `talk.pl review` drafts a demo script from GitLab and the journal: the question the
   sprint answered, the four headline numbers with their change, what to show, a before-and-after, a safe live
   change and a place for the ask.
2. **Edit for the audience.** The draft is a starting point. Jim cut it to what the program manager asks about and
   added the document-era comparison.
3. **Rehearse on Day 9.** Bob and Tom played the program manager and the deputy and interrupted with the questions
   they expected. The rehearsal caught the one mistake that would have hurt most (below).
4. **Write the twin.** The briefing has a written twin, the twelve-week progress memo ([[memos]]), so the numbers
   survive after the meeting.

Here is the draft the system produced for sprint 6, run in the Tidewater sandbox:

```sh run sandbox=tidewater expect="30-SECOND VERSION"
perl "$KIT/tools/gitlab/talk.pl" --local gitlab --project tidewater/model review --sprint 6
```

::: warning The number that nearly went to management
The draft's second section says verify coverage fell by more than sixty points. It did not. The draft took
"coverage" from the new verification matrix, which counts requirements with a *passing recorded result*, while
every earlier review had used the trace's count of requirements with a *verification case*. On the trace's
definition coverage was unchanged at seventy percent. Tom spotted it in the rehearsal because the number looked too
dramatic to be true. The final script ([[scorecard]] explains both measures) used the trace's number and said which
definition it was. Read every drafted number before you say it.
:::

=== The briefing, as given {#briefing-text}

Jim's outline, as filed with the sprint 6 evidence:

{{include: docs/tidewater/sprints/06/briefing.txt}}

Six points, one demonstration, one ask. The sections below take them in order.

=== Slide 1: where we started {#briefing-1}

**Headline: "We started with documents, then a Cameo model nobody could merge or review."**

One slide, two numbers from the drop-1 assessment of [[cameo]]: the diagram count and the package cycles. Jim did
not criticize the Cameo work; the people in the room had paid for it, and Bob and Tom had built it. Jim said what it
could not do: two people could not change it at the same time and merge the result, a review meant screenshots in a
briefing, and nobody could say which version of a diagram was current. The point of the slide is to name the
problem the next five slides solve.

=== Slide 2: what we built {#briefing-2}

**Headline: "Every change to the model is now checked and reviewed before it is accepted."**

A picture, not a list: the flow from a proposed change through the pipeline's checks and a code owner's review to
the model site. Jim named four things and no tool names: a project where the model lives, a set of automatic checks,
the model itself in SysML v2 text, and a web site that management can open on a phone. The vocabulary on the slide
was systems engineering vocabulary ([[devsecops]]): "configuration control," "peer review," "audit," "baseline."

=== Slide 3: the scorecard, sprint by sprint {#briefing-3}

**Headline: "The measures you signed in October, computed by the system."**

The table on the slide is the one in the outline above: TODO lines, lead time, gated changes and morale for each of
the six sprints. Jim chose four columns because each one answers a question the program manager had asked during the
twelve weeks:

- *TODO lines*: "How much of the port is left?" From {{value: docs/tidewater/sprints/02/scorecard.json 1_todo_lines}}
  after drop 1 to {{value: docs/tidewater/sprints/06/scorecard.json 1_todo_lines}}.
- *Lead time*: "How long does a change take now?" A median of
  {{value: docs/tidewater/sprints/06/scorecard.json 2_lead_time_hours}} hours in sprint 6.
- *Gated*: "Is anything getting in unchecked?" Every change since sprint 2:
  {{value: docs/tidewater/sprints/06/scorecard.json 3_gated_changes}} in sprint 6.
- *Morale*: "Is the team going to quit?" The program manager had not asked this out loud. Jim put it on the slide
  anyway, dip and all, because the dip in sprint 3 was the most believable number in the table.

The full twelve-measure scorecard went in the handout, with the one measure that missed
([[scorecard-spider]]) explained in a sentence: "We broke one of the two package cycles; the other is still there,
and finding its last edge is in the January backlog."

=== Slide 4: before and after {#briefing-4}

**Headline: "A requirement change took weeks. A model change takes hours, reviewed and checked."**

The comparison management had asked for, with its honesty built in. The document-era number,
{{value: docs/tidewater/sprints/06/scorecard.json document_era_baseline_notional.ccb_change_working_days}} working
days through the CCB, is labelled notional on the slide, and Jim said why: it is the program's memory of its last
document-era cycle, not a measurement. Jim also said what the comparison does not prove: a CCB change crossed several
organizations, a merge request crosses one team. The claim Jim made was narrower and true: the government team's part
of a change is now measured in hours, and every one is reviewed and checked.

=== Slide 5: the demonstration {#briefing-5}

**Headline: "Watch the model catch a mistake."**

The demonstration followed the review moves the team had practised since sprint 4: start from the question, show the
output and not the tool, make one safe live change, and say what it means. The live change was the same one in every
rehearsal: on a scratch branch, never on `develop`, delete one line that says a part satisfies a requirement, and let
the trace check name the requirement that lost its design.

Rehearse it yourself in the sandbox. First, a scratch copy of the integration branch and the deletion:

```sh run expect="satisfy TW_SYS_003 by IngestService"
git clone -q -b develop gitlab/repos/tidewater/model.git demo
cd demo && git checkout -q -b scratch/demo-satisfy
grep -n "satisfy TW_SYS_003" model/requirements/model/Requirements.sysml
```

Then remove the line, commit, and run the trace check the way a merge request pipeline runs it:

```sh run cwd=demo expect="new: TW-SYS-003"
sed -i '/satisfy TW_SYS_003/d' model/requirements/model/Requirements.sysml
git commit -qam "demo: drop one satisfy"
CI_PIPELINE_SOURCE=merge_request_event CI_MERGE_REQUEST_TARGET_BRANCH_NAME=develop perl ci/job.pl trace || echo "exit status $?"
```

The last line of the output is the whole demonstration: "this change adds trace gaps," naming the requirement. In
the meeting Jim opened the draft merge request on the projector, the pipeline turned red in about two minutes, and
clicked through to the line, then closed the merge request. The program manager's question was the one Jim had
hoped for: "And that runs on every change?" It does.

The model site came second: the part tree, the trace report and the data exchange table Tom had generated in sprint
6, all from the same model, all current as of that morning's nightly pipeline.

=== Slide 6: next semester, and the ask {#briefing-6}

**Headline: "January: the prime's first new drop. We will show what it changes within a day."**

The next milestones in one line each: the prime's drops of January and April, the behavior modeling the converter
cannot carry, and the first full baseline in sprint {{n: 20}}. Then the ask, with dates, carried over from the request
for enablers of October: approval of a second runner machine, and the GitLab tier decision, both by 15 January. An
ask without a date is a hope; an ask with a date is a decision management can make in the room.

=== What management asked, and how the numbers answered {#briefing-questions}

The questions came in the order the team had predicted in the rehearsal, with one surprise.

|| Question | Who asked | The answer, and its source ||
| "How do I know nothing fell on the floor in the port?" | deputy PM | the converter's counts and the trace, every change; the sampled review is next semester ([[proving]]) |
| "What happens when the prime sends the next version?" | program manager | a vendor branch, a change report, an impact report, and one issue per change for the CCB ([[prime-changes]]) |
| "Is this approved to hold program data?" | ISSM | the runner and repository were reviewed in sprints 2 to 5; markings are decided before real content ([[security]]) |
| "Can I see this without asking you?" | program manager | the model site, rebuilt nightly, and the weekly note ([[communicating]]) |
Table: Questions at the December briefing.

The surprise was the last comment, recorded in the sprint 6 quotes: the program manager's "Keep doing this. Show me
the same numbers in March." Jim's reading of it, at the retrospective an hour later, was that the scorecard had
become the program's, not the team's.

=== What the briefing left out {#briefing-left-out}

It left out the tools. Nobody on the slides said "GitLab," "pipeline," "YAML" or "merge request" without a plain
translation beside it. It left out the per-person numbers, which read as a ranking. It left out DORA measures and
value stream stages, which are for the team's own review ([[analytics]]). And it left out the parts of the story that
belonged to the team: the night the runner's disk filled up was one line on the scorecard, not a slide.

=== You can now {#briefing-can-now}

- build a management briefing from evidence, with a conclusion as every headline;
- draft a review script with `talk.pl review`, then check every number it drafted before you say it;
- run a safe live demonstration on a scratch branch that shows the checks catching a real mistake;
- answer the predictable questions with a source for each answer, and close with a dated ask.

=== Applying this to your project {#briefing-applying}

Agree the date of the first management briefing before sprint 1, and work backwards: the scorecard it will show, the
evidence each number comes from, the demonstration you will rehearse. Keep it to a handful of slides with conclusions
as headlines, put the full scorecard in a handout, and rehearse with someone playing the skeptic. Show one thing
working live. Say what did not work. End with an ask that has a date.
