== For the lead: leading a reluctant team {#lead}

::: bridge
You know: leading engineers through a schedule crunch.

Here it is called: leading a reluctant team through a change of method.

What is new: learning time is protected explicitly, and small wins are made visible.
:::

::: survival
What you have: a directive you did not ask for, a deadline, two or more good engineers who did not ask for it either,
and the same lack of experience they have.

What you need: a way to lead the change without pretending to know more than you do: a team that feels safe to be
slow and wrong for a few weeks, protected time to learn, early wins that are real, a workload that does not quietly
become nights and weekends, and a management chain that hears dated plans instead of excuses. This chapter is Jim's
view of the twelve weeks, written for whoever is in Jim's chair on the next project.
:::

=== What Jim was handed {#lead-handed}

The directive arrived on a Thursday ([[directive-memo]]). By the following Tuesday Jim had read it four times, sat
through the program manager's explanation, and come out with three facts. The program would adopt DevSecOps practices
for its systems engineering artifacts. The team would show measurable progress in twelve weeks. And nobody on the team,
including Jim, had ever done any of it.

The team's reaction was not subtle.

> We did documents for twenty years and the system flew. Then they made us do Cameo, and we did it, and now the model
> is a hairball nobody can review. Now this. What happens in three years when the next thing comes along?
> -- Bob, the first team meeting about the directive

Jim's private reaction was close to Bob's. What made the difference over the next twelve weeks was not that Jim became
an expert first. It was a handful of decisions about how to lead three beginners, one of them Jim, through a change
none of them had chosen. Each is a section of this chapter.

=== Make it safe to be slow and wrong {#lead-safety}

For the first month, everyone on the team would be worse at their job than they had been for years. Bob, who could
find any block in the Cameo model blindfolded, would spend twenty minutes on a merge conflict. Tom, who had run the
program's CM database for a decade, would push to the wrong branch. Jim would forget the command to create a branch in
front of the program manager. That is not a risk to manage; it is a certainty to plan for.

Psychological safety, for a team of three systems engineers, comes down to a few concrete habits:

- **A red pipeline is information, not an accusation.** The pipeline will fail on everyone's changes, often, and
  most of all in the first weeks. The team agreed, in the first retro, that nobody says "who broke it" in the stand-up;
  they say "what did it catch". The pipeline's job is to be wrong-detector; a failure means it worked.
- **The lead goes first.** At the sprint 1 kickoff Jim did practice lesson 11 live, on the shared screen, with the whole
  team watching, including the lesson's planted mistake: the merge request goes red on a missing semicolon. Jim read
  the log out loud, found the line with the quickfix list in Vim ([[vim-check]]), fixed it and pushed again. It took
  six minutes. Nobody remembers the semicolon; everybody remembers that the lead's change went red first and that it
  was fine.
- **"I don't know yet" is an acceptable answer** with a date attached ([[talking-bad-news]]). The lead says it out
  loud in front of management, early, so the team learns that it is safe.
- **No question is a nuisance during pairing.** The rule the team wrote on the wiki: "If you have been stuck for
  fifteen minutes, ask. If someone asks you, stop and help." Fifteen minutes is short enough that nobody suffers in
  silence and long enough that people try first.
- **Complaints are heard, then turned into changes.** The retrospective is where "this is pointless" gets said, and
  Jim's rule was to answer a complaint with one of three things: a change for next sprint, an explanation of why not,
  or "I'll ask management", with a date.
- **Measure how people feel, not only what they ship.** The pulse survey at every retro ([[ceremonies-retro]]) is five
  questions, one to five, anonymous. It costs two minutes and it is the only early warning the lead gets.

::: sidebar The semicolon
Jim nearly did not share the screen for that lesson. The plan had been to rehearse it the night before, so the
demonstration would go smoothly. Jim decided against it: the team needed to see a failure handled, not a
performance. In the sprint 1 retrospective, Tom wrote on the "continue" list: "Jim breaking things first."
:::

=== Protect the time to learn {#lead-learning}

The most important decision Jim made was to put learning into the plan as work, with points, where everyone could see
it.

The temptation is the opposite. Management wants measurable progress in twelve weeks; every hour spent on a practice
lesson is an hour not spent closing TODO lines. So the learning happens "on the side": at lunch, after hours, at
weekends. It always does happen, because the work cannot be done without it. But then the plan is a lie (the team's
capacity looks bigger than it is), the people doing the learning resent it, and the first time the schedule slips, the
learning is what gets cut.

Tidewater's plan carries learning as stories, labelled `work::learning`, with a reserve that falls as the team gets
better: {{value: docs/tidewater/work-split.json sprints.1.learning}}% of the task points done in sprint 1 (practice lessons 10 and 11, the survival kit, git from zero, Vim for
models), less in sprint 2 (lessons 12 and {{n: 13}}, the diagrams chapter, the first half of the SysML v2 workbook), and a
small remainder by sprint 6. Part III gives the real numbers for each sprint.

Making learning visible takes one label. In GitLab, a learning story looks like any other (here in the `work`
folder of practice lesson 11's sandbox):

```sh run sandbox=practice-11 cwd=work timeout=300 expect="Learning: practice lessons 10 and 11 \(tom\)"
. ../env.sh > /dev/null
for who in jim bob tom; do
  gl --now 2026-10-05T09:30 --as jim issue new "Learning: practice lessons 10 and 11 ($who)" --label type::story --label work::learning --milestone "Sprint 1" --assign $who --weight 2 > /dev/null
done
gl issue list --label work::learning
```

```text
#2    opened  ~type::story ~work::learning             Learning: practice lessons 10 and 11 (jim)
#3    opened  ~type::story ~work::learning             Learning: practice lessons 10 and 11 (bob)
#4    opened  ~type::story ~work::learning             Learning: practice lessons 10 and 11 (tom)
```

Three things follow from making it visible:

1. **The plan is honest.** Velocity in sprint 1 is low, and everybody knows why. When the program manager asked in
   the first review why only part of the conversion was done, Jim pointed at the learning stories: "That is the
   training you approved, in the plan where you can see it."
2. **The learning is protected.** A story on the board is harder to cut than an evening nobody sees. Jim asked for it
   formally in the request-for-enablers memo ([[memos-tidewater]]): four protected hours a week per person, with what
   happens if not ("the conversion burn-down slows by about a third").
3. **The shrinking reserve is itself a result.** The scorecard shows learning time falling as competence rises.
   Management reads that as the investment paying off, which it is.

::: tip The lead learns first
Jim did practice lessons 10 and 11 in week zero, before the team, alone, and got stuck twice. That made the first
pairing sessions easier: Jim could say "I got stuck there too; here is what helped." A lead who has not done the
lessons can only say "it's in the book".
:::

=== Pair at the terminal {#lead-pairing}

Pairing is two people at one keyboard: a **driver** who types and a **navigator** who reads, thinks ahead and catches
mistakes. They swap every twenty minutes or so. For a team learning a new toolset together it is the fastest way to
spread skill, because the person who knows a thing teaches it while doing real work.

Tidewater's pairing in the twelve weeks:

|| When | Pair | On ||
| Sprint 1 | Tom drives, Bob navigates | the first git commands in the practice repository: branch, commit, push |
| Sprint 1 | Bob drives, Jim navigates | the first conversion report and the TODO inventory |
| Sprint 2 | Tom drives, Jim navigates | the first pipeline, and the PowerShell failure on the real runner |
| Sprint 2 | Tom drives, Bob navigates | the first vendor-branch import; Bob then reviews and merges its merge request |
| Sprint 3 | Jim drives, Bob navigates | the DOORS import and the first trace report |
| Sprint 4 onward | as needed | conflicts, new job types, the first release |
Table: Who paired with whom

Two lessons from that table. First, the person who knows *less* drives. When Tom drove the first git commands with Bob
navigating, Tom learned git and Bob learned how to explain it, which is the step where Bob's own understanding became
solid. Second, the lead pairs too, as navigator and as driver. Jim at the keyboard with Bob navigating was the moment
Bob stopped treating the new tools as Jim's project and started treating them as the team's.

Pairing feels slow. Two people on one task looks like half the throughput. In the first weeks it is the opposite: two
beginners together get unstuck in minutes, where one alone loses an afternoon. The team stopped pairing by default in
sprint 4, when it was no longer needed for routine work, and kept it for anything new.

=== Pick the first wins on purpose {#lead-wins}

A discouraged team needs evidence, early, that the new way does something the old way could not. The lead's job is to
make sure the first wins are real, are noticed and are the team's.

Jim chose the first win before sprint 1 started: run the trace report on drop 1 and look for places where the prime's
DOORS text and the model disagreed. In the document era that comparison was done once before a major review, by hand,
over a week, if at all. Bob ran it on the fourth day of sprint 1, from the command line, and found the TW-SYS-006
drift in about two minutes.

> I found the drift in two minutes. It used to take a week, and we only looked before a review.
> -- Bob, sprint 1, day 4 (from the team chat)

That sentence did more for the transition than anything Jim said in twelve weeks, because Bob said it, about Bob's
own work.

The pattern for choosing first wins:

- **It answers a question the old way could not answer cheaply.** Drift between DOORS and the model; requirements with
  no verification; which packages depend on each other in a cycle.
- **The skeptic does it.** A win the lead announces is a claim. A win the skeptic discovers is evidence.
- **It takes days, not weeks.** The first win had to land in sprint 1. The model site for management (sprint 4) was a
  bigger win, but it would have been too late to be the first one.
- **It is shown, not told.** Jim asked Bob to show the drift in the sprint 1 demo, live, on the command line.

And then the lead makes sure the win is noticed: in the stand-up ("Bob found the drift in two minutes"), in the demo
(Bob shows it), in the weekly note to management, and in the retro ("what worked this sprint?").

=== Watch the workload {#lead-workload}

A team learning a new way of working while delivering under a deadline is a team at risk of quiet overwork. Nobody
announces it. The signs are small: statuses posted at 6 a.m., merge requests opened at 9 p.m., a stand-up line that
says "Y caught up on the vendor merge" on a Monday after a weekend.

Jim's routine for watching it took ten minutes a day:

1. **Read the stand-up statuses for time stamps**, not just content. Work after hours shows in the chat times and in
   the merge request times.
2. **Check blocked items daily**, because a blocked person often works around the block alone, late. A blocked item
   that is three days old is the lead's problem, not the engineer's ([[ceremonies-standup]]).
3. **Commit below capacity.** The team committed about nine-tenths of capacity in each sprint, never all of it; the
   remainder absorbs the surprises (a prime question, an ISSM review that moves) that always come.
4. **Look at the pulse survey's workload question** at every retro. A drop of one point is a conversation, not a
   statistic.

The blocked-items check is one command. Here a blocked task is added to the practice project, then listed:

```sh run expect="Register the second runner"
. ../env.sh > /dev/null
gl --now 2026-10-05T09:40 --as jim issue new "Register the second runner" --type task --label type::task --label workflow::blocked --milestone "Sprint 1" --assign tom > /dev/null
gl issue list --label workflow::blocked
```

```text
#5    opened  ~type::task ~workflow::blocked           Register the second runner
```

When Jim saw the signs, in sprint 3, the response was not "work smarter". It was to drop scope: one story moved to the
next sprint, explicitly, with a note in the weekly report to management saying why.

=== Manage up with dated plans {#lead-managing-up}

The lead stands between the team and the management chain that issued the directive. Done badly, the lead passes
pressure down and excuses up. Done well, the lead passes *plans* up and *protection* down.

What worked for Jim:

- **Agree the measures before the work starts.** In week zero, Jim and the program manager agreed the twelve-week
  scorecard ([[directive-scorecard]]): which numbers would show progress, where they started, what the targets were.
  Every report after that was against an agreed yardstick, so nobody could move the goalposts and nobody could claim
  success by picking flattering numbers.
- **Report the same numbers every week**, in the same order ([[talking-demo]]), in a one-page note that pairs with the
  kit's weekly quad. Management learns to read the trend and stops asking for special reports.
- **Ask for enablers formally, with dates and consequences.** The request-for-enablers memo of sprint 2 ([[memos]])
  turned "we need more time" into five specific asks, each with what it buys, by when, and what happens if not. What management
  approved, and when, is told in [[sprint-2]] and [[sprint-3]]; an ask that is not approved becomes a known risk, on
  the record, rather than a surprise.
- **Bring management to the demo, not the demo to management.** The program manager attended sprint reviews from
  sprint 2 onward. Seeing the merge request go green, live, did more than any slide.
- **Never surprise them.** Bad news went into the next weekly note or the next stand-up summary, the week it was
  known, with a date for the fix ([[talking-bad-news]]).

::: objection "Management just wants the slide with green on it."
Some managers do. But a directive that says "show measurable progress" is a manager asking to be given something they
can defend upward. The weekly note with four numbers, a trend and a dated plan is that thing.

Answer: Give them the numbers in the form they forward. The Tidewater program manager attached Jim's weekly notes
to the program's own monthly report unchanged by sprint 4. That is when the slide requests stopped.
:::

=== Say no with a date {#lead-saying-no}

The lead's most frequent hard conversation is not with the team. It is with a manager or stakeholder who wants
something now that is not in the sprint: a special report, a slide, "just a quick change to the model" before a meeting.

Saying yes to every request breaks the sprint and teaches everyone that the plan does not matter. Saying a flat no
sounds like obstruction. The middle is **no, with a date**:

|| Request | Say ||
| "Can you add the thermal interfaces to the model before Thursday's review?" | "Not this sprint: it's not in the plan and it would push the Stations work. It goes to refinement on Wednesday; the earliest it lands is sprint 5. For Thursday I can show you where it will go." |
| "I need a one-off report of every requirement the prime changed." | "The delta report does that on every drop. The next one is in January; I'll send you its summary the day after it lands." |
| "Just update the briefing slide with the new numbers." | "The model site is updated every night with the numbers; here's the link. If you need a slide, the weekly note has them in the order you used last time." |
| "Can Bob help the other program for a week?" | "Not without moving the conversion: Bob owns the Stations package this sprint. If it's urgent, I'll put it to you as a trade: Bob for a week, Stations moves to sprint 5. Your call." |
Table: No, with a date

Each answer has the same parts: what you will not do now, why (in the requester's terms), when it could happen, and
often what you *can* offer today. The last row is the most useful form of all: turn the request into a trade and give
the decision back to the person who has the authority to make it.

The deputy program manager, who chairs the change board, made exactly this kind of request in sprint 4 (a change to a
requirement before the board had seen it). Jim's answer was to open a merge request for it, so the board could approve
it in the normal flow the same week. That became the team's standard answer to "just a quick change": a merge request
is quick, and it is on the record.

=== Know when to slow down {#lead-slow-down}

There will be a sprint where it goes wrong. For Tidewater it was sprint 3: Bob's hand edit broke the grammar check on a
merge request, the ISSM was unreachable for three days and the toolchain review stalled, and the pulse survey dropped
for the first time. Bob wrote "this is the Cameo year again" in the retro.

The signs that the team needs to slow down, not push harder:

- The pulse survey drops, especially on "I could explain our progress" or "my workload is sustainable".
- Stand-up statuses get shorter and vaguer ("Y stuff T stuff B none"), or stop naming outcomes.
- A red pipeline stays red for a day and nobody mentions it in the stand-up.
- Merge requests get bigger, because people stopped believing in small ones.
- Someone asks, for the first time, whether they could "just do it the old way this once".

What Jim did in sprint 3, and what the book recommends:

1. **Name it, out loud, in the retro.** "This sprint was hard. The numbers show it, and so do you." Pretending it was
   fine would have cost the trust earned in sprints 1 and 2.
2. **Cut scope, not learning.** One engineering story moved to sprint 4. The learning reserve stayed.
3. **Remove the cause, not the symptom.** The ISSM block was the real problem; Jim booked the ISSM's next two review
   slots two weeks ahead, so it could not happen again. The grammar failure was fine: the pipeline caught it before it
   reached anyone else, which is its job, and Jim said so.
4. **Pause new tools.** No new pipeline job or GitLab feature was introduced in sprint 4 beyond what was already
   planned. A team that is struggling does not need one more thing to learn.
5. **Tell management the same week**, as a dated plan: "Sprint 3 delivered less than planned for two reasons; here is
   what changes in sprint 4; the twelve-week targets still hold."

Sprint 4's pulse recovered. Slowing down is not losing; it is how the twelve weeks stay twelve weeks and do
not become a year of burnout.

=== Hand over ownership, on purpose {#lead-ownership}

In week zero, the transition was Jim's project: Jim got the directive, Jim set up the meetings, Jim talked to
management. By the end of the twelve weeks it had to be the team's way of working, or it would stop the day Jim went
on leave. That does not happen by itself; the lead has to give things away, one at a time, and let them be done
differently.

What Jim handed over, and when:

|| When | What | To | How Jim let go ||
| Sprint 1 | the pipeline | Tom | Tom chose the jobs and their order; Jim reviewed but did not redesign |
| Sprint 2 | the vendor-branch import of each prime drop | Tom, with Bob reviewing | the drop runbook became Tom's wiki page |
| Sprint 3 | security owner, with the ISSM | Tom | Tom ran the ISSM meetings; Jim attended the first only |
| Sprint 4 | the Stations package and its code-owner section | Bob | Bob approves Stations changes, including Jim's |
| Sprint 4 | narrating the demo | rotating | Jim closes with the ask, and only that |
| Sprint 5 | preparing the change board's agenda from the open prime changes | Bob | Bob briefs the deputy program manager; Jim chairs |
| Sprint 6 | the weekly note to management | rotating, drafted with `talk.pl weekly` | Jim reads it before it goes, and changes only facts |
Table: What the lead gave away

Two things made the hand-overs stick. First, each one was recorded where the system enforces it: `CODEOWNERS` names
Bob as an owner of the Stations folder, so Jim's own Stations changes wait for Bob's approval. A hand-over that is only
spoken is reclaimed the first time the lead is in a hurry. Second, Jim accepted that the new owner would do it
differently. Tom's pipeline ran jobs in an order Jim would not have chosen; it worked, and changing it would have taken
the pipeline back.

> The first time my approval was the one holding up Jim's merge request, I thought I'd done something wrong. Then I
> realized that was the point.
> -- Bob, sprint 4 retrospective

=== The lead is a beginner too {#lead-beginner}

Jim had twenty-six years of systems engineering and zero weeks of git. The temptation for an experienced lead is to
hide the second fact behind the first: to stay in the management role, delegate the tools and never be seen not
knowing.

Jim did the opposite, deliberately:

- **Did the practice lessons first**, and told the team where they got stuck.
- **Took real tasks**, with points, every sprint: the DOORS import in sprint 3 was Jim's, from the command line, with Bob
  navigating.
- **Asked questions in the stand-up**, in front of everyone: "How do I see which pipeline job failed?" Tom answered in
  one sentence, and two other people learned it.
- **Used the tools the team used.** Jim's weekly note was drafted with `talk.pl`, edited in Vim and built with the same
  memo tool everyone else used. A lead who keeps a private Word template is telling the team the new tools are for
  them, not for leads.

The effect was that the team never had to pretend in front of Jim, because Jim was visibly learning alongside them. It
also meant Jim's judgment about what was hard was accurate: a lead who has not done the work under-estimates it, and
that is how teams end up with plans they cannot meet.

=== The lead's fortnight {#lead-fortnight}

Most of the lead's work in the new way is a rhythm, not heroics. Jim's two weeks, once it settled down in sprint 4:

|| When | The lead | Time ||
| Every day | reads the stand-up chat (times as well as content); checks blocked items; answers one question in the chat or pairs | about {{n: 20}} minutes |
| Day 1 | runs the kickoff: the goal, capacity including the learning reserve, the commitment | {{n: 90}} minutes |
| Days 2 to 8 | takes one or two real tasks; reviews merge requests in their code-owner areas | about half the time |
| Day 9 | refinement and planning poker; the demo dry run; the ask for Friday written down | {{n: 2}} hours |
| Day 10 | closes the demo with the ask; runs the retrospective and its pulse survey | {{n: 90}} minutes |
| Every Friday | reads the weekly note before it goes; sends it with the quad | {{n: 15}} minutes |
| Every second week | thirty minutes with the program manager: the four numbers, the trend, the open asks | {{n: 30}} minutes |
Table: Jim's two weeks

What is *not* on the list is as important: status meetings, slide preparation and chasing people for updates. The
records the meetings produce replaced them ([[ceremonies-why]]). That is the time the lead gets back, and Jim spent it
on two things: real modeling work, and the conversations with management that protect the team.

=== Exercises {#lead-exercises}

::: exercise {#ex-lead-learning} Budget the learning
Plan the first three sprints of your team's transition with an explicit learning reserve: what each person learns,
which practice lessons and chapters, how many points per sprint. Write it as stories with a learning label. What
engineering scope did you have to move to make it fit?
:::

::: exercise {#ex-lead-first-win} Choose the first win
List three questions about your current model that the old way could only answer slowly. For each, name the tool that
answers it in minutes, and the skeptic on your team who should be the one to run it. Which one can land in sprint 1?
:::

::: exercise {#ex-lead-no} Say no with a date
Write your answer to three requests you expect from management in the first month (a special report, an urgent model
change, borrowing a person). Each answer: what not now, why, when, and what you can offer today, or the trade.
:::

::: exercise {#ex-lead-signs} Read the signs
Look back at your team's last two weeks of statuses, merge requests and chat. Which of the slow-down signs do you see?
What would you change in the next sprint, and what would you tell management?
:::

=== Applying this to your project {#lead-apply}

- **Copy**: the lead goes first (shares their own failing change); a visible learning reserve as labelled stories;
  pairing with the less experienced person driving; first wins chosen on purpose and shown by the skeptic; the daily
  ten-minute workload check; the measures agreed with management before the work starts; no with a date.
- **Adapt**: the size of the learning reserve to your team's starting point (a team that already uses git needs less);
  the pulse survey questions to what your team worries about; the format of your weekly note to what your management
  already reads.
- **Decide locally**: who in management gets the weekly note and attends the demo; how much scope the lead may move
  without asking; what the team does when the lead is away (someone has to say "I don't know yet" first).

::: note You can now...
- make it safe for a team to be slow and wrong while it learns, starting with yourself;
- put learning in the plan as visible, protected, shrinking work;
- pair to spread skill quickly, and stop pairing when it is no longer needed;
- choose first wins that convince skeptics because the skeptics find them;
- watch for quiet overwork and respond by cutting scope;
- manage up with agreed measures, dated plans and formal requests for enablers;
- say no with a date, and turn requests into trades;
- recognize a sprint that has gone wrong and slow down on purpose.
:::
